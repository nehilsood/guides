Admit, Queue, or Shed

Your gateway already has a queue, nobody chose its length, and under load it serves everybody eight seconds after they stopped caring. Bounding it turns out not to buy you more completed work — it buys you a quarter of the latency and an honest answer, which is a different and better argument.

The win

Drive the gateway to 44 % goodput with every upstream call succeeding and no timeout violated, then cap the queue and watch goodput land on 43 % — the same — while p99 falls from 7 991 ms to 1 711 ms. Then find the check everyone writes for this, and measure it firing zero times.

Why you, specifically
“Built its custom OpenAI-compatible LLM gateway, then moved the inference path from REST to streaming Gemini Live, cutting end-to-end turn latency 47% at p50 (4.1 s to 2.2 s) and 52% at p90 (5.1 s to 2.4 s), with zero mid-interview session drops.”

Those are numbers from a system that was keeping up. The question that follows them is always the same and it is the one your prep notes leave open: “now scale it to a million concurrent.” The honest answer is not an architecture diagram. It is what the gateway does in the first minute when demand exceeds what Gemini will serve you.

Today it queues, because FakeUpstream holds an asyncio.Semaphore and everything that cannot get a slot waits behind it with no limit on how long. Nobody wrote that policy down. It is the default that arrives with the primitive, and it is a policy all the same.

That sentence is defended in full in chapter 0002 of Résumé Mastery — this lesson is where the mechanism underneath it gets built and broken.

Start here — the plain version

Throughput is how many requests you are handling. Goodput is how many of them arrive soon enough to be worth anything. A server under load can hold throughput perfectly flat while goodput goes to zero, and every server-side metric will look fine the entire time, because from the server's side each request succeeded.

There are only three things you can do with an arriving request: start it now, make it wait, or refuse it. This lesson is about the second and third, and the discovery that the interesting cost of queueing is not memory — it is that a queue quietly converts your latency promise into a function of how many people showed up.

Offered load
What arrives, whether or not you can handle it. Measured at the door, before any of your own limits apply.
The knee
The offered rate at which response time stops rising gently and starts rising steeply. Past it, extra load buys nothing and costs everything.
Load shedding
Refusing excess work deliberately, so the work you did accept stays fast. It is a choice about who fails, made in advance.
Criticality
A request's rank when there is not enough capacity for all of them. The only part of this that is a product decision rather than an engineering one.

The queue nobody chose is the one that decides your p99

Open app/upstream.py and the admission policy is already there, on line 115: self._slots = asyncio.Semaphore(profile.max_concurrency). Eight slots, because the provider gives you eight. Everything else waits. The waiting has no length limit, no age limit, and no way to say no.

That is not an oversight, it is what a semaphore is. It bounds concurrency, which is exactly its job, and lesson 0012 was careful to say that a bulkhead bounds how many and never how long. The part that catches people is what happens when you then add the deadline from 0012 on top: now every request has an expiry, and the queue is full of requests that will reach the front after theirs has passed.

Why the metrics stay green throughout

During the collapse measured below, the upstream's failure rate is zero. Every call it receives, it answers correctly. The stage timeouts are never exceeded. The only thing that has gone wrong is that the answer arrives after the client's deadline — and the client is the only party in the system that can see that.

This is the same shape as lesson 0002's coordinated omission and lesson 0011's slow-but-not-failing dependency, arriving for a third time from a third direction. A server-side metric cannot see a deadline that belongs to the client.

In plain words

The top chart shows that past the capacity line, a queue with no limit and one capped at 16 deliver about the same share of answers in time to be used, 44 and 43 %. The bottom chart shows the wait of the slowest 1 in 100 requests, called the p99. With no limit, that wait runs into the 8-second time limit; with the cap, it stays near 1.7 seconds. The cap pays for that by turning 329 of 576 requests away at once.

    Goodput at 48/s: 44 % → 43 %. It got very slightly worse. If you came here expecting shedding to recover throughput, the measurement says no — capacity is capacity, and refusing work does not create any.

    p99 at 48/s: 7 991 ms → 1 711 ms. That is what the queue bound actually bought, and it is a factor of nearly five. The served column is flat at 244–247 across the last three rows: goodput has plateaued, which is the exact thing the Builders' Library says a good load test should show.

    And 329 people got told immediately instead of eventually

    The shed column is the honest one. Those 329 requests failed in both configurations. Unbounded, they failed after waiting most of eight seconds, having each consumed a queue slot the whole time. Bounded, they failed in about a millisecond and were told, with a Retry-After, when to come back.

  3. In plain words

    A queue empties at a steady rate, so its length sets how long the last request in it waits. With no limit, that wait keeps growing for as long as too many requests arrive. With a limit of 16 and about 21 served each second, the wait in line tops out at about three quarters of a second.

    Derive the 1 711 rather than admiring it. Little's law is L = λW; rearranged, the time an item spends waiting is the queue depth divided by the rate at which the queue drains.

    777 ms of queueing, plus ~390 ms of service
    1 167 predicted, 1 197 measured

    Within 3 %. The queue depth is not a memory setting, it is the latency you have promised. Pick 16 and you have chosen roughly 1.2 seconds; pick 256 and you have chosen twelve, which is past the budget before the request has done anything.

    This is the one derivation to carry out of this lesson, because it inverts the usual conversation. People argue about queue depth as though it were a capacity tuning parameter. It is a p99 tuning parameter, and you can compute it before shipping.

  4. In plain words

    A request can reach the front of a queue with some time left, but not enough to finish. Here one had 166 ms left and its call needed 380 ms, so a check for “already expired” let it through, and it used a slot for an answer nobody received. Checking whether the call still fits in the time left catches these: it threw out 92 and served 29 more.

    So bound every queue and the problem is solved? Not the ones you do not own — a load-balancer surge queue, a TCP buffer, a broker backlog. For those the standard advice is to check, on dequeue, whether the request has already expired. Measure that check.

    The obvious check discarded nothing. Not “few” — zero, of 576

    Look at wasted, which counts requests thrown out of the queue having done no work. The deadline passed? row shows 0. Its entire improvement over unbounded, 44 % to 52 %, came from the 256 cap; the check itself never fired once.

    The reason is arithmetic. A request reaching the front of the queue with 166 ms left has not expired, so the check admits it — and then it needs 380 ms. It burns an upstream slot and returns to nobody. Asking “is it dead yet?” admits everything that is merely dying.

    Swap the question to “does a call still fit?” and the check discards 92 and serves 29 more. That is Deadline.fits() from lesson 0012, applied one layer earlier than it was built for.

    And it changes them a lot. Under FIFO, interactive requests get served about 132 of 311, the same 42 % as everyone else, because a queue is indifferent.     And LIFO, which is the one that sounds wrong and is sometimes right

    Serving newest-first roughly halves the median — 1 196 ms to 460 — because a fresh request has spent none of its budget. It also strands the oldest: 13 or 14 requests starve in the queue outright, every run, and the p99 stops being a number you can quote at all — 1 399, 1 399 and 2 624 ms across three runs. That instability is the finding: under LIFO nothing bounds how long the request at the back waits. The Builders' Library calls it “an extreme version of this approach”, which is the right register for it.

Underneath

One layer down · why a queue is a latency promise

Goodput is not throughput, and only one of them has a user in it

David Yanacek defines it precisely: goodput is “the subset of the throughput that is handled without errors and with low enough latency for the client to make use of the response”. The gap between the two is work you paid for and threw away, and the article is blunt about why that matters — “all the progress that the server made so far on that request goes to waste. And the last thing a system should do in an overload situation, where capacity is constrained, is waste work.”

The unit is wrong, and for LLM work it is wrong by an order of magnitude

A requests-per-second limiter prices a 200k-token context and a twenty-token ping identically. The SRE Book states the general case — “modeling capacity as ‘queries per second’ or using static features of the requests that are believed to be a proxy for the resources they consume… often makes for a poor metric”, and “a better solution is to measure capacity directly in available resources” — and inference is the case where it is most extreme, because prefill time is roughly linear in prompt length.

The same traffic, priced two ways:

requests share tokens share cost at 0.35 ms/token alice, 16 words 480 83% 7 680 4% 86 ms each bulk, 2 000 words 96 17% 192 096 96% 780 ms each

Four percent of the tokens and eighty-three percent of the requests are the same traffic. Whichever column your limiter reads is the reality it enforces, and only one of them predicts how long the upstream will be busy. The honest caveat: this anchor's “tokens” are whitespace words, wrong by about 1.3× against a real BPE tokenizer — which does not disturb the ratio, and does mean a token count from here is not a token count.

What you shed first is a product decision wearing engineering clothes

The SRE Book's mechanism is four criticality levels — CRITICAL_PLUS, CRITICAL, SHEDDABLE_PLUS, SHEDDABLE — and the rule that matters is the ordering: “when a task is itself overloaded, it will reject requests of lower criticalities sooner.” Step 6 shows why that is worth the trouble and also what it does not do. Total served was 247, 234, 246, 247 across four policies. The ordering redistributes; it does not create.

Fairness, and the reason a single global bucket cannot provide it

Yanacek's queue-backlog article puts the multi-tenant case in numbers worth stealing: if one customer spikes unthrottled and builds a system-wide backlog, “it might take on the order of 30 minutes for an operator to be engaged… but if the volume of queued messages was 10x the capacity that the consumer side was scaled to, this means it would take 300 minutes for the system to work through the backlog”. That is Little's law again, at the scale where it ends careers: a thirty-minute mistake becomes a five-hour outage because the queue was the only thing absorbing it.

A single global bucket cannot help, because the tenant draining it is indistinguishable from legitimate aggregate demand. Per-customer limits are what AWS actually does — “a common way that AWS implements fairness is by setting per-customer rate-based limits, with some flexibility for bursting” — and step 5 measures the effect: alice served 480 of 480 with a p50 of 88 ms instead of 498.

Which lands on the sentence worth keeping: a queue you did not choose the length of is a latency promise you did not know you were making. Bounding it does not get you more work done — capacity decides that — it gets the work you do accept back in time, and it gets everybody else an answer in milliseconds instead of a timeout in seconds.

Check yourself

At 48 requests a second the unbounded queue served 255 of 576 and the bounded one served 247. What did bounding the queue actually buy?

  1. The same goodput at one quarter of the latency
  2. More goodput since fewer requests got shed at all
  3. Lower peak queue depth and lower peak memory use
  4. Extra upstream capacity freed up by a shorter queue

Goodput was 44 % against 43 % — unchanged, and fractionally worse. Capacity is set by the upstream and refusing work does not create any. What the bound bought was p99 7 991 ms → 1 711 ms, and 329 requests that failed in both cases finding out in a millisecond rather than after most of eight seconds.

This is the part worth saying unprompted, because the instinctive claim — “we added shedding and throughput improved” — is the one a measurement refutes. Peak queue did fall, but memory was never the constraint; the constraint was that a queue converts your p99 into a function of how many people arrived.

On a 256-deep queue, checking “has this request's deadline passed?” before running it discarded how many of 576 requests?

  1. None at all, because none had actually expired yet
  2. Ninety-two, the ones that could not fit a call
  3. All of the requests that were queued behind others
  4. Roughly half, which is what the cap already did

Zero, out of 576. A request arriving at the front with 166 ms remaining has not expired, so the check passes it — and then it needs 380 ms, burns an upstream slot, and returns to a client that has gone. The entire improvement that row showed over unbounded came from the 256 cap, not from the check.

Changing the question to “does a call still fit in what is left?” discarded 92 and served 29 more. That is Deadline.fits() from lesson 0012 used one layer earlier. The Builders' Library flags the difficulty honestly: “this is still pretty complicated, because we don't know how long the request is likely to take” — so you need a latency estimate, and a wrong estimate sheds work that would have made it.

Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

A spoken answer to “now scale it to a million concurrent” — which is really “what happens in the first minute when you cannot keep up?” Read it aloud, then say it again with the page covered.

  1. Situation

    Our gateway had an admission policy nobody chose. A semaphore for the provider's eight slots, and an unbounded line in front of it.

  2. Mechanism

    Throughput is what you handle. Goodput is what arrives soon enough to matter. A queue holds throughput flat while goodput collapses.

    And every server metric stays green, because the server answered every call correctly. The deadline that was missed belongs to the client.

  3. What I did

    I drove it to forty-eight a second against a capacity of twenty-one. Goodput fell to 44 percent, with the queue three hundred and forty-six deep and zero upstream failures.

    Then I bounded the queue at sixteen, chosen from Little's law rather than by feel. Sixteen waiting over twenty-one served a second is 762 milliseconds of queueing, so the depth is the p99.

    p99 went from 7 991 milliseconds to 1 711. The prediction and the measurement agreed within three percent.

  4. What it cost

    Goodput did not improve. It went from 44 percent to 43. Capacity is capacity, and shedding does not create any.

    What changed is that 329 requests were refused in a millisecond with a Retry-After, instead of waiting eight seconds to fail. And I had to decide whom to serve, which is a product call, not an engineering one.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“We added rate limiting and load shedding, which improved throughput under load.” Two problems in one clause. Shedding does not improve throughput — it cannot, because the upstream's capacity is unchanged — and anyone who has measured it will ask by how much and watch you discover that. The version that holds says what it actually bought: “shedding left goodput flat and cut p99 by a factor of five, and it turned silent eight-second waits into immediate 429s.”

The second trap is a rate limit quoted in requests per second for an LLM workload. The follow-up is “what happens when one tenant sends 200k-token contexts?”, and the honest arithmetic — seventeen percent of requests, sixty-five percent of the capacity — is usually a surprise to the person who set the limit. Price the bucket in tokens, and be ready to say that a request costing more than your burst capacity is refused forever rather than throttled, because that is the sizing bug everyone ships once.

Probes to expect

  1. “Now scale it to a million concurrent.” The question your prep notes leave open. It is not asking for an architecture diagram — it is asking what happens in the first minute of overload, and the honest answer starts with what your system does today rather than what you would build.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Today it queues, and the queue has no length limit — that is the default that arrives with a semaphore rather than a decision anyone made. I measured it at roughly twice capacity: goodput fell to 44 %, the queue reached 345 deep, and the upstream's failure rate was zero throughout, because every call succeeded and simply arrived after the client's deadline.

    So the first change is not more capacity, it is a door. Bound the queue from a Little's-law target, price admission in tokens rather than requests, and return a fast 429 with Retry-After. That does not serve more people — goodput went 44 % to 43 — it means the people you do serve get an answer inside the budget and everybody else finds out immediately.

    Invites · “So what would you actually add capacity for?” — to move the knee, which is the only thing that raises goodput.
  2. “How did you choose the queue depth?” Most answers are a round number and a shrug. Deriving it is the whole difference, and it takes one line of arithmetic you can do at a whiteboard.
    Model answer — say yours first

    From Little's law, L = λW. Rearranged, the time an item waits is the depth divided by the rate the queue drains at. We serve about 21 a second, so a depth of 16 is 762 ms of queueing on top of about 390 ms of service — a predicted p50 of 1 167. Measured 1 197, within 3 %.

    The reason to do it that way round is that it inverts the usual argument. People treat queue depth as a capacity knob and pick a number that feels safe. It is a p99 knob: choosing 256 instead of 16 is choosing twelve seconds of waiting against an eight-second budget, which is a promise broken before the request does anything.

    Invites · “What if your service rate varies?” — then so does the promise, which is an argument for bounding age as well as depth.
  3. “You have a queue you do not control — a load balancer, a broker. What do you do?” The question where the obvious answer is measurably useless. Having the zero is what makes this one memorable.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The standard advice is to check on dequeue whether the request has already expired and throw it away if so. I implemented that and measured it discarding nothing at all — zero of 576. A request reaching the front with 166 ms left has not expired, so it gets admitted, and then it needs 380 ms.

    The check has to be “does a call still fit in the time remaining?”, not “has it expired?” That discarded 92 and served 29 more. It needs a latency estimate, which the Builders' Library is honest about being hard — cache hits and misses cost differently and you do not know which you have — but a rough estimate beats a check that never fires. For the load balancer itself, the fix is configuration: spillover rather than a surge queue, which is why AWS's Application Load Balancer rejects excess instead of queueing it.

    Invites · “What if your estimate is wrong?” — you shed work that would have made it, so estimate low and bound the age too.
  4. “What do you shed first?” Sounds like a technical question and is not. The strongest answer states the ordering, gives the measured redistribution, and names the part that is not yours to decide.
    Model answer — say yours first

    First, the thing worth being clear about: ordering does not change how many you serve. I measured four policies at the same offered load and served 247, 234, 246, 247 — capacity had already decided that. It changes who, and by a lot: a priority floor took interactive requests from about 132 of 311 to 230, while retries went from 67 to 3 of 161.

    For us the live interview turn is protected and batch re-scoring and retries go first, because a late token in a live session is worthless and the session is unrecoverable. The honest weakness is that “this is a retry” is self-reported, so a client that lies gets served better — which is why the Builders' Library pushes the classification to the edge rather than deriving it at each hop.

    Invites · “Who signs off on that ranking?” — the product owner, and it should be written down before the incident.
  5. “Your rate limit is 100 requests per second per tenant. What is wrong with that here?” Checks whether you have thought about LLM workloads specifically rather than porting a REST-API habit. The ratio is the answer and it is large enough to be memorable.
    Model answer — say yours first

    It prices a 200k-token context and a twenty-token ping the same. I measured a tenant sending 17 % of the requests and consuming 65 % of the capacity, because prefill time is roughly linear in prompt length. A request-counting limiter reports that tenant as a minor user right up to the moment everyone else's latency triples.

    So the bucket is priced in tokens, and per tenant rather than globally — a single global bucket cannot be fair because the tenant draining it looks identical to legitimate aggregate demand. With per-tenant buckets the quiet tenant went from about 400 to 480 of 480 served and her p50 from about 500 ms to 87. The sizing trap worth mentioning: the burst has to exceed your largest single request, or that request is refused forever rather than throttled.

    Invites · “How do you price it before you have run it?” — you cannot, which is why the limit is derived from a load test rather than chosen.
