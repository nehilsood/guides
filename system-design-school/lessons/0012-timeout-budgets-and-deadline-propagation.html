Timeout Budgets and Deadline Propagation

Five stages, five perfectly reasonable timeouts, every one of them honoured, and a turn that runs half as long again as the budget you promised. Then the fix, which caps it exactly — by converting twenty-six late answers into failures.

The win

Push a turn past 11 seconds against an 8 000 ms budget with no timeout violated anywhere, then pass one absolute deadline down and watch the maximum land on 8 001 ms, every run — and measure what that costs, which is 26 turns of 200.

Why you, specifically
“…cutting end-to-end turn latency 47 % at p50 (4.1 s to 2.2 s) and 52 % at p90 (5.1 s to 2.4 s), with zero mid-interview session drops.”

That bullet is a claim about a distribution, and behind it is an 8-second per-turn budget that lives in exactly one place: the edge. Every stage underneath it has its own timeout, chosen by whoever wrote the stage, and none of them knows what the others are spending.

The question that finds this is not “what is your p50”. It is “what enforces the eight seconds?” — and if the honest answer is “nothing below the edge”, the p90 in that bullet is a measurement rather than a guarantee.

That sentence is defended in full in chapter 0002 of Résumé Mastery — this lesson is where the mechanism underneath it gets built and broken.

Start here — the plain version

A timeout is relative: “give up after 1600 ms”. Every hop that has one starts its own clock, so five hops with a 1600 ms timeout can legally take eight seconds, and with a retry at each hop, much longer. Nothing in the system notices, because no single component exceeded its own limit.

A deadline is absolute: “be finished by this instant”. It is the same instant however many hops read it, so five hops under one deadline cannot sum past it — the last one gets whatever the earlier ones left. That one difference is the whole lesson, and everything else here is bookkeeping around it.

Turn budget
The end-to-end time you have promised for one user-visible operation. Here, 8 s. It is a promise only if something enforces it.
Timeout
A limit on one call, measured from when that call starts. A property of the call, which is why it cannot be passed down.
Deadline
An absolute instant by which the whole request must be done. A property of the request, which is why it can.
Propagation
Passing that instant to everything the request touches — an argument in-process, a header across a service boundary — so every hop reads the same clock.
Remaining budget
Deadline minus now. The number a stage should actually use as its limit, and the number a retry has to fit inside.
Monotonic clock
A clock that only moves forward. Deadlines use it because a wall clock can step backwards when NTP corrects it, firing deadlines early for reasons no log explains.
Bulkhead
A cap on concurrency for one dependency, so one slow thing cannot consume every worker. Bounds how many, never how long — confusing the two is why people add one and watch latency get worse.
Hedge
Issuing a second attempt before the first has failed, when it is late. Costs capacity instead of time, which is what makes it usable where a retry does not fit.
Deadline exceeded
A distinct failure from “this hop was slow”. Worth its own error type, because a slow hop may be worth retrying and an expired deadline never is.

Five reasonable timeouts do not add up to one budget

The arithmetic looks like it should work. The budget is 8 000 ms and there are five stages, so each stage gets 1 600 ms, and if every stage honours its limit the turn cannot exceed the budget. That is true, and it is also not what any real system looks like, for two reasons that pull in opposite directions.

The first is that nobody derives 1 600. A stage owner asked how long their model call should be allowed to take says “five seconds, that is generous”, and they are right about their stage in isolation. Five such decisions make a ceiling of 25 s for a turn budgeted at 8. The second is that when someone does derive it, the equal slice turns out to be wrong in the other direction — stages are not equally expensive, and a limit set at one fifth of the budget kills a quarter of the turns that would have finished in time.

Why a retry makes this structural rather than sloppy

Give each stage one retry and a stage is now worth two of its timeout. At 1 600 ms that is 3 200 ms for one stage, and five of them is 16 s. Nothing has been configured wrongly: the timeout is per call, the retry is per stage, and both are behaving exactly as written.

This is the reason the fix is not “pick better timeouts”. There is no set of per-call limits that composes into a per-request guarantee, because the quantity being limited is the wrong one. You need something that measures the request rather than the call.

Lab — blow the budget without breaking a rule

Lab 0012 ~20 minutes

Real milliseconds, fake provider

The turns here run concurrently against FakeUpstream and each one times only itself, so these are real elapsed milliseconds rather than a scaled model — which is why 200 turns take one turn's wall clock. What is modelled is the latency distribution of a model call, not the timing machinery.

Know which columns hold still before you quote one. Over five runs of the 5 000 ms case: refused was 25 every time, deadline's maximum was 8 001–8 002 ms every time, and independent completed 174 of 200 every time. The unbounded rows wander, because whether a given turn crosses a timeout depends on event-loop scheduling under load: independent+retry's p99 ranged 8 955–10 578 ms and its maximum 11 053–12 260 ms. That is itself the finding — the bounded row is bounded and the unbounded one is not — so claim the shape and the stable figures, not a particular millisecond.

  1. Start with the timeout that looks correct: the budget divided by the stage count. 8 000 ms over five stages is 1 600 ms each, and by construction five of those cannot exceed the budget.

    $ cd anchor/vendor-eval-gateway
$ uv run python -m bench.deadline --turns 200 --stage-timeout-ms 1600
    budget 8000 ms, 5 stages, per-stage timeout 1600 ms, 200 turns

mode                     p50     p99     max  over budget  completed  refused
independent             2072    3679    3809        0 /200     147 /200        0
independent+retry       2309    5010    5847        0 /200     200 /200        0
deadline                2280    5078    5614        0 /200     197 /200        0
    Zero turns over budget, and 53 of 200 failed

    The budget is perfectly safe and the first row completed 147 of 200 turns. The equal slice is not a compromise between the stages, it is a limit that a quarter of turns cannot meet, because stage latency is a distribution and 1 600 ms sits inside it. The retry row rescues completion to 200 of 200 by spending up to 3 200 ms on a stage.

    So the derived timeout fails in the direction nobody warns you about. It protects the budget by refusing work that would have fitted, because dividing a budget equally assumes the stages are equal and that each one's latency is a point rather than a spread.

  2. Now use the timeout a real team writes. Ask each stage owner how long their model call should get and they say five seconds, which is generous and defensible for one call.

    $ uv run python -m bench.deadline --turns 200 --stage-timeout-ms 5000
    budget 8000 ms, 5 stages, per-stage timeout 5000 ms, 200 turns

mode                     p50     p99     max  over budget  completed  refused
independent             2253    7323    8671        1 /200     174 /200        0
independent+retry       2273    9015   11880        4 /200     200 /200        0
deadline                2258    7005    8001        1 /200     174 /200       25
    p99 of 9 s and a worst case of 11.9 s, against a budget of 8

    Look at the second row. p99 is 9 015 ms and the maximum is 11 880 ms — 48 % past the budget. Every stage honoured its 5 000 ms timeout and every retry was permitted, so there is no configuration error to find and no alert that would fire. The p50 is a healthy 2 273 ms, which is exactly the number that would go on a slide.

    Run it again and that row moves — across five runs its p99 ranged 8 955 to 10 578 ms and its maximum 11 053 to 12 260. That is the point rather than noise in the way. The row has no ceiling, so what you measure is wherever the distribution landed today; the number to carry is “between 11 and 12 seconds against a budget of 8”, not one millisecond figure.

    This is the shape to recognise: a median that looks fine and a tail that is not bounded by anything. Five local limits of 5 s permit a 25 s ceiling, and one retry per stage makes it 50. What you actually observe is wherever the latency distribution lands inside that.

  3. Turn on propagation and change nothing else. One Deadline is built at the edge from the turn budget and handed to every stage.

    $ uv run python -m bench.deadline --turns 200 --stage-timeout-ms 5000 | tail -1
    deadline                2258    7005    8001        1 /200     174 /200       25
    Before / after · one object, passed down

    Maximum 11 880 ms → 8 001 ms. Not “usually under” — across five runs the bounded maximum was 8 001 or 8 002 ms while the unbounded one wandered between 11 053 and 12 260, because a stage is bounded by min(its own timeout, what is left) and the remainder cannot be negative.

    The p50 barely moves: 2 273 → 2 258 ms. Propagation is a tail intervention, and it costs the median nothing.

    And the “1 over budget” is worth explaining rather than hiding

    One turn of 200 is still counted as over, at 8 001 ms. That is the measurement including about a millisecond of loop overhead after the deadline fired, not a turn that escaped — the deadline stopped the work at 8 000. It is worth noticing because it is the difference between “the budget is enforced” and “the budget plus whatever your instrumentation costs is enforced”, and at a tighter budget that gap matters.

  4. Now find the cost, because a change that only improves things is a change you have not measured properly. Compare the completion column between the retry row and the deadline row.

    $ uv run python -m bench.deadline --turns 200 --stage-timeout-ms 5000 | tail -2
    independent+retry       2273    9015   11880        4 /200     200 /200        0
deadline                2258    7005    8001        1 /200     174 /200       25
    200 of 200 became 174 of 200

    Twenty-six turns that used to finish, late, now fail. Across five runs the retry row completed 199 or 200 and the deadline row 173 or 174, so the gap is 26 give or take one. That is not a regression — it is the trade being made explicit. Without a deadline those 26 turns returned an answer after nine, ten, eleven seconds to a user who had been promised eight, and quite possibly to a client that had already given up. With it they fail at the budget.

    Which is better is a product decision rather than an engineering one, and the honest framing is: a deadline converts overruns into failures. If a late answer is genuinely worth more than a fast failure, the correct response is to raise the budget, not to drop the deadline — because at least then the promise matches the system.

  5. Look at the refused column, which is the interaction with lesson 0010 and the part most retry code gets wrong.

    $ grep -n -B1 -A3 "cannot finish inside" bench/deadline.py
    103-                # The question a retry almost never asks. An attempt that
104:                # cannot finish inside the remaining budget is pure load: it
105-                # costs the upstream a whole call and cannot return in time
106-                # to be used.
107-                if deadline is not None and not deadline.fits(args.stage_timeout_ms):
    Twenty-five attempts that would have been started and thrown away

    A retry that cannot finish before the deadline is pure load: it costs the upstream a full unit of work and its result arrives after anyone stopped caring. Checking remaining before starting one refused 25 attempts in 200 turns.

    This is what makes lesson 0010's T ≤ 433 ms enforceable rather than aspirational. Without a deadline, “three attempts fit in 1 600 ms” is a calculation you did once on a whiteboard; with one, the third attempt is actually refused at runtime when the first two ran long. A retry policy without a deadline is a policy nothing checks.

  6. Last, see where the deadline physically lives, and confirm it is absolute rather than a divided-up budget.

    $ uv run python -c "
from app.deadline import Deadline
class C:
    t = 100.0
    def __call__(self): return self.t
c = C(); d = Deadline.in_ms(8000, clock=c)
for stage, spend in enumerate(['0.4','5.0','0.4','9.9'], 1):
    print(f'stage {stage}: gets min(5000, {d.remaining_ms():7.0f}) = {min(5000, d.remaining_ms()):7.0f} ms')
    c.t += float(spend)"
    stage 1: gets min(5000,    8000) =    5000 ms
stage 2: gets min(5000,    7600) =    5000 ms
stage 3: gets min(5000,    2600) =    2600 ms
stage 4: gets min(5000,    2200) =    2200 ms
    The same object, read four times, giving four different answers

    Nothing divided the budget in advance. Each stage asks the same deadline what is left, and the answer shrinks as the clock is spent — stage 3 is handed 2 600 ms because stage 2 took five seconds, without anyone having to tell it that. An equal-slice scheme could not do this: it would have given stage 3 its 1 600 regardless.

    Two implementation details worth carrying. The clock is time.monotonic, never time.time, because a wall clock steps backwards when NTP corrects it and a deadline that moves backwards fires early for reasons no log will explain. And the Deadline is a frozen dataclass: a callee that can extend its own deadline does not have one, and “just give this stage a bit more time” is exactly how an 8-second budget becomes fourteen.

Underneath

One layer down · why relative limits cannot be composed

Four things. The first is the whole argument, the second closes 0010's loop, the third is the distinction that makes people add a bulkhead and get slower, and the fourth is where the thing physically lives.

A timeout is a property of a call; a budget is a property of a request

This is not pedantry, it is the reason no amount of tuning fixes it. A timeout starts its clock when its call starts, so n hops with timeout t permit n·t, and each hop is individually correct the entire time. A deadline is one instant, so n hops reading it permit exactly the deadline, regardless of n. Adding a hop changes the ceiling in the first scheme and changes nothing in the second, which is the property you actually want, because hops get added by people who have never seen your budget.

The ceiling each scheme permits, for five stages with one retry:

scheme ceiling measured max 1600 ms each 5 × 1600 = 8000 ms 3809 ms 1600 ms each + retry 5 × 3200 = 16 s 5847 ms 5000 ms each 5 × 5000 = 25 s 8671 ms 5000 ms each + retry 5 × 10 000 = 50 s 11 880 ms one 8000 ms deadline 8000 ms, always 8001 ms

What you observe is wherever the latency distribution happens to land inside the ceiling, which is why this never shows up as a bug report. The p50 is healthy in every row. Only the last row has a ceiling you could put in a contract.

A retry has to fit the remaining deadline, not restart the clock

Lesson 0010 derived that three attempts fit a 1 600 ms stage slice only while a call stays under 433 ms. Without a deadline that derivation is a whiteboard exercise: nothing at runtime knows how much of the slice the first two attempts consumed, so the third starts regardless. With one, the attempt is checked against what is left and refused if it cannot finish — 25 times in 200 turns here.

What one stage is actually allowed, as the turn is spent, at a nominal 5000 ms timeout:

stage spent so far remaining limit = min(5000, remaining) 1 0 ms 8000 ms 5000 ms 2 400 ms 7600 ms 5000 ms 3 5400 ms 2600 ms 2600 ms 4 5800 ms 2200 ms 2200 ms

Nothing divided the budget in advance. Each stage asks the same deadline what is left, so a stage that ran long leaves less for the next one automatically — which is precisely what an equal slice cannot do, because it would hand stage 3 its fixed fifth regardless.

The refusal is the point rather than a side effect. An attempt that cannot complete before the deadline costs the upstream a full unit of work and produces a result nobody can use, which is the definition of pure load. This is also the cleanest example of the two lessons composing: the budget in 0010 bounds how many retries exist, and the deadline here bounds whether this particular one is worth starting.

Bulkheads bound concurrency, and confusing that with time makes things worse

A bulkhead caps how many calls to one dependency can be in flight, so a slow dependency cannot consume every worker. It says nothing about how long any of them takes. People reach for one when latency is the problem and are surprised when p99 gets worse: capping concurrency at 8 when 20 requests arrive means 12 of them now wait for a slot before starting their own timeout, and that queueing time is invisible to every per-call limit in the system.

Three controls, and the quantity each one actually bounds:

control bounds does not bound timeout one call's duration the request total deadline the request total how many run at once bulkhead concurrent calls any duration at all

You generally want all three, and the failure is using one to do another's job. A bulkhead without a deadline converts a latency problem into a queueing problem and hides it from your timeouts.

Where the deadline lives, in-process and across a boundary

In one process it is an object threaded through the call path, and asyncio.timeout is what enforces it — taking remaining_ms() rather than a constant, so a later stage is bounded by what is left. Across a service boundary the same instant travels as a header and is rebuilt on the other side; gRPC does this natively with grpc-timeout, and over HTTP it is a header you agree on and a middleware that turns it back into a deadline. Both halves have to exist or the budget stops at your edge, which is the failure this lesson opens with.

Two details that are easy to get wrong and expensive to debug. The clock must be monotonic: a wall clock steps backwards when NTP corrects it, and a deadline that moves backwards fires early with nothing in any log to explain it. And “deadline exceeded” deserves to be its own error rather than a generic timeout, because a slow hop may be worth retrying and an expired deadline never is — code that cannot tell them apart will retry into a budget that is already gone.

Which lands on the sentence worth keeping: the budget is not enforced by being written down, and five correct components can produce an incorrect system. The fix is not better numbers, it is measuring the right quantity — and the cost of measuring it is that some requests which used to arrive late now do not arrive at all.

Check yourself

Five stages each honour a 5 s timeout, each retries once, and a turn takes 11 880 ms against an 8 s budget. Which component is misconfigured?

  1. Nothing; a per-call limit cannot bound a request
  2. The stage timeouts, which should each be 1600ms
  3. The retry policy, which doubled every stage timeout
  4. The budget, which was set unrealistically low throughout

Nothing is misconfigured, and that is the finding rather than a dodge. Every stage stayed inside its own limit and every retry was permitted. A timeout measures a call and the budget is a property of the request, so no set of per-call limits composes into a per-request guarantee — five 5 s timeouts with one retry each permit a 50 s ceiling, and 11 880 ms is just where the distribution landed.

The 1 600 ms answer is tempting and was measured too: it does keep every turn inside the budget, and it failed 53 of 200 turns, because an equal slice assumes stages cost the same and that each one's latency is a point rather than a spread.

Propagating one deadline capped the worst turn at 8 001 ms and dropped completions from 200 of 200 to 174 of 200. What happened to those 26 turns?

  1. They finished late before and now fail on time
  2. They were refused by the retry budget token bucket
  3. They hit the per-stage timeout instead of the deadline
  4. They completed faster and stopped appearing in the tail

They used to return an answer at nine, ten or eleven seconds, to a user promised eight and to a client that had quite possibly given up. Now they fail at the budget. A deadline converts overruns into failures — it does not make slow work fast, and any claim that it improved things without this sentence attached is incomplete.

Whether that is an improvement is a product decision. If a late answer really is worth more than a fast failure, the correct response is to raise the budget so the promise matches the system, rather than to remove the thing enforcing it.

Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

A spoken answer to “you promised an 8-second turn — what actually enforces it?” Read it aloud, then say it again with the page covered.

  1. Situation

    Our turn budget is eight seconds, and it lived at the edge. Every stage below it had its own timeout, chosen by whoever wrote that stage.

  2. Mechanism

    A timeout is relative, so every hop starts its own clock. A deadline is absolute, so every hop reads the same instant. Only the second one composes.

    Five stages at five seconds each permit a twenty-five second ceiling, and one retry per stage makes it fifty. Nothing is misconfigured; the limits just do not add up to a promise.

  3. What I did

    I measured it across two hundred turns. The worst case was 11 880 milliseconds against an eight-second budget, with every timeout honoured and no alert that could have fired.

    Then I built one deadline at the edge and passed it to every stage, bounding each by whatever was left. The maximum landed on 8001 milliseconds, every run.

    It also refused 25 retries that could not have finished in the time remaining. That is what makes a retry budget enforceable instead of a whiteboard calculation.

  4. What it cost

    Completed turns went from 200 of 200 down to 174. A deadline converts overruns into failures. Twenty-six answers that used to arrive late now do not arrive.

    The median cost nothing, moving about fifteen milliseconds, because a deadline only ever binds on the runs that were going to break the promise anyway.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“We have timeouts on every external call.” Said as though it were the answer, when it is the configuration that produced the problem. The follow-up is “so what is the worst case for a whole turn?”, and the honest arithmetic — stages times timeout times attempts — is usually a number that surprises the person saying it. The version that holds distinguishes the two quantities: “each call has a timeout, and the request carries an absolute deadline that every stage reads, so the turn is bounded at eight seconds no matter how many hops get added.”

The second trap is claiming deadline propagation as a pure win. It is a tail intervention with a real cost, and an interviewer who has implemented one knows that some requests now fail which previously succeeded late. Saying the number yourself — twenty-six turns of two hundred — is the difference between having read about the pattern and having shipped it.

Probes to expect

  1. “You promised an 8-second turn. What enforces it?” The question behind the résumé bullet. Answering with the p50 misses it entirely — this is about the ceiling, not the distribution. Saying where it is not enforced is the honest opening.
    Model answer — say yours first

    It was enforced at the edge and nowhere below it, which means it was a measurement rather than a guarantee. Each of five stages had its own timeout, so the ceiling was five times the timeout, doubled again by one retry per stage. With 5 s stage timeouts that is a 50 s ceiling for an 8 s budget, and I measured a worst case of 11 880 ms with no timeout violated anywhere.

    The fix is a single absolute deadline built at the edge and passed to every stage, with each bounded by min(its own timeout, remaining). That put the maximum at 8 001 ms on every run, and moved the median by about 15 ms — it is a tail intervention, so it costs the common case nothing.

    Invites · “What did it cost?” — 26 turns of 200 that used to finish late now fail, which is the trade.
  2. “Why not just set every stage's timeout to the budget divided by the number of stages?” Sounds like the obvious fix and is worth having measured, because it fails in a direction people do not expect. Give the number, not the theory.
    Model answer — say yours first

    I tried it: 8 000 over five stages is 1 600 ms each, and it does keep every turn inside the budget — zero over, out of 200. It also failed 53 of those 200 turns, because an equal slice assumes the stages cost the same and that each one's latency is a point rather than a spread. A 1 600 ms limit sits well inside our stage distribution, so it refuses work that would have finished in time.

    A deadline gets this right without anyone allocating anything: each stage asks what is left, so a stage that runs long simply leaves less for the next one, instead of every stage getting its fixed fifth regardless. That is the real advantage over a static split — it reallocates automatically, in the direction the actual run went.

    Invites · “So what do you do when a stage runs out?” — fail fast with a distinct error, or degrade, but never extend.
  3. “How does this interact with your retries?” The composition question, and the place two lessons meet. The specific detail — checking remaining time before starting an attempt — is in the SRE book and almost never in real code.
    Model answer — say yours first

    It is what makes the retry arithmetic enforceable. I had worked out that three attempts fit a 1 600 ms stage only while a call stays under 433 ms, but nothing at runtime knew how much of the slice the earlier attempts had eaten, so the third started regardless. With a deadline, an attempt is checked against the time remaining and refused if it cannot finish — 25 refusals in 200 turns.

    The reason to refuse rather than start is that a late attempt is pure load: it costs the provider a full call and returns after anyone stopped caring. The SRE book puts it as checking there is enough time left before each stage, and treats work past a client's deadline as resources spent while no progress is made.

    Invites · “What about the request already in flight when the deadline passes?” — cancel it, and that is where SSE having no upstream channel bites.
  4. “Would a bulkhead help here?” A trap worth recognising. Bulkheads are good and they do not solve this, and being able to say which quantity each control bounds is the whole answer.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Not with this problem, no. A bulkhead bounds concurrency — how many calls to one dependency are in flight — so it stops a slow dependency consuming every worker. It bounds nothing about duration, so a turn can still run to fifty seconds inside it.

    It can actually make the symptom worse. Capping concurrency at 8 when 20 requests arrive means 12 of them wait for a slot before starting their own timeout, and that queueing time is invisible to every per-call limit in the system. I would want all three controls, with each doing its own job: a timeout for one call, a deadline for the request total, and a bulkhead for how many run at once.

    Invites · “So where does queueing time show up?” — only in an end-to-end measurement, which is why the deadline starts at the edge.
  5. “Where does the deadline physically live once you cross a service boundary?” Checks whether you have implemented this or only read about it. The monotonic-clock detail and the distinct error type are the two things that sound like experience.
    Model answer — say yours first

    In-process it is an object threaded through the call path, enforced with asyncio.timeout given remaining_ms() rather than a constant. Across a boundary the same instant travels as a header and is rebuilt on the other side — gRPC does it natively with grpc-timeout; over HTTP it is a header you agree on plus middleware that converts it back. Both halves have to exist or the budget stops at your edge.

    Two details I would not skip. The clock is time.monotonic, never wall clock, because NTP can step a wall clock backwards and a deadline that moves backwards fires early with nothing in the logs to explain it. And “deadline exceeded” is its own error type rather than a generic timeout, because a slow hop might be worth retrying and an expired deadline never is.

    Invites · “What if a downstream ignores the header?” — then your own deadline still fires, which is why it is enforced on both sides.

Recall — from lessons 0009–0011

Deadline propagation cancels a stage mid-call. What did lesson 0009 say about where that request's work was happening, and what does the cancellation waste?

It was happening on one specific replica, which had already paid prefill for that prompt's prefix and now holds those key-value blocks. Cancelling frees your worker but does not free the replica immediately, and the blocks it built stay resident under eviction pressure until something displaces them. So a cancelled request has already spent the expensive half of the work. The practical consequence is that cancellation is worth much more to the caller than to the provider, and that a retry of a cancelled request routed to the same replica is unusually cheap, because the prefix is still there — which is an argument for cancelling and retrying with affinity rather than cancelling and scattering.

lesson 0009 · work is resident on one replica
0010 derived that three attempts fit a 1600 ms stage only while a call stays under 433 ms, and measured p90 at 670 ms. What does a deadline change about that conclusion?

It turns the derivation from advice into enforcement. Without a deadline the attempt count is a fixed number chosen against the median, so at p90 the third attempt starts anyway and overruns the slice — the arithmetic was right and nothing was checking it. With a deadline each attempt is tested against the time actually remaining, so on a fast run all three happen and on a slow run the third is refused, which is exactly the adaptive behaviour the static number was trying to approximate. That is why the refused count is 25 rather than 0 or 200: it fires only on the runs where the earlier attempts ran long.

lesson 0010 · T ≤ 433 ms, and nothing checked it
0011 found that a breaker ignores a dependency that is slow rather than failing, unless the timeout counts as a failure. How does a propagated deadline change what “the timeout” even means?

It makes the limit vary per call, which the breaker has to be told about. Under propagation a late stage may be bounded at 400 ms rather than its nominal 5 s, so a call can be cut off while the dependency is behaving perfectly normally — and recording that as a dependency failure would open the breaker on evidence about your own budget rather than about the upstream. So the two have to be distinguished: a deadline-exceeded outcome should not feed the failure estimator, while a genuine stage timeout should. This is the concrete reason DeadlineExceeded is its own error type rather than a plain TimeoutError.
