The Breaker You Have to Defend

The three states are the easy part and nobody will ask you about them. What gets asked is where your threshold came from, and the honest answer has to survive the fact that the best source on retries recommends not having this component at all.

The win

Drive a breaker through all three states, then break it twice on purpose: once with a missing minimum-sample gate, where a single failed call refuses 100 healthy requests, and once with a dependency that is slow rather than failing, where the breaker stays shut through 100 requests over budget.

Why you, specifically
“…a two-pass Scout/Evaluator pipeline (Gemini, Tavily) grounding every vendor claim in a cited source.”

Gemini and Tavily are two third-party dependencies sitting inside a hard per-turn deadline, and you do not operate either of them. That is the exact situation a breaker exists for, and also the exact situation where a badly-tuned one turns a partial outage into a total one.

The second place this bites is the pipeline-failure debugger, which triages against a flaky upstream and then retriggers work unattended. A breaker there is protecting a dependency from your automation, which never gets bored and never backs off on its own.

Both bullets are defended in full in chapter 0001 and chapter 0005 of Résumé Mastery — this lesson is where the mechanism underneath them gets built and broken.

Start here — the plain version

A circuit breaker is a small state machine wrapped around calls to one dependency. While the dependency looks healthy it does nothing. When enough calls fail it opens, and every subsequent call fails instantly without touching the dependency at all. After a while it lets exactly one call through to see whether things have improved.

That much is on every diagram. The part that matters is underneath it: the breaker has to decide what “enough calls fail” means, and it does that with a failure estimator — a running measurement of how badly the dependency is doing. Every threshold you will be asked to justify is a property of that estimator, not of the state machine.

Closed
Normal. Calls go through and outcomes are recorded. Confusingly, “closed” is the working state, because the name comes from electrical circuits rather than doors.
EWMA
Exponentially weighted moving average: r = α·x + (1−α)·r. Forgets smoothly instead of at a window edge, and reacts faster.
Error budget
The failure allowance an SLO implies. 99.9 % available over 30 days is about 43 minutes of budget, and it is where a defensible threshold comes from.

The states are trivial; the estimator is the component

Draw the three states and you have drawn a diagram that generates no decisions. Every question you will actually be asked — when does it open, how long does it stay open, what makes it close, why those numbers — is answered by the thing underneath the states, which is a running estimate of how badly the dependency is doing. The state machine just reads that number and compares it to a threshold.

Which means the interesting failures are all estimator failures, and there are two of them worth causing on purpose. The first is measuring a rate over too few samples, so a single bad call reads as total failure. The second is measuring the wrong thing entirely: a breaker sees the outcomes you hand it, so a dependency that answers slowly but correctly is, as far as the breaker is concerned, perfectly healthy.

The source this lesson has to argue with

The Amazon Builders' Library article that lesson 0010 is built on does not recommend breakers. It says, directly: “Circuit breakers… are widely promoted to solve this problem. Unfortunately, circuit breakers introduce modal behavior into systems that can be difficult to test, and can introduce significant addition time to recovery. We have found that we can mitigate this risk by limiting retries locally using a token bucket.”

That is the considered position of people operating the largest fleet with this problem, and it is an argument for 0010's retry budget over this entire lesson. Take it seriously rather than routing around it: a breaker earns its place only where a budget cannot help, and being able to say where that is — out loud, unprompted — is worth more than any threshold you pick.

In plain words

The top row is the three states: closed lets calls through, open refuses them at once, and half-open lets one test call decide. Underneath sits what really decides: a window over recent results that gives one failure rate. With a single result in it, one failure reads as 100 %, so the fix is a minimum number of calls before it may open.

Now cover it and redraw

Sixty seconds. Three boxes, four arrows, and two windows underneath. The two windows are the half that turns a memorised diagram into an answer.

  • The three states with the two forward arrows and the two return arrows from half-open.
  • On the first arrow, both conditions — rate and sample count. Missing the second is the bug.
  • The estimator window under the states, with the number it produces feeding the threshold.
  • The nearly empty window: one cross, 100 %, and 100 healthy requests refused.
Draw the estimator before the states if you can bear to. It is the thing you will be questioned on, and drawing it first stops the answer becoming a recital of three state names.

Lab — open it, then break it twice

Lab 0011 ~20 minutes

One failed call refusing a hundred, and a dependency too slow to notice

  1. Drive the normal case first: 400 calls at 50 ms intervals, with a hard outage across the middle quarter. This is the diagram, as a transition log.

    hard outage from call 100 to 200 of 400
  sent 301, blocked by the breaker 99, failed 25, over budget 0
  final state: closed
  transitions:
    t+   6250 ms  -> open      (failure rate 50% over 50 samples)
    t+  11250 ms  -> half-open (5000 ms elapsed, admitting one probe)
    t+  11250 ms  -> closed    (probe succeeded)
    Twenty-five failures bought, ninety-nine calls saved

    The breaker absorbed 25 failures before it had enough evidence to open — that is the cost of requiring a 50-sample window at a 50 % threshold, and it is unavoidable rather than a tuning mistake. In exchange, 99 calls never reached a dependency that would have failed them, and never spent their own latency budget waiting.

    Note the third transition: the probe succeeded on the first try, so recovery cost exactly one request. That is the half-open design working. Note also what happened between t+6250 and t+11250 — nothing was measured, because nothing was sent. An open breaker is blind by construction.

  2. Now remove the minimum-sample gate, which is a one-line change and the most common way this component is built wrong. One call fails, at the very start, and every one of the other 399 succeeds.

    Before / after · one comparison

    Without the gate: one failed call refuses 100 healthy ones and takes five seconds to recover. The dependency was fine the entire time.

    With it: the breaker never moves, which is the correct behaviour for a single transient failure in four hundred.

    Why this fires in production and not in your tests

    A failure rate over one sample is 0 % or 100 %, with nothing in between. So the ungated breaker is not “sensitive”, it is a device that opens on the first failure after any quiet period — which means process start, a scale-up event, a low-traffic dependency, or three in the morning. Load tests keep the window full and never reproduce it.

    This is also the honest reason the Builders' Library calls breakers hard to test. The bug lives entirely in the interaction between traffic volume and window size, and nothing in the state machine hints that the interaction exists.

  3. In plain words

    A breaker only counts errors. A call that answers correctly but far too late counts as a success, so a slow service never trips it while requests blow their time budget. The fix is to count going over the budget as a failure, so the breaker opens and later calls fail at once.

    Second failure mode, and the one that matters more. The dependency does not fail at all — it answers, late. Every call during the middle quarter blows the 1600 ms stage budget and returns a correct result.

    timeout recorded as a success (the default mistake)
  sent 400, blocked by the breaker 0, failed 0, over budget 100
  final state: closed
  transitions: none -- the breaker never moved

timeout recorded as a failure
  sent 301, blocked by the breaker 99, failed 25, over budget 25
  transitions:
    t+   6250 ms  -> open      (failure rate 50% over 50 samples)
    t+  11250 ms  -> half-open (5000 ms elapsed, admitting one probe)
    t+  11250 ms  -> closed    (probe succeeded)
    A hundred requests over budget and a breaker reporting perfect health

    In the first run the failure rate is 0 % across all 400 calls, because every call succeeded. The breaker is working exactly as designed and is completely useless, because it is a failure detector and the problem is latency. Meanwhile 100 requests blew their budget.

    The second run changes one line: treat exceeding the budget as a failure. Now the breaker opens, and the 100 over-budget requests become 25 over budget and 99 refused instantly. Failing fast is better than failing slow, because a request that fails at 0 ms leaves the rest of the turn's budget intact for a fallback.

    This is the question to be ready for, because “partial degradation” is far more common than “hard down”. A dependency at 3× its normal latency will never trip a breaker that only counts exceptions.

  4. Swap the estimator and leave everything else identical. Sliding window against EWMA, same 400 calls, same outage, same threshold.

    The same breaker, disagreeing with itself by 18 failures

    EWMA opened 900 ms earlier and after 7 failures instead of 25. Nothing about the state machine changed; the whole difference is how the two estimators weight recent evidence. A sliding window has to fill half of itself with failures before the ratio moves, while an EWMA at α = 0.1 is already past 50 % after a short run of consecutive failures.

    Which is better depends on what you are protecting. Eighteen fewer failures is eighteen fewer user-visible errors. It is also a more twitchy device, more likely to open on a burst that was going to pass — and its samples count is the number of calls it has ever seen, not the number it is still weighing, so the minimum-sample gate is weaker protection on an EWMA than on a window.

  5. In plain words

    The right threshold depends on how strict your promise to users is, not on the service you call. A strict promise leaves little room for failure, so one ten-minute outage eats a big share of it, and opening early is worth an occasional false alarm. With a looser promise the same outage barely matters, so you open later.

    Now derive the threshold rather than picking it. Work out what your error budget actually permits, and compare it to the 50 % default you have been running.

    The threshold is a statement about how fast you are willing to burn budget

    At 99.9 % you have 43 minutes for the whole month, so a single ten-minute outage costs 23 % of it. That is what makes a low threshold defensible there: you cannot afford to spend 25 failures gathering evidence, and opening early is cheap relative to the budget.

    At 99 % you have 432 minutes and the same outage costs 2.3 %, so opening early buys little and the false-positive risk dominates. Same dependency, same code, opposite threshold, and the reason is the SLO rather than the dependency. That is the sentence to have ready, because “we used 50 % because that is the default” is the answer this question is designed to catch.

Underneath

One layer down · the estimator, the gate, and whether you want this at all

A breaker is a threshold on an estimator, and the estimator is the design

The state machine contributes three names and no decisions. What decides behaviour is how the failure rate is estimated, and the two common choices genuinely disagree. A sliding window counts the last N outcomes equally, which is easy to explain in a review and measures in requests rather than in time — so a dependency that has gone quiet keeps whatever verdict it had, indefinitely. An EWMA forgets continuously, reacts faster, and never quite forgets, so its notion of “how many samples” is not the same as the window's.

Identical calls, identical threshold, different estimator:

estimator opened at failures first samples sliding window(50) 6250 ms 25 50 ewma(α = 0.1) 5350 ms 7 107

Eighteen fewer user-visible failures, 900 ms sooner, from a component the diagram does not contain. If you can only defend one number in your breaker, defend this one.

The minimum-sample gate, and why low traffic is the dangerous case

A ratio needs a denominator. Over one sample the failure rate is 0 % or 100 % with nothing between, so an ungated breaker is not sensitive — it is a device that opens on the first failure after any lull. The gate is one comparison and it is the difference between a breaker that refused 100 healthy requests and one that correctly ignored a single blip.

What the estimator can even say, by sample count, at a 50 % threshold:

samples possible rates opens on 1 0% or 100% 1 failure 4 0, 25, 50, 75, 100% 2 failures 20 steps of 5% 10 failures 50 steps of 2% 25 failures

Note the direction of the trade: the gate that protects you from false opens is the same number that sets how many real failures you absorb before a true open. There is no setting that does both, which is why the answer is “derived from the SLO” rather than “tuned”.

In plain words

A retry budget caps how many extra tries you send, which protects the other service. A breaker protects you: it fails a call instantly instead of after a full timeout, and it stops first tries, not just retries. If you have no fallback to spend that saved time on, the retry budget alone is enough.

One breaker per dependency, and a clear division of labour

Per dependency, never per service: a breaker over “the downstream” in aggregate opens on the failures of the noisiest one and blocks calls to three healthy others. The scope of the estimator is the scope of the protection.

Three components that get confused, and what each one actually protects:

component protects bounds costs you retry budget the upstream total retry count retries you wanted circuit breaker you your latency spent calls that would work load shedding you your own queue requests you refuse

The breaker column is the narrow one. It protects you from spending 1600 ms discovering something is down, and it does that for first attempts as well as retries — which is the one thing a token bucket cannot do, since a bucket only ever sees retries.

Which lands on an uncomfortable conclusion the Builders' Library got to first: a breaker adds a mode that engages only during incidents, which is the mode with the least test coverage in any system. It is worth having where failing fast buys you a fallback you can actually run, and it is worth not having where a retry budget already bounds the load. Deciding which of those you are in, out loud, is the answer to this topic.

Check yourself

A breaker on a low-traffic dependency opens at three in the morning after one failed call, then closes five seconds later. The dependency was healthy throughout. What went wrong?

  1. The window was nearly empty, so one failure dominated
  2. The cooldown interval was far too short for recovery
  3. The failure threshold was set well below fifty percent
  4. The probe in half-open state succeeded by pure chance

A failure rate over one sample is 0 % or 100 %. With no minimum-sample gate, the first failure after a quiet period is a 100 % failure rate and the breaker opens on it. Measured: one failed call in four hundred refused 100 healthy requests; with the gate at 20, the same run never moved the breaker at all.

The timing in the question is the clue. Three in the morning means low traffic, which means an almost empty window — along with process start and scale-up events, that is precisely when this fires, and it is why a load test never reproduces it.

A dependency's latency triples. Every call still returns a correct result, well past your per-stage budget. What does a breaker that records exceptions do?

  1. Nothing, because it sees failures rather than latency
  2. Opens quickly, because slow calls fill its window
  3. Opens after the cooldown interval has fully elapsed
  4. Nothing, since the minimum-sample gate is blocking it

Nothing at all. Every call succeeded, so the failure rate is 0 % and the breaker reports perfect health while 100 requests blow their budget. A breaker sees only the outcomes you hand it, and a slow success is a success.

The fix is one line: record a timeout as a failure. That turned 100 over-budget requests into 25 over budget and 99 refused instantly — and failing at 0 ms is strictly better than failing at the timeout, because the rest of the turn's budget survives for a fallback. Partial degradation is more common than hard failure, so this is the case worth designing for.

Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

A spoken answer to “you call two third-party services inside a hard deadline — how do you stop them taking you down?” Read it aloud, then say it again with the page covered.

  1. Situation

    Our scoring pipeline calls Gemini and Tavily inside an eight-second per-turn deadline. We operate neither of them, and a bad ten minutes at either one is our outage.

  2. Mechanism

    A breaker is a state machine over a failure estimator, and the estimator is the part that matters. The three states read one number and compare it to a threshold.

    I swapped the estimator and left everything else alone. A sliding window opened after 25 failures; an EWMA opened after seven, 900 milliseconds sooner.

  3. What I did

    I broke it twice on purpose. Without a minimum-sample gate, one failed call out of four hundred refused 100 healthy requests, because one failure in one sample is a hundred percent.

    Then I made the dependency slow rather than failing. The breaker saw a zero percent failure rate and never moved, while 100 requests blew their budget. A breaker detects failures, not latency.

    And I derived the threshold instead of picking it. At 99.9 percent we have 43 minutes a month, so one ten-minute outage costs a quarter of it, and opening early is cheap.

  4. What it cost

    A mode that only runs during incidents, which is the least tested state in the system. The Amazon Builders' Library recommends a retry token bucket instead, for exactly that reason.

    So I keep breakers only where failing at zero milliseconds buys a fallback I can actually run. Where the goal is just bounding retry load, the bucket does it without adding a mode.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“We have a circuit breaker in front of the model calls.” The follow-up is always “what does it open at, and where did that number come from?”, and “fifty percent” with nothing behind it is worse than having no breaker, because it shows the component was installed rather than designed. The version that holds derives it: “it opens at a failure rate we set from the error budget, over a fifty-call window with a twenty-call minimum, and a service with a looser SLO calling the same upstream would want a different number.”

The second trap is describing the three states as though they were the design. Anyone can recite closed, open and half-open; almost nobody mentions the estimator underneath, which is where every real bug and every real threshold lives. If you have thirty seconds, spend them on the window and the minimum-sample gate and skip the state names entirely — the interviewer already knows them.

Probes to expect

  1. “You have a circuit breaker on the model call. What does it open at?” A number on its own fails this. The signal is the derivation, and the strongest version volunteers that a different service calling the same upstream should use a different number.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The threshold comes from the error budget rather than from a default. At 99.9 % over thirty days we have 43 minutes of unavailability for the whole month, so one ten-minute outage spends 23 % of it — that makes being slow to open expensive and a false open comparatively cheap, so we open on a tighter threshold over a smaller window.

    The consequence I would flag is that this is not a property of the dependency. A service with a 99 % SLO calling the identical upstream has 432 minutes of budget, the same outage costs 2.3 %, and it should open later and over a wider window. Same code, opposite tuning.

    Invites · “What window size?” — which is really a question about how many failures you are willing to absorb before opening.
  2. “Walk me through the states.” An invitation to recite, and reciting is the failure. Answer it in one sentence and spend the rest on the estimator, because that is where every bug and every threshold lives.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Closed passes calls and records outcomes, open fails them instantly without touching the dependency, half-open admits exactly one probe which either closes it or reopens it. The states are the easy half though — what decides the transitions is a failure estimator, and that is the part worth talking about.

    I ran the same breaker with two estimators on identical traffic. A 50-call sliding window opened after 25 failures; an EWMA at α = 0.1 opened after 7, about 900 ms earlier. Eighteen fewer user-visible errors, from a component the state diagram does not contain. The window is also why one probe is enough in half-open: it is a deliberately cheap experiment, and an experiment costing a thousand requests is not one you can repeat every few seconds.

    Invites · “Which estimator did you pick?” — and the honest answer names the trade rather than a winner.
  3. “Your upstream is not down — it is three times slower than usual. What does your breaker do?” The best question on this topic, because partial degradation is far more common than hard failure and most breakers sleep through it. Give the measured version, not the theoretical one.
    Model answer — say yours first

    By default, nothing, and that is a real bug rather than a subtlety. A breaker sees the outcomes you give it, so a slow success is a success — I measured a run where every call returned correctly, 100 of them blew the stage budget, the failure rate stayed at 0 % and the breaker never moved.

    The fix is to record exceeding the budget as a failure, which turned that into 25 over budget and 99 refused instantly. Failing at 0 ms rather than at the timeout is the actual win, because the rest of the turn's budget survives for a fallback. So the sentence I would want on the record is that a breaker is a failure detector and only becomes a latency detector if you make the timeout count.

    Invites · “What timeout, then?” — which is the deadline question, and it has its own arithmetic.
  4. “Do you need a circuit breaker at all?” Sounds like a trap and is a genuine question, because the best free source on this argues against them. Knowing the argument and being able to say where a breaker still earns its place is the strongest thing available here.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Often not. The Amazon Builders' Library explicitly prefers a retry token bucket, on the grounds that breakers “introduce modal behavior into systems that can be difficult to test, and can introduce significant addition time to recovery”. I measured the bucket doing the load-protection job on its own: retries cut from 245 to 8, peak from 320/s to 20/s, with no state machine and no mode.

    What a breaker adds is two things, both about me rather than the dependency: it fails calls at 0 ms instead of at the timeout, which returns the rest of the deadline to a fallback, and it applies to first attempts rather than only retries, so it caps the latency cost of an upstream that is simply down. Where I have a fallback worth running, that is worth the mode. Where I do not, the bucket is enough and I would rather not ship an untested state.

    Invites · “What fallback?” — and having a concrete one is what makes the breaker defensible.
  5. “How would you know your breaker is working, in production?” Tests whether you have thought past the happy path into operations. The modal-behaviour criticism is the thing to answer here, and the answer is about observability rather than about code.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Every state change is a logged event with the reason attached — the failure rate and the sample count that triggered it, not just “opened” — and the state is exported as a metric per dependency. Martin Fowler's write-up makes the same point: any change in breaker state should be logged, and a trip should usually raise an alert.

    The number I would actually watch is short-circuited calls, because it is the only one that distinguishes a breaker doing its job from a breaker that is stuck. And I would test the mode deliberately rather than waiting for an incident: force it open in staging and confirm the fallback path runs, because the criticism of breakers is precisely that the open state is the least exercised code in the system.

    Invites · “Have you ever seen one misfire?” — yes, the ungated one, and it refused a hundred healthy requests.
