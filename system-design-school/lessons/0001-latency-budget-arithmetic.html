Latency Budget Arithmetic

You already ship a system with a hard 8 s per-turn budget and a measured 2.1 s median. This lesson turns those two numbers into a table you can defend hop by hop — because in a design review the ceiling and the measurement are never the question. The question is always which hop owns which milliseconds.

The win

By the end you will have a per-hop budget for one interview turn, know which single hop owns the largest share, and be able to say what your remaining headroom is actually for.

Why you, specifically
“Built a custom OpenAI-compatible LLM gateway powering those interviews at ~2.1 s median time-to-first-word under a hard 8 s per-turn budget.”

That sentence is defended in full in chapter 0001 of Résumé Mastery — this lesson is where the mechanism underneath it gets built and broken.

Start here — the plain version

Someone finishes speaking and then waits for the avatar to answer. That wait is not one thing; it is six or eight separate steps happening one after another. A latency budget is nothing more than that list of steps written down with a millisecond figure next to each one, and a total at the bottom you are not allowed to exceed.

That is the whole idea. The reason it is worth a lesson is that once the list exists, three questions answer themselves: which step should you optimise, which steps are not yours to fix, and how much spare time you have when something goes wrong.

Latency
How long one thing takes, start to finish. Usually milliseconds (ms); 1000 ms = 1 second.
Hop
One step in the path a request travels — “look up documents”, “call the model”, “send audio to the browser”. Each hop spends part of the budget.
Critical path
The chain of steps that actually decides the total. Work happening off to the side, that nothing waits for, is free.

A budget is arithmetic, not a target

“8 seconds per turn” is not a goal you aim at. It is a quantity you spend, and every hop in the path takes a cut. Written down, a budget does three things a single median cannot:

  • It tells you the largest consumer, which is the only hop worth optimising first.
  • It separates what you control from what you do not. A TLS handshake and a silence threshold are floors; your retrieval step is a choice.
  • It exposes headroom, which is what absorbs the tail. A system whose median exactly equals its ceiling fails roughly half the time.

Serial hops add; parallel hops take the maximum

This sounds obvious and is the most common arithmetic error in a design review. If retrieval takes 165 ms and ASR finalisation takes 300 ms, then:

  • Run them one after the other and you spend 465 ms.
  • Run them concurrently and you spend 300 ms — the slower of the two, not the sum.

Whenever a hop does not need the previous hop's output, it does not belong on the critical path. Half of all latency work is noticing that.

The drawing shows one turn three ways, each step as wide as its time. One step, the model’s wait before it produces anything (time to first token), is 42% of the turn. Running two small steps alongside others saves 267 ms; halving that one big step saves 440 ms.

Now cover it and redraw

Sixty seconds, and this one is really three bars of the same length with different splits. Draw it in an interview and you have answered “where does the time go” and “what would you fix first” in one move.

  • Hops to scale. Equal-width boxes make every hop look equally worth fixing, which is precisely the mistake.
  • The largest hop marked and named, with its share of the total. If you remember one number from this lesson, 42 % is it.
  • Which hops can overlap, and the one-line reason: they do not consume the previous hop's output.

Underneath

One layer down · medians add, tails do not, and neither of them is your budget

In plain words

Typical (median) step times add up to the typical turn: 2102 ms predicted, 2158 ms measured. Slow cases do not, because the steps are rarely all slow on the same turn. Summing each step’s p99, the time 99 turns in 100 come in under, gives 4701 ms; the real p99 turn is 3812 ms, so the sum is a safe overestimate.

What actually happens when you add eight hops up

Here is the same decomposition with a variance assumption on each hop — lognormal, with the sigma the fake upstream uses for time-to-first-token and smaller ones elsewhere. VAD and the network are held fixed because they are floors, not distributions. 400 000 simulated turns, nearest-rank percentiles:

hop median p99 VAD 250 250 ASR 300 678 ingress 12 19 retrieval 165 331 TTFT 880 2504 TTS 350 703 avatar 90 161 network 55 55 ———————————————————————————— sum of column 2102 4701 ← adding them up actual turn 2158 3812 ← the real distribution

Two results, and they point in opposite directions. The medians very nearly add — 2102 predicted against 2158 measured, the gap being lognormal skew. The p99s emphatically do not: summing the column gives 4701 where the truth is 3812, an overstatement of 1.23×.

The reason is that summing p99s asks for the probability that every hop is simultaneously in its own tail, and independent hops do not cooperate like that. Formally, medians add because expectation is linear; tails compress because variances add rather than standard deviations, so the spread of a sum grows like √n while its centre grows like n. Add more hops and the turn gets slower faster than it gets less predictable.

In plain words

Headroom is the gap between the 8 s limit and what a turn really takes. Ordinary variation never uses it all: even the slowest turn in a thousand reaches only 60% of the limit. It is kept for what the simulation leaves out, like a retry or a stalled service, which can land on the same turn together.

What the headroom is actually worth

Same simulation, against the 8000 ms ceiling:

p50 2158 ms 27 % of budget p95 3150 ms 39 % p99 3812 ms 48 % p99.9 4826 ms 60 % breach rate over 8000 ms: 0.001 %

Which reads like enormous safety and is the most dangerous slide in this lesson. Read what it actually says: if every hop merely varies, you never breach. One in a hundred thousand turns.

Now look at what is not in the model. No retry, which doubles a hop. No upstream stall. No cold model. No queueing at the gateway when concurrency exceeds the provider quota. Lesson 0002 injects a single two-second freeze and takes p99 to 1934 ms on a hop that models at 149 — and every one of those two seconds comes straight out of this headroom.

That is what the remaining 5.9 seconds is for. It is not slack you failed to spend. It is the room a retry, a stall and a bad tail day all have to fit inside together.

Two of your hops are not yours

VAD end-of-speech and the network are floors. A voice-activity detector cannot conclude a turn ended until it has waited out a silence window — shortening it does not make you faster, it makes you interrupt people — and light takes as long as it takes. Together that is 305 ms, about 14 % of the median turn, permanently.

Which is worth stating as a number because it reframes the arithmetic: your addressable budget is not 2102 ms, it is 1797. And of that addressable portion, time to first token is not 42 % but 49 % — essentially half of everything you are allowed to touch, sitting in one hop, provided by someone else.

Medians add, tails compress, and floors do neither. Quote the median and you understate; sum the p99s and you overstate by about a quarter but stay safe; and either way the model only covers variance. The failure that eats your budget is the one this arithmetic cannot contain — which is the entire subject of the next lesson.

Check yourself

Your ceiling is 8 s and the measured total is 2.1 s. What is that remaining 5.9 s actually for?

  1. Headroom for the tail, retries, and slower hops
  2. Nothing at all, because medians hide tail cost
  3. Space to append three more sequential model calls
  4. Assurance that no turn will ever breach budget

Headroom is not slack you have failed to spend — it is what absorbs variance. A median of 2.1 s against an 8 s ceiling says nothing about how often a turn breaches, because the median is the one statistic guaranteed to ignore the half of the distribution that breaches things. It is also what you draw on when a retry doubles a hop.

“Space for three more model calls” is the tempting wrong answer: it treats the budget as capacity to fill rather than variance to absorb, and it is how an 8 s budget quietly becomes a 9 s p99.

Which single change to the table above buys you the most headroom?

  1. Cut time-to-first-token, by far the largest single hop
  2. Shave gateway ingress, already the cheapest hop here
  3. Shorten the VAD silence window, an external floor
  4. Overlap retrieval with ASR, removing one serial hop

The LLM hop is 880 ms of a 2102 ms turn — about 42%. Halving it saves 440 ms. Overlapping retrieval with ASR is a genuinely good idea and it is the second answer, but it can only ever save 165 ms, because that is all retrieval costs. Gateway ingress at 12 ms is not worth an afternoon.

The VAD window is a floor, not a hop you own: shortening it makes the detector cut candidates off mid-sentence, which trades a latency number for a product defect.

Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

A spoken answer to “walk me from the moment the candidate stops speaking to the moment the avatar starts.” This is the question your résumé's two numbers invite, and it wants a drawing plus this script.

  1. Situation

    We had a hard 8-second per-turn budget on a live avatar interview, and a measured 2.1-second median. Two numbers, and no account at all of which hop owned which milliseconds.

  2. Mechanism

    A budget is not a target you aim at, it is a quantity every hop takes a cut of. So I wrote the cuts down: voice-activity detection to declare the turn over, speech recognition, gateway ingress, retrieval, time to first token, speech synthesis, the first video frame, and the network.

    Two of those are floors — the silence window and the speed of light. About 300 milliseconds I will never get back, so my addressable budget is not 2.1 seconds, it is 1.8.

  3. What I did

    Drawn to scale, one hop is 42 % of the turn: time to first token, 880 milliseconds. Gateway ingress and the network together are 3 % — and those are the two I own outright, which is exactly why they are tempting.

    I checked the alternative before committing. Overlapping retrieval under speech recognition and the avatar render under synthesis buys 267 milliseconds. Halving the one big hop buys 440. So the restructuring work went to the back of the queue.

  4. What it cost

    Two honest limits. That hop is someone else’s service. So “halve it” means prefix caching, a smaller model, or a different provider. None of them is free, and one is a quality decision rather than a latency one.

    The budget models variance, not failure. Modelled out, even p99.9 only reaches 60 % of the ceiling. That looks like safety until you notice the model has no retry and no stall in it. That headroom is not slack. It is the room a retry and a bad tail day have to fit inside together.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“We're at 2.1 seconds against an 8-second budget, so we have plenty of headroom.” Every word is true and the conclusion invites the one question you least want: what is the p99? A median against a hard ceiling says nothing whatsoever about how often you breach it, because the median is the single statistic guaranteed to ignore the half of the distribution that does the breaching.

The other trap is subtler and very common: “each hop is within its budget, so the turn is within budget.” That holds for medians and fails for tails. Summing the hops' p99s gave 4.7 seconds where the real turn p99 was 3.8 — so the sum is a genuine upper bound and overstates by about a quarter. Useful, and not the same statement.

Probes to expect

  1. “Walk me from the moment the candidate stops speaking to the moment the avatar starts. Give me the millisecond budget for each hop.” They want the table, not a story. Having it memorised is the difference between sounding like the architect and sounding like a maintainer.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Eight hops. Voice-activity detection has to wait out a silence window before it can conclude the turn ended — about 250 ms and not negotiable, because shortening it means interrupting people. Then speech recognition commits a final transcript, 300. Gateway ingress, 12. Retrieval — embed the query and search — 165. Time to first token, 880. Speech synthesis to first audio, 350. First video frame, 90. Network to the client, 55.

    That sums to about 2.1 seconds, and the shape matters more than the total: one hop is 42 % of it, and the two hops I fully own are 3 % between them.

    Invites · “Which of those did you measure and which did you estimate?” — the right answer names them separately, because a hop you cannot measure independently is a hop you cannot defend.
  2. “Is 2.1 s time-to-first-word or time-to-complete? Which one does the candidate actually experience?” If you cannot answer instantly, the number stops being evidence. Know which metric your bullet quotes.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The one the candidate experiences is time to first word — the avatar starting to speak is the perceptible event, and after that they are listening rather than waiting. Time to complete matters for something different: whether they can interrupt, and how quickly the next turn can start.

    So they are two numbers with two purposes, and quoting one figure without saying which is the actual weakness in how I had written it down.

    Invites · “Which one is your 2.1 s?” — and if the honest answer is “I would have to check how that was gathered”, say that, then say what you would check.
  3. “You have 5.9 s of headroom. Why not add a second verification pass to every turn?” The trap is to say yes. Headroom is variance absorption; spending it on mean-case work is how you build something that breaches under load.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Because the headroom is not spare capacity, it is the room reserved for things going wrong. Modelling the turn out, even p99.9 only reaches about 60 % of the ceiling — and that model contains no retry, no upstream stall and no cold model. A single retry on the largest hop is nearly another second on its own.

    A second verification pass would double the hop that is already 42 % of the turn, and it would do it on every turn to catch a problem on a few. If it is worth doing, it belongs off the critical path or behind a confidence threshold.

    Invites · “When would you spend the headroom?” — on a retry with a propagated deadline, which is lesson 0012: spend it on reliability you can bound, not on work you always do.
  4. “Which of those hops are on the critical path and which could run concurrently?” Retrieval does not depend on the final ASR transcript — a partial is usually enough to start embedding. Saying so unprompted reads as senior.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The test is whether a hop consumes the previous hop's output. Recognition needs the audio, retrieval needs the transcript, the model needs the retrieved context — that chain is genuinely serial and no amount of concurrency touches it.

    Two things can come off it. The avatar's first frame does not need the synthesised audio, so it can render under synthesis — about 90 ms. And retrieval can start on a partial transcript rather than the final one, hiding it under recognition — about 177. Both together are 267 ms, against 440 for halving the big hop, which is why I did the arithmetic before the refactor.

    Invites · “Is overlapping retrieval with ASR actually free?” — no: it means embedding a partial transcript and possibly re-running on the final one, so it trades wasted work for latency.
