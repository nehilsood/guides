p50 Is a Lie

Your own prep notes already flag this one: “median with a stated hard budget invites the p95/p99 question immediately.” This lesson goes further than answering that question. You are going to freeze your gateway for two seconds and watch a standard load generator report that nothing happened.

The win

You will cause a measurable 2-second outage, measure it two different ways on the same server, and see one of the measurements miss it by 13×. After this you will never quote a percentile without saying how it was gathered.

Why you, specifically
“2.1 s median TTFW — median with a stated hard budget invites the p95/p99 question immediately.”
— your own interview-prep.md, soft spot #3

You already know the question is coming. What that note does not yet say is the harder half: even if you have a p99, it is probably wrong, and wrong in the flattering direction. The tool that produced it almost certainly stopped taking samples exactly when your service was at its worst.

That sentence is defended in full in chapter 0002 of Résumé Mastery — this lesson is where the mechanism underneath it gets built and broken.

Start here — the plain version

Time a thousand requests, then sort the timings from fastest to slowest. The one in the middle is the median, also called p50. The one 99% of the way along is p99 — only one request in a hundred was slower than that. These are percentiles, and they are how latency is reported, because a single average hides everything interesting.

Then the twist, which is the actual lesson. The tool you use to produce those numbers is probably lying to you, and lying in the flattering direction. Most load generators wait for a reply before sending the next request — so when your server freezes, the generator stops asking, records nothing during the freeze, and reports that nothing happened. The freeze is real and your users felt it. Your graph is clean.

Percentile
Sort every timing, then pick a position. p95 = 400 ms means 95% of requests finished in under 400 ms.
Median (p50)
The middle timing. Describes the typical request — which is the one nobody ever complains about.
Tail
The slowest few percent. Where all the complaints, timeouts and breached budgets live.
Long tail
Latency is lopsided, not a bell curve: a firm floor, a dense middle, and a few requests far slower than the rest. So the p99 can sit several times above the median.
Coordinated omission
The measurement bug in this lesson. Your load generator pauses while the server is stalled, so the stall's slow requests are never recorded and the percentiles come out far too good. Named by Gil Tene.
Closed-loop
A load generator that sends the next request only after the last one comes back. It throttles itself exactly when the server is struggling — which is what causes the bug above.
Open-loop
A load generator that sends at a fixed rate regardless of whether replies arrive. It keeps demanding while the server is stalled, so the stall shows up.
Nearest-rank
Reporting a percentile by picking a real measured value, rather than averaging between two of them. Averaging invents a number nobody observed, precisely in the region you care about.

Why the median is the wrong statistic

A median tells you about the typical request. Nobody complains about the typical request. In a system with a hard ceiling, the only interesting question is how often do we breach, and the median is the one statistic mathematically guaranteed to tell you nothing about that — it discards the entire upper half of the distribution by construction.

Worse, request latency is not symmetric. It is roughly lognormal: a firm floor set by real work, a dense middle, and a long right tail. In a distribution shaped like that the mean sits above the median and the p99 can sit multiples above both. Google's SRE book puts it plainly — “a typical request is served in about 50 ms, 5% of requests are 20 times slower.”

The arithmetic that surprises people

If one turn needs five sequential calls and each has a p99 of 200 ms, the turn's p99 is not 200 ms. Roughly speaking, the chance that all five avoid their own tail is 0.99⁵ ≈ 0.951 — so about 5% of turns hit at least one slow hop. Your five-stage pipeline turns a rare event into a common one. Tail latency compounds along the critical path, and that is the single most useful thing on this page.

Nearest-rank, not interpolated

A small but load-bearing detail. There are several definitions of “percentile”, and some interpolate between adjacent samples. With a fat tail, interpolating between your top two observations produces a figure nobody measured — an invented number describing the only part of the distribution anyone is arguing about. The load generator in the anchor repo uses nearest-rank, ceil(q/100 × N) − 1, and there is a test asserting it never returns an unobserved value.

A bug found while building this lesson

The first version of that function used Python's round(). Python rounds half to even, so round(99.5) is 100, and for any run of exactly 100 samples the tool reported the maximum as p99. The test in tests/test_load_math.py caught it. Measurement code needs tests exactly as much as the code being measured.

Coordinated omission

Here is the failure that matters, and it is a property of the measuring tool, not the system under test.

Almost every load generator works in a closed loop: spin up N workers, each in a tight cycle of send a request, wait for the response, send the next one. It is the obvious design. It is also the reason your percentiles are wrong.

Consider a server that freezes for 2 seconds. In a closed loop with 6 workers, all 6 are blocked inside a request when the freeze begins. They record 6 slow samples. Then they resume. During the freeze the generator issued no new requests at all — it was waiting, politely, in lockstep with the outage. The 2-second stall contributes exactly 6 data points, no matter how long it lasted.

A real user population does not wait politely. Requests keep arriving throughout the freeze, and every one of them experiences it. The correct model is an open loop: schedule requests at a fixed arrival rate, fire them whether or not earlier ones have returned, and measure each from the moment it was due — not from when a worker happened to be free.

Gil Tene named this coordinated omission: the measurement omits exactly the samples that mattered, and it does so in coordination with the system's own bad behaviour.

  1. Run the comparison. It drives the gateway 1200 times under each scheduling model and injects an identical 2-second freeze 2 seconds into each run.

  2. Read the closed-loop table first. This is the shape of the number almost everyone quotes.

    === closed loop — 6 workers (coordinated omission) ===
requests   1200   errors 0
            TTFW ms    TOTAL ms
    p50        75.0        75.1
    p90       103.4       103.4
    p95       115.3       115.3
    p99       149.7       149.8
  p99.9      2043.8      2043.8
    max      2081.2      2081.2
    You should see

    A pristine table. p99 of about 150 ms. The 2-second outage is invisible until p99.9, and it only surfaces there by luck — 6 bad samples out of 1200 is 0.5%, which sits just inside the top 0.1% by a hair. Run it with 3 workers and even p99.9 goes clean.

  3. Now the open-loop table. Same server, same freeze, same total requests.

    === open loop — 80 req/s (corrected) ===
requests   1200   errors 0
            TTFW ms    TOTAL ms
    p50        79.1        79.2
    p90       584.3       584.3
    p95      1326.6      1326.6
    p99      1932.6      1932.6
  p99.9      2059.4      2059.4
    max      2091.6      2091.6
    The thing to actually notice

    The p50 is identical — 75 ms versus 79 ms. Both tools agree completely about the typical request. But p99 is 150 ms versus 1933 ms: the closed-loop tool under-reported the tail by 13×. Your numbers will differ from these; the shape will not.

  4. Prove the mechanism rather than taking it on faith. Vary the worker count and watch the number of bad samples track it exactly.

    $ uv run uvicorn app.main:app # terminal 1

# terminal 2 — closed loop, 3 workers, then 40
$ uv run python bench/load.py --mode closed --concurrency 3 \
    --requests 1200 --stall-after 2 --stall-ms 2000
$ uv run python bench/load.py --mode closed --concurrency 40 \
    --requests 1200 --stall-after 2 --stall-ms 2000
    You should see

    With 3 workers the freeze produces 3 slow samples and vanishes entirely. With 40 workers it produces 40, which is 3.3% of the run, so it starts appearing at p95. The reported tail is a function of your worker count, not of your service. That is the sentence to remember.

  5. Watch the open-loop generator admit it is behind. This diagnostic does not exist in closed-loop tools, because they cannot fall behind by construction.

    generator fell behind on 1 request(s); worst lateness 6 ms
    You should see

    A small number here on a healthy run. Push --rate past what the gateway can serve and this line grows — that is your saturation point, measured rather than guessed.

So what is your real number?

You cannot know from here. But you can now ask the right question about your own platform, and it is a short one: was the 2.1 s figure gathered from a fixed-rate arrival process, or from workers that waited for each response? If it was the latter — and it usually is — then the honest version of your résumé bullet is not a different number. It is the same number plus a sentence about how it was measured.

Do not over-correct

Open-loop measurement is not automatically “the real number” either. Drive an open loop far past capacity and you build an unbounded queue that no real client would ever tolerate — every latency figure then describes your generator's backlog rather than your service. Real clients time out, retry, and give up. The right rate to measure at is the one you actually expect to serve, plus a defined margin.

Underneath

One layer down · why the correction is what it is, and the arithmetic that forbids averaging

“Use an open-loop generator” is the right conclusion and not yet an explanation. The explanation is that a closed-loop tool's sample rate depends on the thing it is measuring, which is a specific and predictable bias, not noise — and once you can predict it you can compute exactly how blind your tool is before you run it.

You can predict the blindness in advance

A closed-loop worker is blocked for the whole stall and emits one slow sample when it unblocks. So:

bad samples (closed) = number of workers — independent of stall length bad samples (open) = arrival rate × stall length 6 workers → 6 / 1200 = 0.5 % → needs p99.5 to see it 80 req/s × 2 s → 160 / 1200 = 13 % → visible from p90

Read the first line again: the closed-loop count does not depend on how long the stall was. A two-second freeze and a two-minute freeze both produce exactly six bad samples. Your tool's sensitivity to an outage is set by a concurrency flag you chose for unrelated reasons.

Which gives you a rule you can apply without running anything: a closed-loop run with W workers and N requests cannot show a stall below the 1 − W/N percentile. Six workers and 1200 requests means nothing below p99.5 — so quoting p99 from that run is quoting a number that was structurally incapable of containing the problem.

This is also why the lab asks you to vary the worker count. Three workers gives three bad samples; forty gives forty. The bad-sample count tracks the flag exactly, which is the difference between believing the mechanism and having watched it.

Nearest-rank, and why interpolation is dishonest here

With 1200 samples, p99 is the 1188th value in sorted order — an actual request that actually happened. An interpolating percentile would instead blend the values around that rank and report a number no request ever experienced. For a latency SLO that is the wrong choice, because the claim you want to make is “99 % of real turns finished within X”, and X has to be a real turn.

It also decides your minimum sample count, which people get wrong constantly:

to have any p99.9 at all you need n ≥ 1000 1200 samples → p99.9 is the 1198.8th → the 1199th value → a single request decides the whole figure

So the p99.9 of 2058 ms in the closed-loop table rests on one sample. It is directionally true and it is not a number to make a promise on. Anything past p99 needs an order of magnitude more requests before it means what it looks like it means.

Why you cannot average percentiles, ever

This is the single most common quiet error in latency reporting: five services each report p99, someone averages them, and the resulting figure has no interpretation at all. A percentile is a rank statistic, and ranks do not add. The mean of two p99s is not the p99 of the combined population, and it is not an upper or lower bound on it either.

The direction the error runs in matters, and it runs against you. If five serial stages each independently meet p99, the chance a turn escapes all five tails is:

P(no stage in its tail) = 0.99⁵ = 0.951 → 4.9 % of turns hit at least one stage's p99 tail so the pipeline's 95th percentile is roughly a stage's 99th

Five stages that are each “fine at p99” compose into a pipeline that is not fine at p95. That is tail amplification, it is why a five-stage pipeline feels worse than its components look, and it is the correct answer to the second probe below.

To actually combine tails you need the underlying distributions, not their percentiles — which is why HdrHistogram exists and why every serious latency tool ships histograms rather than summary rows.

Service time and response time are different quantities

Service time is how long the work took once it started. Response time is how long the caller waited, which includes sitting in a queue. A closed-loop tool measures something much closer to service time, because a worker cannot queue behind itself — it is not allowed to be waiting for two things at once. That is the deeper reason the two tables differ: they are not two measurements of one quantity, they are measurements of two different quantities, and only one of them is what a user feels.

The open-loop generator's generator fell behind on 1 request(s); worst lateness 8 ms line exists to make that visible. It is admitting that its own scheduler slipped, and it is a diagnostic a closed-loop tool cannot emit even in principle — falling behind is not a state it can be in.

The bias is computable, so it is not an excuse. 1 − W/N tells you the best percentile your run could possibly reveal; the arrival rate times the stall tells you what an honest run would have shown. And whatever you do, do not average percentiles — 0.99⁵ is the number that explains your pipeline.

Check yourself

A closed-loop generator with 8 workers hits a 30-second server freeze. How many slow samples does it record?

  1. Eight, one for each worker blocked inside a request
  2. Hundreds, since the freeze lasted a full thirty seconds
  3. None at all, because a frozen server records nothing
  4. Eight thousand, matching the requests a user would send

Exactly one per worker. The workers are each blocked inside a single in-flight request, and while frozen the generator issues nothing new — so a 30-second outage and a 3-second outage contribute the identical number of samples, differing only in magnitude. Eight samples will not move p99 in any run of meaningful length.

This is why the reported tail scales with your worker count. It is a property of the harness, and it is why the fix is a different scheduling model rather than a bigger run.

One turn makes five sequential calls, each with a p99 of 200 ms. What is the turn's p99, roughly?

  1. Roughly 200 ms, though reached by 5% of turns
  2. Exactly 200 ms, no matter how many stages run
  3. About 1000 ms, because the five values add up
  4. Around 40 ms, as the delay splits across calls

The magnitude of a single slow hop stays around 200 ms — one call went slow, not all five. What compounds is the probability: 0.99⁵ ≈ 0.951, so roughly 5% of turns now contain at least one tail event. A once-in-a-hundred problem has become a one-in-twenty problem purely by adding stages.

“The p99 values add up” is the seductive wrong answer. It would be right for the worst case, but percentiles are not additive, and treating them as such over-provisions badly. The correct instinct is that adding stages to a pipeline degrades the tail even when every stage is individually fine — which is precisely the risk profile of a five-stage schematization pipeline.

Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

A spoken answer to “you said 2.1 seconds median — what is p99, and how did you measure it?” This is the follow-up your résumé invites, so it is the one worth being able to give cold.

  1. Situation

    I had a median of 2.1 seconds against a hard 8-second per-turn budget, and I was quoting the median because it was the number the tool printed.

  2. Mechanism

    The problem is that most load generators are closed loop: a fixed set of workers, each sending again only when the last response comes back. So when the server stalls, the workers stall with it and simply stop asking.

    That means each worker contributes exactly one slow sample no matter how long the stall lasted. The tool's sample rate depends on the thing it is measuring. That is coordinated omission.

  3. What I did

    I injected an identical two-second freeze into two runs of the same server. Closed loop with six workers reported a p99 of 149 milliseconds — clean. Open loop, at 80 requests a second, reported 1934. Same freeze, 13× apart.

    And I can show why in advance: six workers over 1200 requests is 0.5 %, so nothing below p99.5 could ever have contained it. The p99 I would have quoted was structurally incapable of seeing the outage.

  4. What it cost

    Rate-scheduled load is harder to run. The generator can fall behind and become the bottleneck, so it has to report its own lateness. Mine did: one request, eight milliseconds late.

    The honest limit is that I now have two numbers instead of one, and they measure different things. Closed loop is roughly a service time; open loop is a response time and includes queueing. Only the second one is what a candidate in an interview actually feels.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“Our p99 is 150 milliseconds.” Stated without how it was gathered, this is the number that gets picked up and pulled on, and there is a good chance it came from a fixed-worker run that could not have seen your worst two seconds. Always attach the method — “p99 of 1.9 seconds under open-loop arrival at 80 a second” is a smaller-sounding claim and a much harder one to knock down.

The other one to never say: “each stage is fine at p99, so the pipeline is fine at p99.” Percentiles do not add. Five serial stages each meeting p99 leaves 4.9 % of turns hitting at least one tail, so a stage's p99 is roughly the pipeline's p95. Averaging percentiles across services is the same error wearing a suit.

Probes to expect

  1. “You said 2.1 s median. What is p95, p99, and how did you measure them?” The third clause is the real question. Answering the first two without it is the trap — a confident wrong p99 is worse than “I know our tooling is closed-loop, so treat that figure as a floor.”
    Model answer — say yours first

    The “how” is the important half, so I would lead with it: it depends entirely on the load model, and most tools give you a number that cannot see your worst minutes. I ran the same server through an identical two-second freeze twice — fixed workers reported p99 at 149 ms, rate-scheduled arrival reported 1934 ms. Thirteen times apart.

    And I can show it is structural rather than bad luck: six workers over 1200 requests is 0.5 % of samples, so nothing below p99.5 could have contained the freeze at all.

    Invites · “So what is your real p99?” — the open-loop one, and it is the number I would put an SLO on because it includes queueing, which is what the user feels.
  2. “Your pipeline has five stages. If each is fast at p99, why is the whole thing slow at p99?” They are testing whether you know tails compound in probability along the critical path. Have the 0.99ⁿ arithmetic ready.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Because percentiles do not compose by addition. If each of five serial stages independently meets its p99, the chance a turn misses every tail is 0.99⁵, which is 0.951 — so 4.9 % of turns hit at least one stage's tail.

    Which means a stage's p99 is roughly the pipeline's p95. The parts each look fine and the whole is not, and no stage owner is wrong. It is also why averaging p99s across services produces a figure with no interpretation — to combine tails you need the distributions, which is what histograms are for.

    Invites · “So how do you fix it?” — either shorten the critical path so there are fewer tails to hit, or bound each stage with a propagated deadline, which is lesson 0012.
  3. “How would you know if your gateway froze for two seconds last Tuesday?” A dashboard of medians would not show it. This is the bridge to lesson 0032 — you need percentile histograms and traces, not averages.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Honestly? Right now, probably not — and that is the uncomfortable half of this lesson. A two-second freeze inside an 8-second budget breaches nothing, so no alert fires, and if the dashboard is a median or even a p99 from a fixed-worker probe, six samples out of thousands will not move it.

    What would catch it is a histogram rather than pre-aggregated percentiles, so the tail is still reconstructable after the fact, plus an alert on the rate of change of the tail rather than on a threshold. A synthetic prober at a fixed arrival rate would also see it, because it cannot stop asking.

    Invites · “What would you alert on, concretely?” — and the honest answer is that I have the measurement discipline now and not yet the alerting, which is lesson 0033.
  4. “Would you rather have a p50 of 2 s or a p50 of 3 s with a much tighter p99?” Usually the second, and the SRE book says why: people tolerate a slightly slower system far better than a highly variable one. Saying that unprompted signals you have thought about users, not just graphs.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The 3-second one, without much hesitation, because I have a budget rather than a target. Nothing rewards me for finishing a turn in two seconds instead of three — the conversation is human-paced. What hurts is a turn that breaches 8 seconds, because at that point the candidate is sitting in silence wondering if it broke.

    So the second option is strictly better: it converts headroom I was not using into variance I no longer have. The general form is that a hard ceiling makes the tail the thing you optimise, and the median mostly a comfort.

    Invites · “Is that always true?” — no. If the median sat close to the ceiling, or if cost scaled with time, I would be trading real money for variance and would want the numbers first.
  5. “What is the difference between service time and response time here?” Service time is how long the work took; response time includes the queueing before it started. A closed loop measures something closer to the former and calls it the latter.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Service time is how long the work took once it started. Response time is how long the caller waited, which includes queueing before it started. For one turn that is the difference between “the model took 880 ms” and “the candidate waited 2.9 seconds”.

    And it is exactly what the two tables measure. A closed-loop worker cannot queue behind itself — it is never waiting for two things at once — so it reports something close to service time. Open loop lets requests pile up and measures from when each was due, which is a response time. They are not two measurements of one quantity.

    Invites · “Which one does your dashboard show?” — server-side spans are service time, and if that is all you have you are blind to queueing at the edge by construction.
