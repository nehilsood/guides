Eviction, and Why LRU Is Wrong

Your cache from lesson 0003 has no memory limit and therefore no eviction policy. That is fine for a laptop and a liability in production. This lesson fills Redis until it refuses writes, then shows you why the default everyone reaches for is the wrong default for an embedding workload.

The win

You will exhaust a 32 MB Redis, see writes fail rather than evict, then watch allkeys-lru and allkeys-lfu produce measurably different hit rates on the same access pattern — and know which one your workload wants.

Why you, specifically
“Designed an on-device RAG and function-calling layer over a quantized MobileLLM, covering 50+ TV functions from natural-language queries.”

On-device is where eviction policy stops being academic. A TV has a hard memory ceiling you cannot raise by changing an instance type, the access pattern is heavily skewed — a handful of functions account for most invocations — and skew is precisely the condition under which LRU and LFU diverge. You have already shipped the workload this lesson is about.

Start here — the plain version

A cache has a fixed amount of memory. Fill it, and something has to go before anything new can come in. Eviction is the act of deleting an entry to make room, and the eviction policy is simply the rule for choosing which one dies.

Two surprises make this a lesson rather than a footnote. The first is that Redis's out-of-the-box answer is not to evict at all — it starts refusing to store anything new while continuing to answer normally, which is very hard to notice. The second is that the policy everyone reaches for, “delete whatever was used longest ago”, is the wrong one for a workload like yours, and this lesson makes the difference visible on real numbers.

maxmemory
The memory ceiling you set. Without one, Redis grows until the machine objects.
Hot set
The entries actually in use right now. The thing you are trying to protect.

In plain words

Google’s Memorystore and AWS ElastiCache ship with a policy called volatile-lru. It only deletes keys that were given an expiry time (a TTL). If none were, it deletes nothing and refuses new writes, just like the open-source default, while its name makes eviction look switched on. So the only safe answer is the one you have checked.

First: eviction is not automatic

The single most common production surprise here. In Redis open source the default maxmemory-policy is noeviction, which means that when memory runs out Redis does not make room — it starts returning errors on writes while continuing to serve reads normally.

Do not carry that default onto GCP. Managed Redis does not agree with open-source Redis here. Memorystore for Redis and AWS ElastiCache both default maxmemory-policy to volatile-lru, and Redis Enterprise defaults to volatile-lru for most databases (noeviction for Active-Active).

That matters more than it looks, because volatile-lru only ever evicts keys that have a TTL. If nothing in your keyspace has a TTL, volatile-lru behaves exactly like noeviction — writes start failing — except now the policy name on the dashboard says you configured eviction. This is the worst of the three states: broken, and looking correct.

The policies

Redis names policies as {scope}-{algorithm}. Scope is allkeys (anything is fair game) or volatile (only keys with a TTL). The algorithms:

From the Redis documentation on key eviction. Note lrm — it is new, and most tutorials predate it entirely.
AlgorithmEvictsRight when
lruLeast recently used — reads and writes both refresh the timestamp.A subset of keys is accessed far more than the rest. Redis's own docs call this the good default under a Pareto-ish distribution.
lfuLeast frequently used, via a decaying probabilistic counter.Popularity is stable over time and you want to resist one-off scans flushing your hot set.
lrmLeast recently modified — only writes refresh the timestamp.Redis 8.6+. Read-heavy workloads where you want to shed data nobody has updated, regardless of how often it is read.
randomAnything.All keys are equally likely to be needed — a repeating full cycle over the keyspace. Rarer than people think, and cheap when true.
ttlShortest remaining TTL first (volatile-ttl only).Your code can meaningfully predict which entries are disposable and encode that as a short TTL.

In plain words

The hot set is 200 keys read again and again. The sweep is 20 000 keys from a batch job, each used once, and five times bigger than the whole cache. When the cache is full, LRU (least recently used) throws out what was touched longest ago, so the hot keys go and 5 of 200 survive. LFU (least frequently used) throws out what is used least often, so the sweep goes and all 200 survive.

Now cover it and redraw

Sixty seconds, on paper or a whiteboard. You are not reproducing the picture; you are reproducing the argument, and the argument is four marks long.

  • Two populations drawn to scale — the sweep has to run off the edge of your paper, or the diagram says nothing.
  • The ceiling, with the hot set fitting inside it and the sweep obviously not.
  • The number each policy sorts on, written on its branch: last touch, hit count.
  • Both survivor counts. If you only keep one thing, keep that LRU landed on the same side as random.
If it took longer than a minute, you drew the picture. Draw the argument.

Why LRU is the wrong default here

LRU's weakness is that it is fooled by recency without frequency. Consider your evaluation platform running a batch job that embeds a 49-dataset benchmark — a single sweep across thousands of distinct texts, each touched exactly once.

Under allkeys-lru, every one of those one-shot embeddings is “recently used”, so they evict the genuinely hot entries that live interviews depend on. Your batch job flushes your interactive cache. Hit rate collapses for the path that has an 8-second budget, in service of a path that has no budget at all.

Under allkeys-lfu, those one-shot keys have a frequency counter of one. They are the first things evicted, and your hot interactive set survives the sweep. That is the whole argument, and it is a real operational difference rather than a preference.

Lab — fill it up and break it

Lab 0004 ~12 minutes

Exhaust Redis, then compare policies

  1. Confirm the deliberately hostile configuration the compose file ships. 32 MB and noeviction are chosen so you can hit the wall in seconds rather than hours.

    $ docker compose up -d redis
$ docker compose exec redis redis-cli config get maxmemory maxmemory-policy

  2. Fill it with plain SETs, one command each, and watch Redis start refusing them. Send them down a pipe so 400 000 round trips take seconds.

    You should see

    Hundreds of thousands of OOM command not allowed errors, and memory pinned exactly at the ceiling — 117 927 keys stored, 282 073 refused, used_memory_human:32.02M. Redis did not make room. It stopped accepting.

  3. Now break the ceiling on purpose, because maxmemory is not one. Flush, then do the identical 400 000 writes inside a single Lua script.

    $ docker compose exec redis redis-cli flushall
$ docker compose exec redis redis-cli eval \
    "for i=1,400000 do redis.call('set','junk:'..i,string.rep('x',200)) end return 'done'" 0
$ docker compose exec redis redis-cli info memory | grep used_memory_human
    You should see

    No error at all, all 400 000 keys written, and 116 MB of memory against a 32 MB maxmemory — 3.6× the limit you set. Nothing was evicted and nothing was refused.

    Then run the same script a second time, without flushing:

    $ docker compose exec redis redis-cli eval \
    "for i=1,10 do redis.call('set','more:'..i,string.rep('x',200)) end return 'done'" 0
    (error) OOM command not allowed when used memory > 'maxmemory'.
  script: 5874aa98..., on @user_script:1

    Refused, and exists more:1 returns 0 — not one key was written. Same script, opposite outcome. Underneath explains why, and it is the most useful thing in this lesson.

  4. Watch what your own service does in that state. Leave Redis full and hit the retrieval endpoint with a query you have never used.

    You should see

    A cache.set_failed warning both times, and 166 ms on both — the cache never populates, so every request pays full price forever. Your lesson 0003 wrapper is why the service still works at all. Without it this would be a 500.

  5. Turn on eviction and watch the difference immediately.

    $ docker compose exec redis redis-cli config set maxmemory-policy allkeys-lru
$ docker compose exec redis redis-cli set canary 1
$ docker compose exec redis redis-cli info stats | grep -E 'evicted_keys|keyspace'
    You should see

    OK on the write, and a non-zero evicted_keys. Redis made room by throwing something away. Those two counters — evicted_keys and the keyspace_hits/keyspace_misses pair — are the ones to put on a dashboard.

  6. Now the real comparison. Simulate the skewed-plus-sweep pattern: a small hot set queried constantly, interrupted by a one-shot batch sweep. Run it under each policy.

    You should see

    On Redis 8.10.1 this machine produced:

    PolicyHot keys residentevicted_keys
    allkeys-lru5 / 20016 953
    allkeys-lfu200 / 20016 969
    allkeys-random4 / 20016 975

    Include allkeys-random. It is the control, and it is the finding: LRU did not merely underperform LFU here, it landed on the same side as random. Every policy evicted about seventeen thousand keys to hold the same ceiling — the only thing that differed was which seventeen thousand.

  7. Last, run the same experiment under volatile-lru — the policy your managed Redis probably has right now. Run it twice: once with no TTLs anywhere, then once with a TTL on every key.

    # pass 1 — nothing has a TTL (SET, as before)
$ docker compose exec redis redis-cli flushall
$ docker compose exec redis redis-cli config set maxmemory-policy volatile-lru
$ docker compose exec redis redis-cli config resetstat
# ... warm the hot set and run the sweep exactly as in step 6 ...
$ docker compose exec redis redis-cli info stats | grep evicted_keys
    You should see, on pass 1

    Zero evictions, and 16 941 of the 20 000 sweep writes refused. The hot set “survived” 200 / 200 — but not because the policy protected it. It survived because Redis was refusing all new writes. volatile-lru with no TTLs in the keyspace is noeviction wearing a policy name that looks deliberate.

    Now pass 2: identical workload, but use setex ... 3600 instead of set for both the hot set and the sweep, so every key is an eviction candidate.

    evicted_keys:17036
hot keys resident: 4 / 200

    Now it evicts — and it has degenerated into plain LRU, destroying the hot set exactly as allkeys-lru did. So volatile-lru fails in both directions: silently frozen without TTLs, and recency-fooled with them. That is the default on Memorystore and ElastiCache, and it is the reason the first probe below is the one that matters.

Underneath

One layer down · why the thing you just configured is allowed to be this approximate

In plain words

Redis checks the memory limit once, when a command arrives, and a Lua script counts as one command. Once a script’s first write is let in, Redis will not stop it halfway, so it can run far past the limit: 32 MB became 116 MB in the lab. Leave real room between the limit and the container’s memory, or the operating system kills Redis instead of evicting anything.

maxmemory is an admission check, not a ceiling

The name promises a wall. It is not one. Redis tests memory when it admits a command, and a Lua script is one command. The scripting documentation states the rule exactly: a command that could enlarge memory is refused “only if this is the first write in the context of this script, otherwise we can't stop.” It cannot stop, because stopping halfway would leave a partially applied script, and a script is supposed to be atomic. Redis chooses atomicity over the memory limit.

Which is measurable in three runs, all against maxmemory 32mb and noeviction:

A. flushed first, then EVAL 400 000 SETs → done, used_memory 116.20 MB (3.6×) B. same script, memory already over → OOM ... on @user_script:1, zero keys written C. DEL one key, then SET, still at 116 MB → the SET lands

A is the first write being admitted and then never re-checked. B is the same check refusing the script at the door — which is why the error in the lab's step 2 is real, but belongs to the second invocation. C is the hole: the DEL is the first write, so the SET is not, and the check never applies to it (redis#8478).

The operational consequence is not academic. maxmemory has to be set with real headroom under the container's memory limit, because a single script, a MULTI block or one large value can carry you past it — and the thing enforcing the actual limit is then the kernel's OOM killer, which does not evict, it kills. Redis says so in its own FAQ: maxmemory is not the exact maximum amount of memory a Redis instance can use.

Why sampling five keys is close enough to true LRU

Redis keeps no LRU list. A true one costs a pair of pointers per object, which on small values is a large fraction of the thing being stored — you would spend the memory you were trying to save. Instead, each eviction round samples maxmemory-samples keys at random (default 5) and, since Redis 3.0, feeds them into a persistent pool of the best candidates seen so far, so good candidates survive between rounds.

The reason that works is that you do not need the least-recently-used key. You need any key from the cold tail, and cold keys are the overwhelming majority of a cache under pressure. If the hot fraction you must not touch is h, the chance that all N sampled keys are hot is:

P(evict something hot) = hN h = 0.20, N = 5 → 0.205 = 0.03 % h = 0.20, N = 10 → 0.2010 = 0.00001 % h = 0.50, N = 5 → 0.505 = 3.1 %

So the sampling error is governed by how skewed your traffic is, not by how big the keyspace is — which is why the same default works from 10 000 keys to 10 million, and why raising maxmemory-samples to 10 buys almost nothing once your hot set is genuinely small.

Why LFU cannot work without decay

An LFU counter that only ever goes up is not a measure of popularity, it is a record of all history — and a cache is a claim about the present. Without decay, a key that was hot last week outranks a key that is hot right now, forever, and the cache slowly ossifies into a museum of last month's traffic. That is what lfu-decay-time exists for: it halves idle counters every N minutes (0 means never, which is the setting that produces the museum).

And the counter is not a count. It is an 8-bit Morris counter — a probabilistic, logarithmic estimate living in 0–255, incremented with a probability that falls as the counter rises. lfu-log-factor sets how fast it saturates; at the default of 10 it takes on the order of a million accesses to reach 255. So two keys sitting at 200 and 205 are not five accesses apart, they are multiples apart. Reading those numbers as counts is the mistake that makes LFU tuning feel arbitrary.

Both policies are estimators, and maxmemory is an admission check. So the defensible form of “what is your eviction policy” is never a policy name on its own — it is which number the policy sorts on, how approximate that number is, and how much headroom sits under the real memory limit.

Check yourself

Redis hits maxmemory under the default policy. What does your service observe?

  1. Writes begin to fail while reads keep working normally
  2. Reads and writes alike fail until memory is released
  3. The oldest keys are dropped and nothing visibly changes
  4. Redis restarts itself and comes back with nothing stored

The default is noeviction: Redis refuses commands that would add data and serves reads as normal. This is the quiet failure mode — hit rate decays as the working set drifts while every health check stays green, because health checks read.

“Oldest keys dropped” describes what you probably assumed was happening, and it is only true once you have explicitly chosen an allkeys-* policy. Choosing it is the fix; the point of this lesson is that nobody chose it for you.

A nightly batch embeds 20,000 unique texts. Which policy protects your interactive hot set?

  1. LFU, because every swept key is used just once
  2. LRU, because the batch keys are the most recent
  3. Random, because it treats all the keys the same
  4. TTL, because the batch keys expire before the rest

LFU. Every swept key has a frequency counter of one, making them the first candidates for eviction, so your hot interactive entries survive the sweep. Under LRU the swept keys are the most recently used and therefore the best protected — the batch job flushes the cache that your 8-second-budget path depends on.

The TTL answer is a genuinely good instinct pointing at the wrong mechanism: giving batch writes a short TTL and using volatile-ttl would also work, and it is more explicit. It costs memory per key for the expiry and it requires the batch path to know it is a batch path. Worth mentioning in an interview as the alternative.

Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

Not a summary to read. A spoken answer to “tell me about a caching decision you made.” Read it aloud once, then say it again with the page covered. If a sentence needs a second breath, cut it — you will lose it under pressure.

  1. Situation

    On the on-device RAG layer for the TV, I had a memory ceiling I could not raise by changing an instance type. And I have the same shape again now: a prefix cache in front of the model, holding a shared system prompt that every request needs.

  2. Mechanism

    When a cache fills, some rule decides what dies. Redis's default rule is not to decide — it starts refusing writes and keeps serving reads, so every health check stays green while the hit rate quietly decays.

    Once you do turn eviction on, the policy reads exactly one number per key and evicts the lowest. LRU's number is when the key was last touched. LFU's is how often.

  3. What I did

    I measured it rather than arguing. Two hundred hot keys, hit fifty times each, then a twenty-thousand-key batch sweep into a fixed ceiling. Under LRU, five of the two hundred hot keys survived. Under LFU, all two hundred. Random left four. On this workload LRU is not a choice, it is a coin flip.

    Our batch job touches each text exactly once, which is the worst possible input to a recency rule, so we moved to allkeys-lfu.

  4. What it cost

    LFU resists change as well as scans. A key that was hot last week keeps its counter until decay catches up, so a usage shift shows up late. That made lfu-decay-time a decision I had to make rather than inherit. The honest limit: if our access pattern churned faster, LRU’s forgetfulness would be the feature.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“We use LRU.” Almost nobody does, and it is the single easiest claim in this lesson to disprove in one command. If nobody ran config set, open-source Redis is on noeviction and evicting nothing at all. If it came from Memorystore or ElastiCache it is on volatile-lru — which evicts nothing either, unless something in that keyspace carries a TTL.

So say the true version, which is stronger anyway: “it depends who provisioned it, and I have checked mine — here is what config get maxmemory-policy returns.” Having checked is the signal. Assuming is the tell.

Probes to expect

  1. “What is your Redis eviction policy?” “I don't know, whatever the default is” is honest and is also two different answers: noeviction if someone ran the open-source image, volatile-lru if it came from Memorystore or ElastiCache. Both can be silently refusing writes right now — the second one while displaying a policy name that looks deliberate. Run the config get from step 1 against your real cluster before your next interview.
    Model answer — say yours first

    “It depends who provisioned it, and I have checked mine.” Open-source Redis ships noeviction, which refuses writes rather than evicting; Memorystore and ElastiCache ship volatile-lru, which evicts only keys with a TTL and therefore evicts nothing at all if nothing in the keyspace has one. Both of those are a frozen cache wearing different labels.

    Then give the number: on our workload I measured allkeys-lru keeping 5 of 200 hot keys through a batch sweep against 200 of 200 under allkeys-lfu, so we run LFU with lfu-decay-time set deliberately.

    Invites · “How much headroom is there between maxmemory and the pod's memory limit?” — because maxmemory is an admission check, not a wall, and one Lua script can carry you 3.6× past it.
  2. “Your batch job runs at 2 a.m. and interactive latency is bad at 8 a.m. Why?” Cache flushed by the sweep under LRU, then a cold-start morning. This is a favourite question because it requires connecting two subsystems that look unrelated on a dashboard.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The 2 a.m. sweep evicted the 8 a.m. hot set. Every key the batch touched looked freshly used to a recency policy, so it displaced the entries the interactive path depends on, and the morning starts cold.

    The fix is not more memory, it is the right sort key: LFU puts every once-touched batch key at a count of one and evicts those first. If you cannot change the policy, give batch writes a short TTL and run volatile-ttl, which is more explicit and costs memory per key for the expiry.

    Invites · “How would you have caught this from a dashboard?” — evicted_keys against the keyspace_hits/keyspace_misses ratio, correlated by hour. Neither alone shows it.
  3. “Is Redis's LRU a true LRU?” No — sampled approximation with a candidate pool, tunable via maxmemory-samples. Answering this correctly is a strong signal that you have read the docs rather than a blog post.
    Model answer — say yours first

    No, and it does not need to be. A true LRU list costs pointers per object, which on small values is a large slice of the thing you were trying to store. Redis samples maxmemory-samples keys per round — five by default — and keeps a pool of the best candidates across rounds.

    It works because you never need the coldest key, only a key from the cold tail. If the hot fraction is 20 %, the chance all five samples are hot is 0.2⁵, about 0.03 %. That is why the same default holds from ten thousand keys to ten million: the error depends on skew, not on size.

    Invites · “So when would you raise maxmemory-samples?” — when the hot fraction is large, near 50 %, where the error is 3 % rather than 0.03 %. On a sharply skewed cache it buys nothing but CPU.
  4. “On the TV, you had a hard memory ceiling. How did you decide what to keep resident?” Straight from your Samsung work, and now you can answer it in eviction-policy vocabulary rather than ad hoc terms.
    Model answer — say yours first

    At the time, by hand and by instinct — I kept the function definitions that got invoked most and let the long tail reload. What I could not do then was name it, and it was a frequency policy with a manual decay.

    Said properly now: a fixed ceiling with a heavily skewed access pattern is exactly the condition where recency and frequency diverge, because a handful of TV functions account for most invocations. Anything that walks the full function catalogue once — a sync, a reindex — is a sweep, and under a recency rule it evicts the handful that matter.

    Invites · “What would you measure to prove the policy was right?” — hot-set survival across a sweep, which is the one experiment in this lesson and takes ten minutes.
  5. “What is your prefix-cache hit rate, and what evicts a prefix?” The scaling question from your own prep notes, asked as an eviction question. A shared system prompt is the hottest possible key and the one you least want evicted — which is the case for frequency over recency, in the place where it costs real money.
    Model answer — say yours first

    A shared system prompt is the hottest possible key and the one you least want evicted, because a prefix miss is not a slower read — it is re-running prefill and paying for it in GPU time. So this is an eviction question with a line-item cost.

    What evicts a prefix is another tenant's traffic filling the same KV cache, and under a recency rule a burst of unrelated long prompts will do it. That is the case for frequency over recency in the place where it is most expensive, and it is also why routing has to be prefix-aware — round-robin across replicas throws the hit away at the load balancer.

    Invites · “So how do you route to keep the prefix warm?” — which is lesson 0009, and the honest answer today is that I do not yet, and that is the gap.
