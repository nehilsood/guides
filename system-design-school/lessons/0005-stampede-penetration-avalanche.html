Stampede, Penetration, Avalanche

Three named ways a cache turns from an accelerator into the cause of an outage. All three are invisible at low traffic, all three are trivial to reproduce deliberately, and one of them is the most likely explanation for any unexplained breach of your 8-second budget.

The win

You will cause a real stampede with 200 concurrent cold requests, watch one cache miss become 200 upstream calls, then fix it with single-flight and TTL jitter and see the upstream call count collapse to one.

Why you, specifically
“Built a custom OpenAI-compatible LLM gateway powering those interviews at ~2.1 s median time-to-first-word under a hard 8 s per-turn budget.”

A stampede is the single most likely mechanism by which that budget gets breached in production. It needs three ingredients you have: a cache, an expensive miss path, and concurrency. It does not show up in staging because staging has no concurrency, and it does not show up in a closed-loop load test because — as you now know from lesson 0002 — that tool throttles itself exactly when the server stalls.

That sentence is defended in full in chapter 0001 of Résumé Mastery — this lesson is where the mechanism underneath it gets built and broken.

Start here — the plain version

So far a cache has only helped. This lesson is about the three ways it turns around and becomes the reason you are down. All three are the same shape: the cache stops answering, and the full weight of your traffic arrives at whatever was hiding behind it — a database, or in your case a model you pay per token for.

They differ only in why the cache stopped answering. One popular entry expired and everybody rebuilt it at once. Or people are asking for things that do not exist, so there is nothing to cache. Or a great many entries expired at the same instant because they were all created at the same instant. The fixes are small — a lock, and a random number added to expiry times — and this lesson makes you cause each failure before fixing it, because the numbers are the point.

Stampede
One hot entry expires and every request that wanted it rebuilds it simultaneously. Also called a thundering herd or the dogpile effect — three names, one event.
Penetration
Requests for things that do not exist. Every one is a miss, so every one goes through to the source. The cache is present and contributing nothing.
Avalanche
A large number of entries expiring together, usually because they were written together with an identical TTL. A cliff rather than a slope.
Single-flight
The fix for a stampede: let exactly one request do the expensive work while the rest wait for its result. Also called request coalescing.
Double-check
The easily-forgotten half of single-flight — after you win the lock, look in the cache again, because someone may have filled it while you were waiting.
TTL jitter
The fix for an avalanche: instead of expiring everything at exactly 3600 seconds, use 3600 plus a small random offset. Expiries spread out on their own.
Negative caching
The fix for penetration: remember that something does not exist, briefly, so the second request for it is also a hit.
Stale-while-revalidate
Hand back the expired value immediately and refresh it in the background. Nobody ever waits; you accept a short window of slightly old data.
Probabilistic early expiration
Refresh an entry a little before it expires, at random, so the expiry never arrives while anyone is waiting. Published as XFetch.

The three failures

1. Stampede — many clients, one missing key

Also called thundering herd or dog-piling. A popular key expires. In the microsecond before anyone repopulates it, 200 concurrent requests all check the cache, all miss, and all proceed to the expensive miss path simultaneously. One logical cache miss becomes 200 upstream calls.

The upstream, which was comfortably serving 15 requests per second, now receives 200 at once. It queues, slows, or rejects. Every one of those 200 requests is now slow, and if the miss path has a retry policy, you have just built a retry storm on top of a stampede.

The fix: single-flight

Also called request coalescing, or leases in the Facebook memcache paper. The first request to miss acquires a short-lived lock on that key and proceeds. The other 199 wait briefly and then read the value the winner wrote. One miss, one upstream call, 200 satisfied requests.

2. Penetration — requests for keys that will never exist

Every request asks for something not in the cache and not in the store. Nothing populates, so every request goes through to the backend. A cache with a 0% hit rate is worse than no cache: you pay the lookup and get nothing.

This happens accidentally when clients request nonexistent identifiers, and maliciously when someone discovers they can bypass your entire cache layer by requesting random keys. The fix is to cache the negative result — store a tombstone with a short TTL so “this does not exist” is itself a cache hit. Where the keyspace is huge, a Bloom filter in front answers “definitely absent” without a lookup at all.

3. Avalanche — everything expires at once

You warm your cache at deploy time with a uniform TTL of one hour. One hour later, every single entry expires within the same second, and every subsequent request misses simultaneously. This is a stampede across your whole keyspace rather than a single key, and it recurs hourly, forever, until someone notices the sawtooth.

The fix is TTL jitter: instead of exactly 3600 seconds, use 3600 plus a random offset — say ±10%. Expiries spread out and the cliff becomes a gentle slope. It is a one-line change, and while the Amazon Builders' Library does warn that adding TTLs can make a thundering herd worse, jitter tends to get filed under retry advice rather than cache advice — which is why plenty of production caches still expire in lockstep.

A third fix worth knowing by name

Single-flight and jitter are what this lesson's lab builds, and between them they are enough. But there is a third mechanism, and naming it in an interview is cheap and lands well: probabilistic early expiration, published as XFetch in Vattani, Chierichetti and Lowenstein's Optimal Probabilistic Cache Stampede Prevention (PVLDB vol. 8, pp. 886–897, 2015).

The idea: store how long the value took to recompute (delta) alongside it, and on every read decide whether to refresh early:

refresh_now = -delta * beta * math.log(random.random()) >= ttl_remaining

Reads near expiry get progressively more likely to trigger a refresh, and expensive values (large delta) start refreshing sooner than cheap ones. beta defaults to 1.0. Nobody waits on a lock and no request ever sees a miss, because the value is replaced before it expires.

The tradeoff is that you are doing some recomputation you did not strictly need, and that the mechanism only fires when a key is being read — a key nobody touches still expires cold. So the honest ranking for your gateway is: single-flight because it bounds the damage absolutely, jitter because it is one line, and XFetch when a specific key is both hot and expensive enough that even one stampede-free miss is too slow. Your shared-prefix embeddings are exactly that shape.

The same family includes stale-while-revalidate: serve the expired value immediately and refresh in the background. It eliminates the stampede outright and costs you a window of staleness you have to be able to justify — fine for embeddings, not fine for a rubric score.

The distinctions are worth memorising as a set — interviewers ask about one and are pleased when you name all three.

Lab — cause a stampede, then stop it

Lab 0005 ~20 minutes

200 cold requests, one key

Needs: Docker Desktop running lesson 0003 cache in place

  1. Reset to a clean, sane Redis and start the gateway with a slow embed step, so the miss window is wide enough to drive a herd through.

    $ docker compose exec redis redis-cli flushall
$ docker compose exec redis redis-cli config set maxmemory-policy allkeys-lfu
$ EMBED_MS=600 uv run uvicorn app.main:app # terminal 1

  2. Fire 200 concurrent requests for the same query against a cold cache.

    $ seq 200 | xargs -P 200 -I{} curl -s -o /dev/null \
    -X POST 'localhost:8000/v1/retrieve?q=identical+query'
$ curl -s localhost:8000/v1/retrieve?q=identical+query -X POST | jq '.embed_calls'
      200
    The stampede, in one number

    embed_calls is about 200, not 1. Every request missed, and every request paid the full 600 ms. Your cache provided no protection whatsoever at exactly the moment protection mattered — and this is the normal, correct behaviour of naive cache-aside.

  3. Add single-flight to app/cache.py. Two layers: an in-process lock per key (free, covers one worker) and a Redis lock (covers every replica).

    app/cache.py — additions 
    import asyncio
import random

# One asyncio.Lock per key, created on demand. Covers concurrency inside
# this process — which on a single uvicorn worker is all of it.
self._locks: dict[str, asyncio.Lock] = {}

def _lock_for(self, key: str) -> asyncio.Lock:
    if key not in self._locks:
        self._locks[key] = asyncio.Lock()
    return self._locks[key]

def _jittered(self) -> int:
    """TTL with +/-10% noise, so a warmed cache does not expire as a cliff."""
    return int(self._ttl * random.uniform(0.9, 1.1))

async def get_or_compute(
    self, text: str, compute: Callable[[], Awaitable[tuple[float, ...]]]
) -> tuple[float, ...]:
    k = self.key(text)

    hit = await self._try_get(k)
    if hit is not None:
        self.hits += 1
        return hit

    # Single-flight. Only one coroutine per key gets past this line.
    async with self._lock_for(k):
        # Re-check inside the lock: while we waited, the winner very
        # likely populated it. Skipping this check is the classic bug —
        # it turns 200 parallel misses into 200 serial ones, which is
        # slower than the stampede you were fixing.
        hit = await self._try_get(k)
        if hit is not None:
            self.hits += 1
            return hit

        self.misses += 1
        value = await compute()
        await self._try_set(k, value, self._jittered())
        return value

    Extract _try_get and _try_set as the wrapped best-effort helpers you already wrote in lesson 0003 — the bodies do not change.

Here every query is legitimately new, which is itself the lesson: a cache does nothing for a workload with no repetition, and you should measure your repeat rate before assuming one will help.

The better fix, when you can afford it

Single-flight still makes 199 requests wait for the winner. Where a slightly stale answer is acceptable — and for embeddings it always is, since they never really change — serve-stale-while-revalidate is strictly better: keep the expired value, return it immediately, and refresh in the background. Nobody waits at all. Amazon's article calls this a soft TTL versus a hard TTL, and for anything on a hard latency budget it is usually the right shape.

Underneath

One layer down · why the fixes have the shape they do

Single-flight, jitter and probabilistic refresh all look like three unrelated tricks. They are three answers to the same question — how many of these clients should be allowed to do the work? — and each one gets its shape from an argument you can reconstruct rather than memorise.

Single-flight costs a second GET, and that is not optional

Run the penetration step and read the Redis counters, not the application's:

200 distinct never-seen queries → keyspace_hits: 0 → keyspace_misses: 400

Two lookups per query, not one, on every single miss: the check before taking the lock, and the re-check after getting it. That is the true cost of the pattern, and it is the correct trade — a second GET is tens of microseconds against a 600 ms recompute.

The reason the re-check cannot be skipped is that the lock does not communicate anything. It only serialises. A waiter that wakes up and computes has learned nothing from having waited, so 199 waiters each pay full price in turn: 200 × 600 ms = 120 seconds for a request that used to take 600 ms in parallel. Turning concurrency into a queue is a real and popular way to make a stampede worse, and the only thing preventing it is one if.

Why jitter is multiplicative, and why 10 % is a real decision

An avalanche happens because a batch of keys written together expires together. The fix is to make expiry times independent, and the useful way to think about it is that you are choosing how wide to smear the load. With a TTL of T and jitter of ±j, the keys written in one burst expire spread over 2jT. At T = 3600 s and j = 0.1 that is a 720-second window — the measured TTLs came back between 3266 and 3946, which is exactly that band.

So the sizing question is not “what is a sensible jitter” but: how many keys, and how much recompute can I afford per second?

expiries per second ≈ keys / (2 · j · T) 20 000 keys, T = 3600, j = 0.10 → 20000 / 720 ≈ 28 /s 20 000 keys, T = 3600, j = 0.01 → 20000 / 72 ≈ 278 /s

Jitter is multiplicative rather than additive for the same reason percentages usually are: it has to stay proportional across TTLs that differ by orders of magnitude. A flat ±60 s is generous on a 60-second TTL and meaningless on a day-long one.

Where XFetch's formula comes from

Jitter spreads expiry but still lets the value expire, so somebody always eats a cold miss. Optimal Probabilistic Cache Stampede Prevention (Vattani, Chierichetti, Lowenstein, VLDB 2015) removes that by refreshing early, at random, with a probability that rises as expiry approaches:

refresh_now if −Δ · β · ln(U) ≥ ttl_remaining U ~ Uniform(0,1)

Read it in pieces and it stops being magic. Δ is how long the recompute takes, so the whole term scales with the cost of being wrong — an expensive value starts trying to refresh earlier. −ln(U) is an exponential draw with mean 1, which is what makes the decision independent per request and therefore un-synchronised across clients. And β tunes eagerness: β = 1 is the paper's default, β > 1 refreshes sooner and does more redundant work, β < 1 hugs the deadline and risks the miss you were avoiding.

The consequence worth saying out loud: because the probability is driven by Δ, a slow-to-compute value protects itself harder. That is the property you want and the reason this beats a fixed “refresh at 90 % of TTL” rule, which re-synchronises every client onto the same instant and reinvents the avalanche.

Penetration is a different problem wearing the same clothes

A stampede is many clients wanting one real value. Penetration is clients asking for values that will never exist, so there is nothing to cache and no lock to hold — every request is a guaranteed miss straight through to the expensive thing behind. Single-flight does not help at all, because there is no single flight to share.

The two answers are caching the negative result (cheap, correct, and costs memory proportional to how many distinct bad keys someone can invent) or a Bloom filter in front (constant memory, no false negatives, and a false-positive rate you choose). Which is right depends entirely on whether the bad keys are bounded — and if an attacker picks them, they are not.

All three fixes are answers to “how many clients should do this work?” Single-flight says one, and pays a second GET. Jitter says a controlled number per second, and you size it from your key count. XFetch says one, early, chosen at random in proportion to how expensive the work is.

Check yourself

You add a per-key lock but forget to re-check the cache inside it. What happens under 200 concurrent misses?

  1. All 200 compute in turn, which is slower than before
  2. Only one computes, because the lock on its own suffices
  3. All 200 fail, because the lock will always time out
  4. Half of them compute, as the lock covers late arrivals

The lock serialises them, it does not eliminate them. Each of the 200 waits its turn, acquires the lock, sees no reason to re-check, and computes — so you have converted 200 parallel 600 ms calls into 200 sequential ones. The last request now waits two minutes. You have made it dramatically worse while appearing to add a fix.

The double-check inside the lock is the entire mechanism. This is a real bug that ships regularly, and it is why the comment in the lab code is three lines long.

Which failure does single-flight not help with at all?

  1. Penetration, where every single requested key is a distinct one
  2. Avalanche, where a warmed keyspace all expires at one moment
  3. Stampede, where one single popular hot key has just expired
  4. Modal latency, where the cold code path is much slower

Coalescing works by merging concurrent requests for the same key. If every key is different there is nothing to merge, so penetration passes straight through. It needs a different fix — negative caching, or a Bloom filter where the keyspace is large.

Avalanche is the interesting near-miss: single-flight genuinely does help there, because an avalanche is many simultaneous per-key stampedes, and coalescing each one caps the damage. It just does not solve it — you still take a synchronised wave of misses, which is why jitter is the actual fix and coalescing is the safety net underneath.

Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

A spoken answer to “a hot key expires and 500 requests arrive at once — walk me through it.” This is the single most likely caching question you will get. Read it aloud, then say it again with the page covered.

  1. Situation

    We had an 8-second per-turn budget and an embedding cache in front of a 600-millisecond retrieval hop. The cache made the median look great and hid a failure mode entirely.

  2. Mechanism

    A cache miss is not instant — it takes as long as computing the value. So there is a window, as wide as that computation, in which every arriving request also misses. Nothing is in the cache yet to stop them.

    One key expiring under load therefore turns into as many identical computations as you have concurrent clients. The cache does not fail; it just stops helping at the exact moment you need it.

  3. What I did

    I caused it deliberately: 200 concurrent requests for one cold key, and I got 200 embedding calls for one value. Then I put a lock per key in front of the miss path, so one caller computes and the rest wait. Same command afterwards: one call.

    And jittered the TTLs by ten percent, so a batch written together does not expire together and hand me the same problem across every key at once.

  4. What it cost

    Two things. Every miss now does two cache lookups instead of one: the check before the lock, and the re-check after it. I measured that as 400 misses for 200 queries. Cheap, and worth it.

    The other cost is a real risk: the waiters must re-check inside the lock. Skip that and all 199 compute anyway, one after another. Two hundred serial 600-millisecond calls is two minutes — I would have turned a stampede into a queue and made it strictly worse.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“We added a lock, so only one request recomputes.” That is the right instinct and an incomplete claim, and the follow-up is always the same: what do the other 199 do when they get the lock? If the answer is “compute”, the lock made things worse, and saying the sentence without the re-check invites exactly that question.

The other trap is offering “we just raise the TTL.” A longer TTL makes the stampede rarer and not one bit smaller, and it buys that by serving staler data. It is a way of hoping the problem happens on someone else's shift.

Probes to expect

  1. “A popular cache key expires and you get 500 concurrent requests. Walk me through what happens.” Name it as a stampede, describe the upstream amplification, then give single-flight and stale-while-revalidate as options with the tradeoff between them. Naming the failure first is what separates a confident answer from a groping one.
    Model answer — say yours first

    All 500 miss, because the value takes 600 ms to compute and there is nothing in the cache to stop them for that whole window. So you get 500 identical computations of one value, 500× the provider bill, and a latency spike on a path with a hard budget.

    I have actually run this: 200 concurrent requests for one cold key gave exactly 200 embedding calls. The fix is a lock per key so one computes — and critically the waiters re-check the cache after acquiring it, which took it to one call.

    Invites · “And if the process holding the lock dies?” — the lock needs a timeout longer than p99 of the compute, and the waiters need a decision: compute anyway, serve stale, or shed.
  2. “You added a lock per key. What is the bug?” They are checking for the double-check. If you also mention that in-process locks do not span replicas, you are answering the follow-up before it arrives.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The waiters have to re-check the cache after they get the lock. Without that, waiting taught them nothing: each one computes in turn, so 200 parallel misses become 200 serial ones. At 600 ms each that is two minutes, against 600 ms for the stampede I was fixing.

    The second bug is scope. An asyncio.Lock covers one process, so with four uvicorn workers you get four computations, not one — better than 200 and not what the diagram claims. Beyond one replica it has to be a Redis lock, and then it needs a timeout and a fencing token.

    Invites · “Is a naive Redis lock safe?” — no, and that is lesson 0030: a lock without a fencing token cannot stop a stalled holder from acting after its lease expired.
  3. “Your cache hit rate is 2%. Is the cache helping?” No — you are paying a round trip for almost nothing. Either the workload has no repetition, or you are being penetrated. Measure the repeat rate before defending the cache.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Almost certainly not, and I would want to know which of two very different problems it is. If the keys are unique per request — a user's raw query text, say — then there is nothing to hit and the cache is pure added latency plus a Redis to operate.

    If the keys should repeat but do not, the key is wrong: too much in it, unnormalised text, or something per-request like a timestamp or a request id. That is a bug with a two-minute fix. And 2 % with high cardinality is the penetration shape, where the answer is caching negatives or a Bloom filter rather than a better key.

    Invites · “So what hit rate would justify it?” — the break-even is where hit_rate × saving exceeds the added round trip on every miss, which for a 600 ms compute against a 1 ms GET is a very low bar.
  4. “Why does latency spike every hour on the hour?” Avalanche from uniform TTLs, probably set at warm-up. The tell is periodicity: a sawtooth on a fixed interval is almost always an expiry cliff.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Everything was written at the same time, so everything expires at the same time. A TTL of one hour set during a deploy or a warm-up loop means the entire keyspace has the same expiry instant, and on that instant every key is a miss simultaneously — an avalanche rather than a stampede.

    Jitter fixes it: ±10 % on a 3600-second TTL smears expiry across 720 seconds. I verified the spread — stored keys came back with TTLs between 3266 and 3946 rather than all reading 3600.

    Invites · “How much jitter is enough?” — size it: keys ÷ (2jT) is your recompute rate at the smear, so 20 000 keys at ±10 % of an hour is about 28 per second.
  5. “Would you rather serve a 5-minute-stale embedding or make the user wait 600 ms?” For an embedding, stale every time — the value is a pure function and does not meaningfully change. Knowing which cached values tolerate staleness is the judgement being tested.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Stale, easily, and for this data it is not even close. An embedding is a pure function of the text and the model version — a five-minute-old vector for unchanged text is not stale, it is correct. The model version is in the cache key, so the only thing that could invalidate it changes the key.

    Which is the general form of the answer: the question is not how old the value is, it is what could have changed underneath it. For a rubric score I would answer the other way, because someone can re-run an evaluation and the old number is then wrong rather than old.

    Invites · “So would you serve stale while refreshing?” — yes: stale-while-revalidate gives every caller a fast answer and lets one caller refresh, which is single-flight with the waiting removed.

Recall — from lessons 0002–0004

Why would your closed-loop load generator have failed to find this stampede?

Two reasons compounding. It warms the cache on its first pass and then measures the warm path, and its workers throttle themselves the moment the upstream stalls — so the 200-way amplification never happens and the slow window is barely sampled. Reproducing a stampede requires genuine concurrency against a cold cache, which is what xargs -P 200 gave you.

lesson 0002 · coordinated omission
You set allkeys-lfu in step 1. Why is that the right policy for this lab specifically?

Step 5 sweeps 200 unique keys through the cache, each used exactly once. Under LRU those would evict the hot identical+query entry that steps 2 to 4 depend on, and you would be debugging your lock while your key quietly disappeared. Under LFU the one-shot keys go first. The lab is a miniature of the batch-job-flushes-cache problem.

lesson 0004 · LRU vs LFU under scans
A stampede sends 200 requests to an upstream sized for 15/sec. Which lesson-0002 idea explains why the damage exceeds 200 slow requests?

Queueing, and tail compounding. The upstream queues, so requests wait behind each other and each one's response time includes that queueing — not just its own service time. If any hop then retries, the retries arrive on top of the queue. This is exactly why lesson 0011's circuit breaker and lesson 0012's timeout budgets exist, and why a stampede is an availability problem rather than a latency inconvenience.

lesson 0002 · service time vs response time

This lab is the acceptance test for the whole format. If embed_calls goes from ~200 to 1 on your machine, the break-fix approach is working and I will keep building lessons this way. If it does not, tell me what you saw — that result matters more to me than any other number in Tier 1.
