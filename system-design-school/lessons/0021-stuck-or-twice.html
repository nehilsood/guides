Stuck or Twice

Kill a worker halfway through a job and the job can only be one of two things afterwards: lost, or done twice. Pause the worker instead of killing it and the choice gets starker. Postgres cannot tell a paused process from a busy one, and every mechanism that stops waiting for it — a lease, a heartbeat, a session timeout — did the job a second time. The only strategy that waited never duplicated anything, and it was stuck for as long as the pause lasted.

The win

Run a job queue on the Postgres you already have, and kill its workers with a real SIGKILL under six consumer strategies. Count lost jobs and doubled jobs for each. Pause a worker with SIGSTOP and watch the database fail to notice. Then get duplicates with no failure at all, from a lease and a slow tail, and predict how many before counting them. The fix that survives all of it puts the dedupe on the far side of the call.

Why you, specifically
“sayjini.com — Built a voice-only e-commerce experience on ONDC using LLMs, RAG, and function calling, enabling hands-free catalog search and checkout.”

The chapter that defends this bullet describes a network where nothing is a function call. One search goes to a gateway and sellers answer separately, each as its own callback, and the flow runs through fixed stages to confirm — the one step that cannot be taken back. Every arrow in that flow is a message handed to somebody else, and the question an interviewer asks about any of them is the same: “what if the thing handling it dies halfway?”

Lesson 0014 answered that for a client retrying a request. This lesson answers it for the worker that does the confirm, on the far side of a queue. That worker dies between placing the order and recording that it did. Or it pauses, a lease runs out, and a second worker places the same order while the first one is still alive. Both of those happened here, on purpose, and the counts are below.

The bullet is defended in full in chapter 0018 of Résumé Mastery. This lesson is the delivery semantics underneath its last paragraph.

Start here — the plain version

A queue holds jobs until a worker takes one. The worker does the work and then tells the queue it is done, and the queue forgets the job. Two steps: do it, and say so. A worker can die between them, and whichever order it does them in decides what dying costs.

Say done first and die, and the job is lost. Do it first and die, and the job is still in the queue, so another worker does it again. There is no order that avoids both. What you can choose is which failure you accept, and then whether the thing the work was done to can recognise a repeat.

Effectively-once
At-least-once delivery, and an effect that is safe to receive twice. What an observer sees, never what the wire does.
Lease
A job taken for a fixed time, after which anyone may take it. SQS calls it the visibility timeout; Pub/Sub, the ack deadline.
Heartbeat
A worker extending its own lease while it works, so a slow job is not taken from it.
Dedupe key
What the receiver of an effect refuses to apply twice. The producer's idempotency key from lesson 0014, not the queue's job id.
SIGKILL, SIGSTOP
Two ways to stop a process with no warning. Kill ends it, and its connections close. Stop freezes it, and its connections stay open.

There is no third order

The Kafka documentation puts the whole subject in two sentences, and they apply to every queue ever built. A consumer can “read the messages, then save its position in the log, and finally process the messages,” which “corresponds to ‘at-most-once’ semantics”; or it can “read the messages, process the messages, and finally save its position,” which is at-least-once. Saving the position is acknowledging. There is no third ordering of two steps.

So exactly-once is not a choice of order. It is at-least-once plus a receiver that refuses a repeat, or it is an effect that commits in the same transaction as the acknowledgement, which only works when the effect is in the same database. Everything else on this page is those two sentences, measured, plus one complication they do not mention: a worker does not have to die for its job to be redelivered. It only has to be slow.

In plain words

This is the picture to draw. A worker claims a job, the order is placed on the seller’s side, and only then does the worker acknowledge, telling the queue the job is done. A crash before the order loses the job if it was acknowledged first; a crash after the order and before the acknowledgement does it twice. Only two fixes reached zero doubles: the seller refusing a key it has seen, or an effect committed together with the acknowledgement.

Now cover it and redraw

Sixty seconds. Twelve marks, the standing ceiling. The top is a timing diagram with two crash points and the bottom is five lines, so get the order of the three boxes right and the rest follows.

  • Two lanes, and claim, order placed, ack in that order, the middle one on the lower lane.
  • The two crosses: before the order, lost; after it, twice.
  • The pause bar: the lock waits, everything else doubles.
  • The three fixes and which one is wrong: own table, seller's key, same transaction.
Put the order on the seller's lane, not the worker's. If it sits on the worker's lane it looks like part of the worker's transaction, and the whole point is that it is not.

Lab — kill the workers, count the jobs

Lab 0021 ~25 minutes

Six ways to consume one queue, a real SIGKILL, and a pause nobody notices

  1. Start Postgres, fill a queue with 800 jobs, and drain it with four worker processes while one of them is killed every few hundred milliseconds. Three strategies: acknowledge first, hold the row lock through the work, or take a one-second lease.

    800 jobs, 4 worker processes, one SIGKILL every 0.15-0.45 s, 12 kills, work 40 ms, lease 1.0 s
strategy        lost  applied twice  redelivered   waited, p50
ack-first          7              0            0             -
hold-lock          0              6           11         47 ms
lease              0              5           11      1,008 ms
    The same twelve kills, and each strategy chose which way to fail

    Acknowledging first lost 7 jobs in 12 kills and never repeated one. The other two lost nothing and repeated 6 and 5. Each kill lands somewhere in a 40 ms job: before the order is placed, or after it and before the delete. That is the only thing that varies, and it decides which column the kill shows up in.

  2. Now the three fixes, under the same kills: the worker's own “already done?” table, a key the seller refuses to apply twice, and an effect written in the same transaction as the delete.

    800 jobs, 4 worker processes, one SIGKILL every 0.15-0.45 s, 12 kills, work 40 ms, lease 1.0 s
strategy        lost  applied twice  redelivered   waited, p50
dedupe-local       0              4           10      1,015 ms
keyed              0              0           11      1,016 ms
same-tx            0              0           10         42 ms
    Before / after · the check everyone writes first changed nothing

    dedupe-local checks processed_jobs before placing the order and writes to it after. It applied 4 twice here, and 2 to 9 across six runs, which is no better than having no check. The crash that causes a duplicate lands between the order and the record, so it is exactly the crash that stops the record being written. The next worker checks, finds nothing, and places the order again.

    keyed sends the job's key with the order, and the seller's unique index refuses the second one: zero twice, with 11 redeliveries. The duplicates still arrive; they just stop mattering. same-tx writes the effect in the worker's own transaction, so it commits with the delete or not at all: zero twice with no key, and the fast recovery of the lock. That is Kafka's recipe, “letting the consumer store its offset in the same place as its output”, and it only exists when the output is here.

  3. Now do not kill the worker. Freeze it with SIGSTOP partway into its first job, leave it frozen for five seconds, and let it go. Five ways to consume, and a second worker ready to take over.

    two workers, 20 jobs of 160 ms; worker A is stopped with SIGSTOP partway into its first job and resumed 5 s later

                                   first applied  applied   what worker A said on resuming
hold-lock                            after 5.3 s        1   nothing
hold-lock, idle-in-tx timeout 2 s    after 2.1 s        2   ack failed: InterfaceError, connection is closed
lease 2 s                            after 2.1 s        2   ack refused: job 1 had been leased again
lease 2 s + heartbeat                after 2.2 s        2   ack refused: job 1 had been leased again
keyed, lease 2 s + heartbeat         after 2.1 s        1   ack refused: job 1 had been leased again
    Stuck, or twice — and nothing on the server could tell which it was

    Holding the lock applied the job once and left it waiting 5.3 s. A frozen process's sockets stay open, the kernel keeps answering for it, and Postgres sees a session that is idle in a transaction — the same thing it sees when a worker is waiting on a slow model call. The server's keepalive would not help either. tcp_keepalives_idle is 0 here, which the documentation says “selects the operating system’s default” — 7 200 s in this container — and a keepalive is answered by the operating system rather than the process, so a frozen one still answers.

    Every way of not waiting did the job twice. The session timeout from lesson 0019 killed A's transaction at two seconds, B placed the order, and A woke up and placed it again on a connection nobody had killed. The lease ran out and did the same. The heartbeat changed nothing, because a frozen process sends none. In all three, A's acknowledgement was refused and its order was not. Only the keyed seller got to once.

  4. No kills and no pauses. Four hundred jobs whose work times follow a lognormal with a 100 ms median, against four lease lengths, and then the shortest lease again with a heartbeat. The bench prints its own prediction first.

    400 jobs, 8 consumers, no crashes; work lognormal, median 100 ms, sigma 0.6

lease                  P(work > lease)  predicted  applied twice
150 ms                          25.0%        100            108
300 ms                           3.4%         13             13
600 ms                           0.1%          1              1
1200 ms                          0.0%          0              0
150 ms + heartbeat              25.0%          0              0
    A duplicate needs no failure, only a tail longer than the lease

    Nothing went wrong in this run and 108 of 400 jobs were done twice. Every job slower than its lease was handed to a second worker while the first was still working, and both finished. The prediction column is just the lognormal's tail beyond the lease, times 400. At 300 and 600 ms it lands on the prediction, within one, in every run. At 150 ms the count ran a little above it, 107 to 116 in five runs, and one plausible reason is that a redelivered job can outlive its lease a second time.

    So a lease is a bet on your p99, and the bet is written down as a duplicate rate. The heartbeat takes it to zero for a slow worker. Step 3 is the case it cannot cover.

  5. The other end of the queue. Three hundred confirms, and one enqueue response in ten is lost after the enqueue committed, so the producer sends it again. Consume with no key, with the receiver keyed on the job id, and with the producer's own key on the enqueue.

    300 confirms to enqueue; the response to one enqueue in ten is lost after it committed, and the producer retries it

                                         delivered  applied
no key anywhere                                342      342
receiver keyed on the job id                   342      342
producer's key on the enqueue                  300      300
    A retried enqueue is a new job, so the job id is the wrong key

    342 orders for 300 intents, and keying the receiver on the job id did not remove one of them, because each retry is a different row with a different id. The key has to identify the intent, and only the producer knows it. With it on the enqueue — ON CONFLICT (key) DO NOTHING, lesson 0014's insert-first — the retry lands on the same row and there are 300.

Underneath

One layer down · why exactly-once is a property of the receiver

A crash between two steps has chosen one of them

Every strategy, against the two places a kill can land in one job:

strategy killed before the effect killed after it, before the ack measured, 12 kills ack-first lost done once 7-10 lost hold-lock redone, fine done twice 2-6 twice lease redone, fine done twice 3-7 twice dedupe-local redone, fine done twice: no record yet 2-9 twice keyed redone, fine refused by the seller 0 same-tx rolled back with the ack rolled back with the ack 0

The middle two columns are the whole argument. No strategy has two good cells unless the effect and the acknowledgement are one operation — either because they share a transaction, or because the receiver makes the second effect a no-op. Everything else only chooses which bad cell it lives in.

In plain words

A lease lends a job to a worker for a fixed time, then lets another worker take it. A frozen worker looks just like one still working, so the queue either waits, possibly forever, or hands the job on and both may finish it. A heartbeat, the worker renewing its own lease, covers a slow worker but not a frozen one, which sends nothing.

Slow and stopped look the same from outside, so a lease is a bet on your tail

With work times lognormal at median m and spread σ, the share of jobs slower than a lease L is a normal tail:

P(work > L) = 1 - Φ( ln(L / m) / σ ) m = 100 ms, σ = 0.6, 400 jobs lease ln(L/m)/σ P(work > L) predicted measured 150 ms 0.68 25.0 % 100 107-116 300 ms 1.83 3.4 % 13 12-13 600 ms 2.99 0.1 % 1 1 1200 ms 4.14 0.0 % 0 0

So you can compute a duplicate rate from a latency distribution before running anything, and lesson 0002 already told you where the tail comes from. A lease set from the median doubles a quarter of the work; one set from the p99 doubles one job in a hundred. The heartbeat removes slowness from the bet entirely and leaves one case: a process that has stopped and will start again.

That case is not exotic. A garbage-collection pause, a laptop lid, a container being throttled, a node whose network drops for ten seconds and comes back — each looks, from Postgres and from the queue, exactly like a worker still thinking. Nothing outside the process can distinguish “slow” from “stopped and about to resume”, and so every queue must either wait indefinitely for the one it cannot tell about, or give the job to someone else and accept that both may finish. That is the title, and it is the same impossibility as lesson 0014's lost response, seen from the worker's side. Lesson 0032 adds the fencing token that lets the receiver tell which of the two was first.

In plain words

A dedupe is a record that says “already done”. Written by the worker after the order, it misses the crash in between, and the next worker orders again. It works only when the same operation writes the record and the effect, like the seller’s unique key. And the key must name the customer’s intent, which only the producer knows, not the queue’s job id.

The dedupe has to live where the effect lands

Where each record is written, and whether a crash can separate it from the effect:

record written by separable from the effect? twice none - - 2-7 worker's processed_jobs, after the worker, later yes: crash in between 2-9 seller's unique key, with the order the same insert no 0 the effect itself, in the ack's txn the same commit no 0 job id as the key, producer retried the same insert no, but the key is wrong 42 of 300

The last row is the one people miss: the record was in the right place and the key named the wrong thing. A dedupe works when it is written by the effect and keyed on the intent, and each half fails without the other.

Exactly-once, as it is sold

The products that offer exactly-once are precise about it, and the precision is the lesson. Kafka's is exactly-once for Kafka-to-Kafka processing inside transactions. Pub/Sub's exactly-once delivery means no redelivery after a successful acknowledgement, and the page lists what it does not cover: it applies “within a cloud region”, only to pull subscriptions, and “a subscription might receive multiple copies of the same message due to publish side duplicates, even with exactly-once delivery enabled.” Kafka's own documentation warns that “many systems claim to provide ‘exactly-once’ delivery semantics, but it is important to read the fine print.”

Read that fine print against step 3 and step 5 and it is the same pair of holes every time: a worker that does the effect and then cannot acknowledge, and a producer whose retry is a new message. No transport closes either, because both happen outside it. Which is why the interview answer is not the name of a product feature. It is where the key is minted, where it is checked, and what the receiver does with a key it has already seen.

Which lands on the sentence worth keeping: a queue can promise you at-least-once and a lease length; exactly-once is something the receiver does, with a key the producer minted. Everything between them only chooses whether a stopped worker leaves a job stuck or does it twice.

Check yourself

A worker checks its own processed_jobs table before placing an order, and writes the job there after the order is placed. Under twelve kills it still placed 2 to 9 orders twice. Why?

  1. The kill lands after the order and before the record
  2. The check reads a stale snapshot of the processed table
  3. The lease runs out before the check has fully completed
  4. Two workers both insert the same processed row at once

A crash between two writes in two systems separates them, and the duplicate-causing crash is precisely the one between the order and the record. The next worker checks, finds no record, and places the order again. The check is correct about everything it can see; it simply cannot see an effect whose record was never written.

None of the distractors is the mechanism. The check reads committed data, the lease is a full second against 40 ms of work, and a duplicate insert into processed_jobs would conflict harmlessly. The fix is to move the record to where the effect lands, which is what keyed does.

A worker holding a job's row lock is frozen with SIGSTOP for five seconds. Nothing else is configured. What does Postgres do with the lock?

  1. Keeps it, because the session still looks alive
  2. Releases it once the TCP keepalive probes fail
  3. Releases it when the deadlock detector runs next
  4. Hands the job to another waiting worker instead

It keeps it. A stopped process keeps its sockets open and the kernel keeps acknowledging on its behalf, so the session is simply idle in a transaction, which is also what a worker waiting on a slow model call looks like. In the lab the job waited 5.3 s for a five-second pause and was applied once.

Keepalives would not help even if they were configured, because the operating system answers them on a frozen process’s behalf. The deadlock detector looks for cycles of waiting sessions, and this is one session holding a lock and one skipping past it. SKIP LOCKED means nobody waits on it at all — the job just sits there.

Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

A spoken answer to “you have a queue of confirm jobs. How do you make sure each order is placed exactly once?” Read it aloud, then say it again with the page covered.

  1. Situation

    A worker takes a confirm job, places the order with the seller, and acknowledges the job. It can die between those two steps.

  2. Mechanism

    Acknowledge first and a crash loses the order. Acknowledge last and a crash places it twice. There is no third order.

    And a worker does not have to die. A slow one loses its lease, and a paused one looks alive to everything.

  3. What I did

    I measured it with real process kills. Acknowledging first lost 7 to 10 in 12 kills; acknowledging last doubled 2 to 7.

    The fix is a key minted with the intent, sent with the order, and refused by the seller the second time. Zero doubled, under kills and pauses.

  4. What it cost

    It needs the receiver's cooperation, and the key has to name the intent. Keyed on the job id, a retried enqueue still doubled 42 of 300.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“We use a queue with exactly-once delivery, so each job runs once.” The follow-up is “what happens if the worker dies after calling the seller and before acknowledging?”, and no delivery guarantee answers it, because the job was delivered exactly once and done twice. The version that holds: “delivery is at-least-once; the confirm is effectively-once because the seller refuses a key it has already seen.”

The second trap is offering the worker's own dedupe table. It is the first fix anyone writes, it is in this lab, and it doubled as many jobs as having no check at all. If you propose it, say where the record is written relative to the effect before somebody asks.

Probes to expect

  1. “At-most-once or at-least-once for the confirm step — which, and why?” The opener. The weak answer picks one; the strong one says neither is enough for an irreversible step and says what goes on top.
    Model answer — say yours first

    At-least-once delivery, because a lost confirm is an order the customer said yes to and never got, and nobody notices until they complain. But at-least-once on its own places some orders twice: in my lab, 2 to 7 in 12 kills for a worker that acknowledges after the work.

    So on top of it, the confirm carries a key minted when the user says yes, and the seller side refuses a key it has seen. That turned the same kills into zero duplicates with eleven redeliveries. The redeliveries are the queue doing its job; the key is what makes them harmless.

    Invites · “What if the seller cannot dedupe?” — then choose at-most-once plus reconciliation, and say so.
  2. “Why not just check a processed table in the worker before doing the job?” A trap disguised as a simplification. It tests whether you know where the crash that causes duplicates lands.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Because the record is written after the effect, and the duplicate comes from a crash between the two. I measured it: the worker-side check doubled 2 to 9 jobs in 12 kills, no better than no check. The next worker checks, sees no record, and places the order again.

    A record only helps if the same operation writes it and the effect. That is either the receiver's unique key, or — if the effect is a row in my own database — the effect and the acknowledgement in one transaction, which also got zero.

    Invites · “Then why keep it?” — you do not; it protects against a redelivery a table queue cannot produce.
  3. “How long should the visibility timeout be?” Sounds like configuration. It is a question about whether you know a lease produces duplicates on its own.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Longer than the tail of the work, and it is computable. The duplicate rate is about the probability a job outlives its lease. With work at a 100 ms median I predicted 100 duplicates in 400 for a 150 ms lease and measured 107 to 116 over five runs, with no failures at all; at 600 ms it was one.

    So I would set it from the stage's p99.9, add a heartbeat so a slow worker keeps its job — that took 150 ms to zero — and keep the receiver's key anyway, because a heartbeat cannot cover a paused process.

    Invites · “Why not a very long lease?” — a crashed job then waits that long; 1 s versus under 50 ms here.
  4. “Why not hold the row lock for the whole job? A crash releases it.” Sounds like the Postgres-native answer, and half of it is right. The other half is the lesson's title.
    Model answer — say yours first

    It does release on a crash, and fast — under 50 ms to redelivery against a second for a lease. It still doubles, 2 to 6 in 12 kills, because the crash that matters is after the effect and before the commit.

    Its real failure is a worker that stops without dying. I froze one with SIGSTOP: Postgres kept the lock and the job waited 5.3 s for a five-second pause, and would have waited for an hour. Add a session timeout and it becomes a lease, and doubled. It also holds a transaction open for the whole of the work, which is lesson 0019's horizon on the busiest table.

    Invites · “So which do you use?” — a lease with a heartbeat, and the key on the receiver.
  5. “Pub/Sub has exactly-once delivery now. Doesn't that solve it?” A product question with a semantics answer. The test is whether you have read what the feature promises.
    Model answer — say yours first

    It solves redelivery after a successful acknowledgement, within one region, on pull subscriptions. It does not solve a subscriber that places the order and dies before acknowledging, and the documentation says publish-side retries still arrive as “redeliveries with different message IDs”.

    I measured that second one: a producer retrying lost enqueue responses made 342 jobs for 300 intents, and a receiver keyed on the job id removed none of them. The producer's own key fixed it. So I would still key the confirm, and treat the feature as fewer redeliveries rather than a guarantee.

    Invites · “Then why use a broker at all?” — lesson 0022 argues you may not need one yet.
