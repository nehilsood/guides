Resilience Patterns Card

Six controls, what each one actually bounds, and the threshold you have to be able to defend for it. Every number here was measured in a lesson; nothing on this sheet is introduced here.

Which lever?

The question an interviewer is really asking is never “do you know what a circuit breaker is”. It is whose problem does this solve, and what does it cost the people it does not help. Six rows, and the last column is the one most answers leave out.

ControlProtectsBoundsCosts you
Retry budgetThe upstreamTotal retry countRetries you wanted, during a real blip
Circuit breakerYouLatency spent on a dead dependencyCalls that would have worked

The division that unlocks the rest: a breaker and a retry budget protect the upstream from you. Admission control and shedding protect you from your callers. A deadline protects the request from all of them. Answers that blur these sound fluent and fall apart on the second question.

Retry arithmetic

Three shapes, and only one of them is the famous number. Serial stages add; nested layers multiply. The exponent is the nesting depth of retry logic, not the number of stages.

shape                        predicted   measured
serial, halts on failure             3          3
serial, every stage flaky           15         15
nested, retry at each layer        243        243
                                   ^^^
      3**5 — five layers deep, not five stages wide.
      The nesting you did not write is the SDK's: provider
      SDK inside your wrapper inside your own loop is 27x.
  • Attempt count is set by the tail, and at the tail it is one. nT + (n−1)·base ≤ slice. For a 1 600 ms slice at 200 ms base: three attempts need T ≤ 433 ms, two need T ≤ 750 ms. Measured stage latency was p50 387, p90 670, p99 1 042 — so three attempts fit at the median, two at p90, one at p99. The requests slow enough to want a retry are the ones with no room for it. Those get a hedge or a fallback.
  • The retry window is the quantity nobody writes down: the total wall clock the attempts span. Full jitter averages backoff/2, so it halves the window — which against a fixed-duration outage means giving up early. Measured against a 4 s outage: no jitter completed 49 of 49 with a 5 000 ms window; full jitter completed 3 of 49 with 2 507 ms. Jitter decides arrival shape; the window decides outage survival.
  • Only a budget bounds the count. A token bucket at ~10 % of request rate, per dependency and shared across call sites — not per call site, because the amplification comes from the number of call sites.

Thresholds you have to defend

For each of these the interview question is “where did that number come from?” and “it is the default” is a losing answer. One line each, saying what it is derived from.

A breaker is a failure detector, not a latency detector, unless timeouts are recorded as failures. Measured: a dependency that answered every call correctly and late held the failure rate at 0 %, the breaker never moved, and 100 requests blew the stage budget. Counting the timeout as a failure turned that into 25 over-budget and 99 refused. And the estimator is the component that matters, not the three states: on identical traffic, a sliding window opened after 25 failures and an EWMA at α=0.1 after 7.

What to shed first

Ordering does not change how many you serve — capacity already decided that. It changes only which ones. Four policies at the same offered load served 247, 234, 246 and 247 of 576. Under FIFO, interactive requests got 132 of 311; under a priority floor, 230 of 311, with retries dropping from 67 to 3 of 161.

  1. Health checks, always. Fail these and the load balancer removes you, which in a brownout is the last thing you want.
  2. Whatever lets work already begun finish. end() before start(), page four before page one — otherwise clients cannot complete what they started and you get a brownout anyway.
  3. Interactive before batch. A late token in a live session is worthless; a batch job does not care.
  4. Retries last. That one is an argument rather than a citation: a retry is the only request whose caller has already been told to come back and has a budget for doing so. The weakness is that the signal is self-reported, which is why this classification belongs at the edge.

Price admission in tokens, not requests. Measured: one tenant sending 17 % of requests consumed 65 % of the capacity, because prefill is roughly linear in prompt length. Per-tenant buckets took the quiet tenant from 395 to 480 of 480 served and her p50 from 498 ms to 88. Sizing trap: the burst must exceed your largest single request, or that request is refused forever rather than throttled.

And check the right thing on dequeue. “Has the deadline passed?” discarded 0 of 576 — a request with 166 ms left is not expired and still cannot finish a 380 ms call. fits(estimate) discarded 92 and served 30 more.

Idempotency checklist

Exactly-once does not exist: a sender cannot tell a lost request from a lost response, so it retries, so the receiver must deduplicate. Effectively-once is dedupe plus a repeatable apply.

DecisionThe answerWhy it is not the obvious one
KeyClient-minted UUID, before the first attemptNot a payload hash — two identical charges are two charges.
ScopeTenant + operation, never globalA key minted for cancel must not satisfy confirm.
FingerprintHash of the request, stored beside the key; sort the fields firstSame key, different body is a client bug — reject it, never replay.
LifetimeLonger than any client's retry windowA correctness parameter. No index catches a key that is already gone.
OrderInsert first, catch the violationRead-then-write leaves a window. The database is the only atomic check.
Statesabsent / in_progress / succeededWithout the middle one the loser cannot tell “done” from “being done”.

The measurement to remember: sixteen concurrent duplicates through read-then-write with no constraint applied 16 of 16, with zero errors raised. Adding the unique index made it correct and returned 15 user-visible 500s on legal retries. Inverting the two statements gave 1 apply and 0 errors — same index, same fifteen collisions, different code expecting them. It breaks from two concurrent duplicates, not “under load”.

The order to reach for them

  1. Deadline first. Nothing else composes, and every other control needs to know how much time is left.
  2. Then a retry budget rather than a breaker, unless you specifically need to fail at 0 ms so a fallback gets the remaining deadline, or to cover first attempts rather than only retries. The Builders' Library recommends the bucket instead of a breaker, and being able to say why is worth more than having built one.
  3. Then admission control, sized from Little's law rather than by feel. It will not raise your goodput — measured 44 % → 43 — and it cut p99 from 7 998 ms to 1 711.
  4. Then idempotency, because everything above increases the number of duplicate and partially-completed requests in flight. A deadline that cuts a turn off mid-pipeline manufactures partial failures at a known rate: 26 turns in 200.
