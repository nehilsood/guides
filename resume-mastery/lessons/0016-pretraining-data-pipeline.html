A pretraining data pipeline at 150 GB

Three cheap filters and one expensive join, and the order they run in is most of the design. The word that will be tested is “clean,” because the only proof that data is clean is a model trained on it.

The win

Name the three stages, say why they run in that order, and explain why MinHash and union-find are one algorithm rather than two — then get ahead of the two questions that follow: what came out the other end, and what removed the passwords?

The line being defended
“Built a 3-stage preprocessing pipeline — fineWeb/C4-style quality filters, MinHash deduplication, repetition removal — distilling 150 GB of Confluence exports into clean LLM pretraining data.”
Samsung Research · bullet 4 of 5

In plain words

The pipeline takes a huge dump of company wiki pages and keeps only the text worth teaching a model. It makes three cleaning passes, and the cheap pass that throws away the most goes first, so the slow pass has less to read. The slow pass finds near-copies two at a time. Those pairs must be grouped before anything is deleted, or you either delete every copy or keep whichever one happened to come first.

Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

For “walk me through the data pipeline.” Three stages is the easy part. The ordering argument and the union-find point are what make it sound built rather than read about.

  1. Situation

    A company wiki export, about 150 gigabytes. Most of it is navigation, templates and the same release note copied across spaces.

    The job was to get out of it the text that is actually worth training on.

  2. Mechanism

    Three passes. One judges each document on its own. One removes near-copies across documents. One removes documents that repeat themselves.

    The order is the design. Cheap and destructive first, so the expensive stage that compares everything to everything sees the smallest corpus.

  3. What I did

    The part I would defend is the dedup. MinHash gives you pairs of near-copies, and a pair is a fact, not a deletion decision.

    So the pairs go through union-find and you keep one document per group. Without that, deleting everything with a duplicate removes all of them, and keeping the first one seen depends on file order.

  4. What it cost

    Two things I would rather say first. The filters came from web-crawl work, and some of them are wrong on a wiki. C4 drops pages with curly braces, which here means code blocks.

    And nothing in these three stages removes a password. A credential in a code block is unique, well-formed and not repetitive, so every filter keeps it.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“I built a three-stage pipeline with fineWeb-style filters and MinHash dedup to clean 150 gigabytes.” That is the bullet read aloud, and it names two papers as though naming them were the work. The follow-up is “so what came out the other end,” and if you do not have the retention numbers the whole thing deflates. Lead with the ordering argument and the union-find point instead.

The other trap is the word distilling. Said out loud, on a résumé that also claims knowledge distillation, it sounds like a teacher-student setup. Say reduced, and if somebody asks, say you would change the wording.

Behavioural · the story for this bullet

For “tell me about a time you found a bug that nothing was failing on” or “a correctness problem you could easily have shipped.”

  1. Decision

    Whether near-duplicate detection could stop at pairs, which is what the algorithm produces, or had to go on to groups before anything was deleted.

  2. Situation

    The dedup stage produced candidate pairs and a deletion list. It ran, it finished, and the output looked reasonable. Nothing failed and no count was obviously wrong.

  3. Action

    I checked what happened to a chain. A resembles B and B resembles C. Deleting every document that appears in a pair removes all three. Keeping the first one seen makes the survivor depend on the order files were read in.

    So the pairs went into union-find. Group first, then keep one member per group under a stated rule rather than an arbitrary one.

  4. Result

    The same input produced the same corpus on every run. That is what makes it possible to attribute a later model change to anything at all.

  5. What it cost

    Grouping introduces chaining. A long chain of documents that each resemble the next collapses to one, even though the two ends share nothing. I have no clean fix for that beyond tightening the threshold, which trades one error for the other. The real gap is that I never measured either rate against a labelled sample.

Start here — the plain version

A company wiki gets exported. Most of it is not text you want a model to learn from. Navigation, headers, page templates, the same release note copied across forty spaces. The pipeline's job is to throw away the parts that are not worth learning and keep the parts that are.

It does that in three passes. One looks at each document and asks whether it is any good. One looks across documents and removes the near-copies. One looks inside a document and removes the parts that repeat. Each answers a different question, which is why they are three stages and not one.

Quality filter
A cheap per-document test. Is it long enough, does it read like prose, does it look like a navigation page rather than content.
Shingle
A short overlapping run of words from a document. Documents become sets of shingles so they can be compared as sets.
Jaccard similarity
Shared shingles divided by total distinct shingles. One means identical, zero means nothing in common.
MinHash
A short signature of a document. Two signatures agree at a position with probability equal to the Jaccard similarity, which is the whole trick.
Banding
Cutting signatures into bands and matching on whole bands. It is how you find candidate pairs without comparing every document to every other.
Union-find
A structure that turns a pile of “these two are the same” facts into groups. It is how pairs become clusters.
Repetition removal
Dropping documents that repeat themselves. Duplicate lines, one phrase dominating, the same paragraph three times.
Retention rate
How much survives each stage. The single most informative number about a pipeline, and the one the bullet does not give.
Domain-adaptive pretraining
Continuing to train an existing model on a specific corpus. What 150 GB is actually sized for, as against training from scratch.
Ablation
Building two versions of the corpus, training a small model on each, and measuring the difference. The only real test of a filter.

Why the order is the design

Three stages, and they are not interchangeable. The cheap stage that discards the most goes first. The expensive stage that compares documents to each other goes last enough to see the smallest possible corpus.

Quality filtering is a regex pass over one document at a time. It costs almost nothing per document and it removes more than anything else does. Everything downstream then runs over a much smaller corpus.

Deduplication is the expensive one. It has to compare documents against each other, which means shingling, hashing, and a join over candidate pairs. Every document you removed earlier is a document it never has to look at.

Repetition removal is per-document again, so it could run anywhere. Putting it after dedup just avoids spending it on documents that were about to be deleted anyway. That is the weakest of the three ordering arguments, and I would say so.

MinHash and union-find are one algorithm

MinHash tells you that two documents are near-copies. That is a fact about a pair. What you actually need is a decision about which documents to delete, and a pair is not a decision.

Say A and B are near-copies, and B and C are near-copies. Delete every document that has a duplicate and you delete all three. Keep the first one you see and the answer depends on the order the files arrived in.

Union-find is what closes the gap. Feed it every candidate pair, get back groups, keep one member of each group. Now the result is the same regardless of file order, which is the property that makes the pipeline reproducible.

The cost is chaining. A resembles B, B resembles C, and so on down a long line where the two ends share nothing. Connected components will collapse that whole chain to one document. It is a real failure and it is worth naming before someone else does.

Web filters on a company wiki

The C4 heuristics were designed for a web crawl. A corporate wiki is not a web crawl. Several of those filters remove exactly the content that made the corpus worth using in the first place.

C4 drops any page containing a curly brace, because on the open web that means JavaScript. On an engineering wiki it means a code block, and code blocks are some of the most valuable text in there.

The same applies to the rule about lines ending in terminal punctuation. That removes tables, API references and configuration samples. All of them are prose-poor and information-rich, which is not a combination web filters were built to respect.

What fineWeb actually contributed was the method rather than the filters. Each filter was kept because an ablation showed it helped a downstream eval. Borrowing the filter list without borrowing the ablation is taking the answer and leaving the reasoning.

The two questions the bullet invites

What came out of the other end, and what removed the secrets. Neither number is in the bullet, and both get asked. Having the first ready and volunteering the second is most of the difference.

The retention rate is the informative number. A pipeline that keeps ninety per cent barely ran, and one that keeps two per cent probably threw away something it should not have. Per-stage counts are the first thing to show.

The second question is worse, because none of the three stages addresses it. An internal wiki contains credentials in code blocks, customer names, and pages about individual people. A quality filter keeps those, because they are high quality by every measure it has.

So the honest version of “clean” is narrow. It means deduplicated and reasonably well-formed. It does not mean safe to train on, and the gap between those two is a fourth stage that is not in the bullet.

  1. What does “C4-style quality filters” actually mean?

    A specific published list, deliberately simple and mechanical. Keep only lines that end in terminal punctuation. Drop documents under a length threshold. Drop boilerplate such as cookie and javascript notices. Drop any document containing blocklisted words. What that buys is a defensible starting point costing nothing to implement, instead of inventing quality heuristics and arguing about them. They were written for open web crawl, where the enemy is spam and navigation furniture. A Confluence export is not the open web. Filters tuned for one distribution discard good documents in another, and the punctuation rule alone will eat code blocks and tables.

    Red flagSaying “standard quality filters” with no list. The rules are published and short, and knowing them is the difference between citing a paper and using one.

  2. What did FineWeb actually contribute?

    The method, more than the filters. For each candidate rule they trained small models with and without it, and kept only the ones that measurably improved downstream results. The ablations were published. What that buys is evidence a filter helps, rather than a plausible story that it should. Several widely copied heuristics do not survive that test. It is the standard the field moved to, and it is the standard your own filters should be held to. If a filter was never ablated, you know it removed data and you do not know what it bought.

    Red flagTreating it as a bigger C4. The contribution was the evidence, and copying the filters without the method misses the point entirely.

  3. Why MinHash rather than comparing documents?

    Because pairwise comparison is quadratic and impossible at this size. First each document becomes a set of shingles, which are overlapping windows of consecutive words, so that reordering a sentence cannot hide a copy. MinHash then replaces that huge set with a short signature. It is built so the chance two signatures agree in any position equals the Jaccard similarity of the original sets. Banding hashes slices of those signatures, and only documents landing in the same bucket for some band are ever compared. What that buys is a quadratic problem becoming roughly linear. Comparing every pair here is not slow, it is impossible.

    Red flagNaming MinHash without naming the reason. Somebody will ask what it is approximating, and the answer is Jaccard similarity.

  4. What do the band and row counts decide?

    The similarity threshold. More bands makes the filter more permissive and returns more candidates. Fewer makes it stricter and misses near matches. So choosing them is choosing what you are willing to call a duplicate. What that buys, done deliberately, is a threshold you can defend rather than one you inherited. It pairs with the shingle size, which is the other real knob. Short shingles match too readily and flag unrelated documents. Long ones miss small edits, and Confluence pages are very largely small edits of each other. Both are tuning decisions with a curve behind them, not defaults to take from an example.

    Red flagUsing the tutorial values and calling it tuned. The honest version names the threshold you were aiming at and admits how you picked it.

  5. Why run exact deduplication before near deduplication?

    Because exact is nearly free and near is expensive. Exact means hashing each document and grouping identical hashes. One pass, and it catches an enormous share of real duplication. A wiki export is full of those, since pages get copied wholesale and then edited. Near duplicates need the whole MinHash and locality-sensitive hashing machine, and cost orders of magnitude more. What running cheap first buys is the expensive pass operating on a much smaller corpus. It also changes the retention number, so say which pass a figure refers to. The word deduplication covers both, and the figures are very different.

    Red flagQuoting one deduplication number. Two passes remove different things, and a combined figure hides which one did the work.

  6. What does the 150 GB actually count?

    Say whether it is the raw export or the extracted text. A Confluence export is a dump of an internal wiki. Pages as HTML or XML, plus attachments, macros, inline comments and the full version history of every page. Those can easily dominate the byte total. So 150 gigabytes of export may contain far less prose than it sounds. What naming the source precisely buys is that every filter decision follows from the corpus being a wiki rather than the web. The number that means something for pretraining is tokens, and that is a different measurement again.

    Red flagLetting the byte count stand as the scale of the work. Tokens are the unit that matters, and converting is one more sentence.

  7. Where does this pipeline stop being embarrassingly parallel?

    At deduplication. The corpus is split into shards, each processed independently, and the results merged. Filtering is per document, so you shard and run everywhere, and throughput is proportional to cores. Deduplication is inherently global, because a document's duplicate may be in any other shard. That needs a shuffle. What the shuffle decides is the whole infrastructure question. It is why you reach for Spark or Ray rather than a process pool on one large machine. Repetition removal is per document again. So one of three stages sets the architecture, and it is not the one people plan for.

    Red flagDescribing the whole pipeline as parallel. One of the three stages is not, and it is the one that shapes the infrastructure.

  1. In two sentences, what does this pipeline do?

    It turns a company wiki export into text worth training on. Three passes. One judges each document, one removes near-copies across documents, and one removes documents that repeat themselves.

    Red flagCalling it cleaning. That word is doing a lot of work here, and the next question is usually about what it does not clean.

  2. Why do the stages run in that order?

    Cheapest and most destructive first. Quality filtering is one regex pass per document and it removes more than anything else. Deduplication has to compare documents against each other, so it should see the smallest corpus possible. Every document filtered earlier is one it never has to look at.

    Red flagSaying the order does not matter. It is the only design decision in the bullet that costs nothing to get right and a lot to get wrong.

  3. How does MinHash actually work?

    Cut each document into overlapping short runs of words. Hash that set many times and keep the minimum each time. Two signatures agree at a position with probability equal to the overlap between the two sets. So counting agreements estimates similarity without comparing the documents.

    Red flagDescribing it as a similarity hash and stopping. The property worth naming is that agreement probability equals set overlap, because that is why the estimate is unbiased.

  4. You have a pile of near-duplicate pairs. What now?

    Union-find, and this is the part people skip. A pair is a fact, not a decision. If A matches B and B matches C, deleting everything with a duplicate deletes all three. Keeping the first one seen makes the output depend on file order. Grouping first and keeping one per group fixes both.

    Red flagTreating the pairs as the output. Order-dependence is the bug this causes, and it is invisible until someone reruns the pipeline.

  5. What is repetition removal, and how is it different from dedup?

    It looks inside one document rather than across documents. Duplicate lines, one phrase dominating the text, the same paragraph appearing three times. A page can be unique in the corpus and still be mostly its own navigation bar repeated.

    Red flagConflating the two. They catch different things, and saying which is which is the cheapest way to show you built it rather than read about it.

  6. The filters came from web-crawl work. Does that transfer?

    Only partly, and the mismatches matter. C4 drops any page with a curly brace because that means JavaScript on the web. On an engineering wiki it means a code block. The rule about lines ending in punctuation removes tables and API references. Those are the highest-value pages in the corpus.

    Red flagNaming fineWeb and C4 as a credential. The interesting answer is which of their rules are wrong here and why.

  7. 150 GB went in. What came out?

    That is the number I want ready, because it is the first thing anyone asks. Per-stage counts, not just a total. A pipeline keeping ninety per cent barely ran. One keeping two per cent probably deleted something it should not have. The shape of the drop is what tells you which stage is misconfigured.

    Red flagNot knowing. It is the single most informative number about the whole pipeline and its absence reads as not having looked.

  8. What is the riskiest claim in this bullet?

    “Distilling.” It collides with knowledge distillation, which is a completely different thing and appears on the same résumé. I would say reduced or filtered instead. The second risk is “pretraining data,” because 150 GB is sized for continued pretraining rather than training from scratch.

    Red flagDefending “distilling” as a figure of speech. On a résumé that also lists model optimisation, it reads as the technique.

  1. How do you set the near-duplicate threshold?

    Through the band layout. Cut the signature into more bands and the threshold drops, because a match in any one band makes a pair a candidate. That gives an S-shaped curve rather than a hard cutoff. You are choosing where the curve is steep, which is really a choice about which mistake you prefer.

    Red flagDescribing it as a similarity number you set. The number is a consequence of the band layout, and knowing that is the difference between using it and understanding it.

  2. Does shingle size matter?

    A lot. Short shingles make unrelated documents look similar, because everyday phrases collide. Long shingles miss real near-duplicates that differ by a word inserted every sentence. Five words is the usual starting point and it is a starting point rather than an answer.

    Red flagCalling it an implementation detail. It is the parameter that decides what the word duplicate means in this pipeline.

  3. Within a duplicate group, which document survives?

    Whichever rule you can defend, as long as it is a rule. The longest, the most recently edited, or the one in the most canonical space. What you must not do is take an arbitrary member, because then two runs over the same input give two different corpora.

    Red flagNot having a rule. The wiki has version history and space names, so a sensible rule is available and skipping it is a choice.

  4. What goes wrong with connected components on a big corpus?

    Chaining. A resembles B, B resembles C, on down a long line, and the two ends have nothing in common. Connected components collapses the whole chain into one group and keeps a single document. On a wiki full of page templates that chain can get long.

    Red flagNot knowing this happens. It is the standard criticism of the technique and the interviewer probably knows it.

  5. How would you run the grouping across several machines?

    The candidate pairs are the awkward part, not the documents. Pairs come out of a distributed join, and grouping them is a global operation. At this size the pair list usually fits on one machine, so collecting it and running union-find locally is the honest answer. Iterative label propagation is the version that scales past that.

    Red flagReaching for a distributed graph framework first. Checking whether the pair list fits in memory is the cheaper question and usually ends the discussion.

  6. How do you decide which quality filters to keep?

    By ablation, which is what fineWeb actually contributed. Build the corpus twice, once with a filter and once without, train a small model on each, and compare on a downstream eval. Borrowing a filter list without the ablation takes the answer and leaves the reasoning behind.

    Red flagJustifying filters by inspection. Reading a hundred documents tells you the filter fires, and it does not tell you whether the model got better.

  7. How do you know the output corpus is good?

    Three levels, and only the last is real. Per-stage counts tell you each stage did roughly what you expected. Reading a sample at each boundary catches the embarrassing mistakes. Training a small model on two variants is the only thing that answers the actual question, and it is the expensive one.

    Red flagStopping at counts. Counts prove the code ran, which is not the same claim as the data being better.

  8. Did this need a cluster?

    Probably not for stages one and three. Both are per-document passes, and the whole export streams through one machine in under an hour on a decent disk. Stage two has the genuine argument for distribution, because the candidate join is where the work is. Saying that is better than reaching for Spark by default.

    Red flagTreating 150 GB as big data. It is one disk. Knowing when not to distribute reads better than knowing how.

  9. The bullet says three stages. What is stage zero?

    Parsing, and it is usually where most of the real work went. A Confluence export is structured markup with macros, attachments, comment threads and per-page metadata. Getting text out of that without losing code blocks or keeping navigation chrome is fiddly, and nothing downstream can fix a bad job of it.

    Red flagSkipping it because it is not in the bullet. It is the stage that determines what the other three are even looking at.

  10. Is 150 GB a lot of pretraining data?

    No, and I would rather say so. Very roughly it is tens of billions of tokens before filtering and considerably fewer after. That is domain-adaptive pretraining, or continued pretraining on an existing model. Training from scratch wants two or three orders of magnitude more.

    Red flagLetting gigabytes stand in for tokens. Tokens are the unit that matters, and converting in your head is expected.

  11. Someone asks you to rerun it next month. What has to be true?

    Same input and same code gives the same output. That needs the union-find grouping, a deterministic survivor rule, and pinned hash seeds. Without those three, the corpus quietly differs between runs and you can no longer attribute a model change to anything.

    Red flagAssuming determinism. Two of the three stages have order-dependent implementations that look correct.

Why use MinHash rather than hashing each document and comparing hashes?

  1. Near-duplicates survive an exact hash match every time
  2. Exact hashing becomes impossibly slow at this scale
  3. Hash collisions occur more often than people expect
  4. Exact hashes cannot be computed across many machines

An exact hash finds exact copies. The problem on a wiki is the same release note in four spaces, a template filled in differently, a page with one paragraph added. All of those hash differently and all of them are the same text.

Exact hashing is also the faster option, which is why speed is the wrong reason to reject it. MinHash estimates set overlap instead of equality, and that is the property the problem needs.

Which stage should run first, and for what reason?

  1. Quality filtering, because it eliminates the most
  2. Deduplication, because it runs over everything twice
  3. Repetition removal, because it rewrites each document
  4. Tokenisation, because it dominates the elapsed time

Quality filtering is one regex pass per document. It costs almost nothing and it removes more than the other stages do, so everything downstream sees a much smaller corpus.

Deduplication is the stage that benefits most from that. It compares documents against each other, so every document filtered earlier is one it never has to shingle, sign or join.

  1. An API reference page is almost entirely repeated structure. Should it go?

    No, and this is where a web-tuned repetition filter is wrong. A parameter table repeats by design. It is also one of the more useful pages in the corpus. The fix is to measure repetition on prose and exclude structured blocks. Loosening the threshold until the filter stops firing is the lazy version.

    Red flagLoosening the threshold. That trades a precise fix for a blunt one and lets the real repetition back in.

  2. Four hundred pages share a template and differ in one field each.

    Near-duplicate detection groups them and keeps one, which is mostly right. But the differing field may be the only thing of value, and keeping one copy throws away three hundred and ninety-nine facts. The better move is to detect the shared boilerplate and strip it, then judge what is left.

    Red flagAccepting the group as a duplicate. The pages are duplicates by similarity and not by content, which is the case where the measure and the intent part ways.

  3. A page exists in v1, v2 and v3, all near-identical.

    Keep the newest and only the newest. This is the case where an arbitrary survivor is actively harmful, because two of the three are known to be out of date. The export carries modification dates, so the rule is available. Using it is the difference between deduplication and choosing correctly.

    Red flagKeeping the longest. Longest is a reasonable default in general and it is the wrong one when older versions are superseded.

  4. A forty-word page contains the one command everyone needs.

    A length filter removes it, and that is a real loss rather than a rounding error. Any threshold has this cost. The way to find out is to read the discarded set rather than only the kept one. Sampling what you threw away is the cheapest check in the pipeline.

    Red flagOnly inspecting what survived. The discarded pile is where the expensive mistakes are.

  5. Part of the wiki is not in English.

    Then a language filter is a decision and not a cleanup step. Dropping it loses real content from teams who wrote in their own language. Keeping it changes what the model is being trained for. Either is defensible and it should be stated as a choice, since it is not a quality question at all.

    Red flagFiling it under quality. It is a scope decision wearing a filter's clothes.

  6. A page is well-written, unique, and factually wrong.

    Every stage keeps it. Nothing in this pipeline measures truth, only form and novelty. That is a genuine limit and it is worth saying plainly, because a stale internal wiki is full of confident, well-formed, out-of-date instructions.

    Red flagSuggesting a model could judge correctness. On an internal corpus there is no ground truth to judge it against.

  7. Defend the word “distilling” in this bullet.

    I would not. I would change it. On a résumé that also claims knowledge distillation, the word reads as the technique rather than as a figure of speech. Reduced or filtered says the same thing and costs nothing, and the interviewer's question then goes somewhere useful.

    Red flagDefending it. This is the rare case where conceding the wording is strictly better than winning the point.

  8. What would you change?

    Add the fourth stage for secrets and personal data, because nothing here looks for either. Publish per-stage retention and a sample of what each stage discarded. And ablate at least the two filters I inherited from web-crawl work, since those are the ones most likely to be wrong on a wiki.

    Red flagProposing more filters. The gap is evidence and safety, not coverage.

  5. “What stops a secret ending up in the training data?” Volunteer this one rather than waiting for it. It is the gap the word clean hides.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Nothing in these three stages, and I would raise it before being asked. A credential in a code block is unique, well-formed and not repetitive, so every filter I have keeps it. The same goes for pages about individual employees. Clean here means deduplicated and reasonably well-formed, and it does not mean safe to train on. Closing that gap needs a fourth stage doing secret scanning and entity detection, and it is the first thing I would add.

The lessons behind this chapter

System Design School teaches these mechanisms from first principles and breaks them in a lab. This chapter defends what you did with them. An interviewer moves between those two registers without warning.

  2. SDS 0002 · p50 is a lie The mean document decides nothing here. Every filter threshold is a statement about a tail, and a single retention percentage hides both of them.
