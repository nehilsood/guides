The career narrative

This is the only question you are guaranteed to be asked, and the only one where a long answer is worse than a short one. Fifteen seconds, decided in advance, said the same way in every round.

The win

One sentence of fact, one of reason, one of forward intent — then stop. And the reason has to pass a hard test: it must be something this job cannot reproduce. Otherwise you have just explained why you will leave again.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“I was looking for more impact and a better growth opportunity.” It means nothing, it is available at every company on earth, and it therefore explains nothing about why you would stay at this one. Worse, it is the sentence everyone says, so it registers as an answer that was not prepared. Name the specific structural thing instead, and name the version of it that exists here.

The other trap is length. Thirty seconds on a seven-month role tells them it is a wound. Fifteen seconds and a stop tells them it is a fact you have already thought about, which is exactly the impression the whole answer exists to create.

The vocabulary — what each named thing is

Every other chapter uses this slot for technology. This one has none. The specialist vocabulary here belongs to the other side of the table, and it is worth knowing exactly, because these words decide outcomes.

Every entry answers four things. What it actually is, in plain words. Where it bears on this record. What it buys, meaning what knowing it changes. And the follow-up it produces once the word is on the table.

Regretted attrition
the number a hiring manager is judged onCompanies split departures into two kinds. Regretted attrition is the people they wanted to keep and lost. Unregretted is everybody else. Only the first is tracked as a problem, because replacing a good engineer costs a recruiting process, a notice period of empty seat, and months of ramp before the replacement is useful. Managers carry that number into their own reviews. In this record. It is the reason four employers in under three years is a question at all. What it buys. Knowing this reframes the whole conversation. The concern is a cost on their side, not a judgement about your character, and hearing it that way stops you answering defensively. The follow-up. Answer the cost, not the character. “Here is why the next one is different” speaks to their risk. “I am loyal” speaks to nothing they measure.
Ramp time
the cost being pricedThe period between a hire starting and the point where they produce more than they consume in other people's time. For a senior engineer it is commonly three to six months, during which they are consuming code review, onboarding, context and patience. In this record. Short tenures mean an employer paid the ramp and collected relatively little afterwards. What it buys. It makes the objection concrete and therefore answerable. An abstract worry about commitment has no reply. A worry about six months of unreturned investment does. The follow-up. Shorten it in the answer. Naming what you would need in week one, and what you could realistically ship in month one, converts their concern into a plan they can picture.
Notice period
and why it is long in IndiaThe contractual gap between handing in a resignation and actually leaving. In India it is frequently 60 or 90 days, against the two weeks that is customary in much of the world, and it is enforceable rather than a courtesy. In this record. It changes the arithmetic behind every date on the page, because a three-month gap between roles may be a notice period rather than a break. What it buys. Stating it early removes a scheduling surprise that can genuinely cost an offer. The follow-up. Say yours in the first conversation. A 90-day notice is a real constraint on a hiring timeline, and it is far better raised by you than discovered at the offer stage when a start date has already been promised to somebody.
Probation
the first monthsAn initial employment period, typically three to six months, during which the notice required is much shorter on both sides. It exists so that a mismatch can be ended quickly by either party, and ending inside it is an ordinary event rather than a failure. In this record. A departure inside probation reads quite differently from one after two years, and that cuts both ways. What it buys. A specific and unremarkable explanation for a short stint, where silence invites a much worse one. The follow-up. If a short role ended inside probation, say so plainly. Left to the dates alone, an interviewer will assume something larger happened, and the assumption is usually worse than the fact.
Counter-offer
the retention moveA raise or promotion offered by your current employer after you have resigned, specifically to stop you leaving. It is common enough that most recruiters plan for it, and the industry statistic they all quote is that most people who accept one leave anyway within a year. In this record. It is one of the standard causes of a short stay somewhere, and interviewers know it. What it buys. Understanding why the question is asked at all, which is not idle curiosity. The follow-up. Whether you would accept one. The honest answer is the useful answer, because a candidate who takes a counter-offer costs the hiring company an entire completed process, and that is precisely why they ask.
Background check
what gets verifiedA formal verification, run either by the employer or by a third-party firm, of the factual claims on your résumé. In practice that means employment dates, job titles, and in India frequently salary as well, confirmed against payroll records rather than against your account of them. In this record. Every date on the page is checkable, and dates are the most common quiet discrepancy. What it buys. Knowing what is checked tells you exactly where precision is mandatory and where description is fine. The follow-up. Make the dates on the page match the payslips exactly. Rounding a departure to the nearest quarter to tidy a gap is the kind of small thing that fails a check, for no benefit whatsoever.
Reference check
the other verificationA conversation with somebody who worked with you. Sometimes they are people you nominated, and sometimes they are found independently through a shared connection, which is increasingly easy. Unlike a background check this is about judgement rather than facts. In this record. A short stay raises the likelihood that somebody is asked why it was short. What it buys. It sets the standard your own account has to meet, which is consistency rather than polish. The follow-up. Your version and theirs should not surprise each other. That is a concrete reason to be plain about why a role ended, rather than diplomatic to the point where your account and the reality no longer obviously match.
Contract and full-time
the distinction to stateTwo genuinely different employment relationships that look identical in a list of dates. A fixed-term or agency engagement has an agreed end built into it from the first day. Permanent employment does not. In this record. A contract that ran its full length and ended on schedule is not a short tenure. It is a contract that finished. What it buys. One word on the page removes an entire line of questioning, at no cost to anything. The follow-up. Label it explicitly on the résumé. An unlabelled twelve-month contract reads to a stranger as a twelve-month resignation, and that is a misunderstanding you handed them for free.
Level and band
what the title does not tell themInside a company, roles sit at internal grades that determine scope, expectations and pay range. That grade is the real currency, and it is normally not public. Titles, by contrast, are set by convention and inflation, and vary enormously between employers, so “Senior” at one company may be two grades below “Senior” at another. In this record. Four employers means four title conventions with no shared scale between them. What it buys. It explains why your titles are not doing the work you think they are doing. The follow-up. Describe scope instead of title. What you owned, how many people depended on it, what you decided without asking. Scope compares across companies. “Senior” does not.
The trajectory
the thing they are reading forNot a formal term, but the thing an interviewer is actually assembling while they scan the dates. Four jobs pointing in one direction is a career with an argument behind it. Four jobs pointing in four directions is a pattern, and a pattern predicts the next move. In this record. It is the question underneath the question about job changes. What it buys. It is the one part of this conversation you can genuinely control, because the facts are fixed and the reading is not. The follow-up. State the direction yourself, in one sentence, before anybody asks. If the résumé does show an arc, name the arc, and each move stops being an event and becomes a step.

What is actually being asked

Not why you left. Whether this happens again. A senior hire takes months to become worth what it costs, so a seven-month tenure is a loss that somebody has to explain internally.

Reading it as a risk estimate rather than a judgement changes what a good answer has to do. It does not have to justify the past. It has to make a repeat look less likely than it did a minute ago.

That is why the forward-looking half matters more than the explanation. An explanation is about what already happened, which nobody can change. The forward half is the only part that speaks to the thing being priced.

And it is why length hurts. A long answer signals that the topic is uncomfortable, and discomfort is exactly the signal they are scanning for. Short and unbothered does more work than any wording.

Plain meaningThey are not asking you to justify yourself. They are working out whether this costs them again next year.

Three sentences, then stop

A fact, a reason, and what you are optimising for now. About fifteen seconds. Then be quiet and let them ask a follow-up if they want one, which they usually do not.

The fact is neutral and needs no adjectives. Seven months, and what the work was. Stating it plainly yourself is what makes the rest sound like information rather than defence.

The reason is the hard part and it has one rule. It must be about the work rather than about people, and it must be something this job does not reproduce. Test it before you use it.

The forward half names something concrete about this role. Not growth, not impact. A specific thing that this company has and the last one did not, which is why you are here rather than anywhere.

Plain meaningSay what happened, say why in a way that cannot happen here, say what you are after now. Then stop talking.

The test the reason has to pass

Say the reason out loud. Then ask whether it would also be true of the job you are interviewing for. If it would, you have just described the next departure.

“The work was too narrow” only works if this role is broad, and they know whether it is. “I wanted to own something end to end” only works if this role offers that, and it is a promise you are making as much as a reason.

Reasons that pass the test are usually structural. Something about scope, ownership or the kind of problem, which the new role visibly has and the old one visibly did not.

Reasons that fail are usually about people or about money. Both are heard the same way. If the manager was the problem, there will be a manager here too, and if the number was the problem, there will be a bigger number elsewhere.

Plain meaningWhatever you say drove you out, ask whether it exists here too. If it does, you have explained why you will go again.

The story these four roles actually tell

The moves are not the story. What changed across them is. On this résumé each step bought more ownership, and the titles moved with it, which is a trajectory rather than a pattern of leaving.

Research prototypes at Samsung. Then a shipped agentic system at Innovaccer with the guardrails around it. Then a platform owned end to end at CloudSufi, at a higher level than the role before it.

That is the frame. Not four employers, but prototype, then shipped feature, then owned system. It is true, it is checkable from the bullets, and it is the only version of the timeline that is a story rather than a list.

Two peer-reviewed publications sit across the same years, which says the output was continuous even while the employers were not. Nobody drifting produces that.

  1. Four companies since 2023. What is the interviewer actually worried about?

    A cost, not a character flaw. Companies split departures into two kinds. Regretted attrition is the people they wanted to keep and lost. Unregretted is everybody else. Only the first is tracked as a problem. Replacing a good engineer costs a recruiting process, an empty seat through a notice period, and months of ramp. Managers carry that number into their own reviews. So the worry is that they pay to hire you and you leave before it pays back. What knowing this buys is the right register. Answer the cost. Saying you are loyal addresses nothing they measure.

    Red flagHearing it as an accusation. It is a risk calculation, and treating it as one keeps the conversation useful instead of defensive.

  2. What does ramp time have to do with it?

    It is the thing being priced. Ramp time is the period between starting and the point where you produce more than you consume in other people's time. For a senior engineer it is commonly three to six months, spent consuming code review, onboarding and context. A twelve-month stay pays back some of that. A seven-month stay pays back very little. What naming it buys is an objection that can actually be answered, because an abstract worry about commitment has no reply. You can shorten it in the answer by saying what you would need in week one and what you could ship in month one.

    Red flagPromising to stay a long time. It is unfalsifiable and everybody says it. A concrete first-month plan is the version they can act on.

  3. When do you raise your notice period?

    In the first conversation, unprompted. A notice period is the contractual gap between resigning and leaving. In India it is frequently sixty or ninety days, against two weeks in much of the world, and it is enforceable rather than a courtesy. That makes it a real constraint on a hiring timeline. It also explains gaps between roles on your own page. What raising it early buys is that it stays scheduling. Discovered at offer stage it looks like something you were managing. There is no version where waiting helps you, and several where it costs you the role.

    Red flagWaiting to be asked. Recruiters plan around start dates, and a surprise at the end is the most expensive moment to deliver one.

  4. If your current employer counter-offered, would you take it?

    A counter-offer is a raise your current employer makes after you resign, specifically to stop you leaving. It is common enough that recruiters plan for it. The statistic they all quote is that most people who accept one leave anyway within a year. They are asking because a candidate who takes one costs them an entire completed process. What understanding that buys is knowing this is not idle curiosity. Answer directly. If money were the issue you would have raised money. If it is not, say what the issue is, and say a raise does not change it. Vagueness here reads exactly like the answer they fear.

    Red flag“I would consider it.” True of everyone and fatal here, because it tells them the offer they are about to make may be used as leverage.

  5. What does a background check actually verify?

    A formal verification of the factual claims on your résumé, run by the employer or a third-party firm. In practice that means employment dates, job titles, and in India frequently salary, confirmed against payroll records rather than your account of them. What knowing the scope buys is clarity about where precision is mandatory and where description is fine. Dates are where quiet discrepancies live. Make the months on the page match the payslips exactly. Rounding a departure to the nearest quarter tidies the résumé and fails a check, and the check happens after you have accepted.

    Red flagSmoothing a gap by stretching a date. It buys nothing, and it is the one category of problem that surfaces after the offer.

  6. One of these was fixed-term. Does that count as a short stay?

    No, and it is your job to say so on the page. A fixed-term or agency engagement has an agreed end built into it from the first day. Permanent employment does not. They look identical in a list of dates and they are different relationships. A contract that ran its full length and ended on schedule is a contract completing. What one label buys is an entire line of questioning removed, at no cost to anything. An unlabelled twelve-month entry reads to a stranger as a twelve-month resignation. That is a misunderstanding you handed them for free.

    Red flagExplaining it in the room. By then they have already read the dates and drawn a conclusion. Fix it on the page.

  7. Why is your title not useful evidence here?

    Because titles are not comparable across companies. Inside a company, roles sit at internal grades that determine scope, expectations and pay range. That grade is the real currency and it is normally not public. Titles are set by convention and inflation, so “Senior” at one company may be two grades below “Senior” at another. Four employers means four conventions with no shared scale. What knowing this buys is understanding why your titles are not doing the work you assumed. So describe scope instead. What you owned, who depended on it, what you decided without asking. Scope compares across companies. Senior does not.

    Red flagArguing that a title was under-levelled. It sounds like grievance, and the scope description makes the same point without the complaint.

  2. What shape should the answer have?

    A fact, a reason, and what you are optimising for now. About fifteen seconds. Then stop and let them follow up if they want to. The forward half is the part that speaks to what they are actually pricing, and it is the part most people leave out.

    Red flagFilling the silence afterwards. The pause feels long to you and normal to them, and everything said into it sounds like justification.

  3. “You were at Innovaccer for seven months.”

    Say the fact back plainly, give one reason about the work, and name what the move bought. On this résumé it bought a level and a platform to own, which is checkable from the titles. What you must not do is spend thirty seconds on it.

    Red flagAny sentence about the manager or the team. It is heard as a preview of how you will describe this place.

  4. “And you have only been at CloudSufi nine months.”

    This one is harder, because the answer cannot be about what went wrong. It has to be about what this role offers that the current one cannot, said without criticising a job you still hold. Anything negative about a current employer is the most expensive thing you can say.

    Red flagExplaining why the current role disappointed you. The one you are in is the one they listen to most carefully.

  5. “Walk me through your résumé.”

    Two minutes, chronological, one sentence per role, ending on what you are looking for. Do not pause on the transitions, because pausing marks them. The thread to carry is ownership. Prototypes, then a shipped system, then a platform owned end to end.

    Red flagReciting the bullets. They have read those. What they want is the connective tissue between the roles.

  6. Do you raise it or wait?

    Raise it, early, in the first few minutes. It converts a concern they would have dug up into one you have already handled. It also removes their reason to probe, because they have the answer without having had to ask for it.

    Red flagHoping it will not come up. The dates are on the page and somebody always adds them up.

  7. Why does it have to be the same sentence every time?

    Because interviewers compare notes, and two different reasons is a much bigger problem than one short role. A reason that shifts between rounds reads as an improvised one. Writing it down and saying it until it is boring is the entire technique.

    Red flagTailoring the reason per interviewer. The tailoring is what gets noticed, not the reason.

  8. What if a departure was not your decision?

    Then say so plainly and early, and the question mostly disappears. A layoff is a fact about a company rather than about you, and it is the one explanation that needs no defending. What damages people is hedging around it until it sounds like something else.

    Red flagDressing it up as a choice. It is checkable, it is common, and the vaguer version sounds worse than the real one.

  9. “Where do you see yourself in a few years?”

    The same question again, wearing a different hat. The answer that works names something that takes years and exists here. Depth in a domain, or ownership of something that grows. Anything achievable in a year is an answer that schedules your next departure.

    Red flagNaming a title. A title is portable and says nothing about staying anywhere in particular.

  10. So what is the sentence?

    That one is yours, and it has to be true. A rehearsed sentence you do not believe sounds exactly like one. The scaffold is fact, reason, forward. The reason has to pass the reproduction test. Write it, then say it aloud twenty times.

    Red flagImprovising it in the room. This is the one answer where improvisation is guaranteed to be worse than preparation.

  1. What if the honest reason was money?

    Then it needs a frame. Stated bare, it predicts the next move exactly. Compensation following scope is a different sentence from compensation for its own sake. If the move also came with more to own and a higher level, lead with that. It is true, and it is the part that does not recur here.

    Red flagMaking it the headline. Everyone understands it and nobody hires on it, because a larger number always exists somewhere.

  2. They push back on your answer.

    Repeat it, do not extend it. The temptation is to add detail, and detail is what makes it sound like a story being built. Saying the same thing again, calmly and without new material, is what a settled answer looks like.

    Red flagAdding a second reason under pressure. Two reasons read as neither being the real one.

  3. Does the answer change for a recruiter?

    No, and that is the point. The recruiter writes it down and it travels. A version tuned for a screening call becomes the version the hiring manager compares against later. That is how inconsistency gets manufactured out of nothing.

    Red flagTreating the screen as informal. It is the round where your words get transcribed most literally.

  4. What does the two-minute version contain?

    One sentence per role and one thread through them. Not the bullets, which they have read. Move through the transitions at the same speed as everything else, because slowing down at a short tenure is what marks it as a problem.

    Red flagSpending longer on the short roles. Time spent is read directly as discomfort.

  5. Which questions are this question in disguise?

    Where do you see yourself. What are you looking for. What would make you turn down an offer. Why us. All four are asking how long you stay, and answering them consistently matters as much as the direct version does.

    Red flagAnswering these from a different frame. They are the same question and a careful interviewer is checking the answers against each other.

  6. “What would have kept you at the last place?”

    A sharp question, because a vague answer here undoes the earlier one. The good version names something specific and structural, and it should be the same thing your reason implied. If it is something this company has, say so, because that is the whole point of naming it.

    Red flagSaying nothing would have. That says the decision was about leaving rather than about arriving somewhere.

  7. “You have breadth. Do you have depth?”

    The fair version of the concern, and the résumé answers it. The same thread runs through every role. Agents, retrieval, evaluation and the guardrails around them, across four employers and two publications. That is depth in a domain that happened to move between companies.

    Red flagListing technologies. The answer is that one problem area recurs, not that many tools appear.

  8. Is there a gap anywhere in these dates?

    No, and that is worth knowing, because it removes a whole category of question. The roles run continuously from January 2023, and the degree finished inside the first one. Saying that cleanly if asked is better than working it out live.

    Red flagBeing unsure about your own dates. Hesitating over the timeline makes a clean one sound unclean.

  9. What about references from a seven-month role?

    Expect the question and have a name ready. A short tenure with a willing reference is a much smaller concern than a short tenure with a shrug. Knowing who you would name, before being asked, is most of the work here.

    Red flagAvoiding the shortest role when references come up. The omission is the thing that gets noticed.

  10. Should you ask them the same question back?

    Yes, and it repositions the conversation. Ask how long people stay on this team, and what the last person who left went on to do. That is a normal question from somebody evaluating a decision rather than hoping for one. It also gives you the information you need.

    Red flagAsking it as a retort. Timing matters, and it belongs in your questions at the end rather than immediately after theirs.

  11. “What are you working on outside the job?”

    Name something concrete and current rather than an adjective. A curriculum you are actually working through beats any claim about being curious, because it is checkable and it has a shape. This workspace and its companion are both real answers to that question.

    Red flagAnswering with an intention. Everyone is planning to learn something, and nobody is asked to prove it.

  12. When is the question itself a signal about them?

    When it keeps coming back after a straight answer, or when it turns into a comment about loyalty. One careful pass is due diligence. Three is a place that will treat the tenure as leverage later, and noticing that is part of what you are there to do.

    Red flagAbsorbing repeated pressure as though it were fair. The interview goes both ways and this is one of the clearer signals available.

The reason you give for leaving has to satisfy one hard constraint. Which?

  1. It must be something this job cannot reproduce
  2. It must be entirely outside your own control
  3. It must involve nobody you worked with then
  4. It must be flattering about the previous employer

Say the reason out loud and ask whether it would also be true of the job you are interviewing for. If it would, you have described the next departure in your own words, and the interviewer will hear exactly that.

Keeping people out of it is a good rule too, and it is a rule about tone rather than the load-bearing constraint. Reasons outside your control are rarer than people pretend, and a structural reason about scope works fine.

When should a short tenure come up in the conversation?

  1. Before they do, in the opening minutes
  2. Only when they ask about it directly
  3. At the offer stage, with the recruiter
  4. In the written follow-up note sent afterwards

Raising it yourself converts a concern they would have dug up into one you have already handled. It also removes their reason to probe, because they have the answer without having had to ask for it.

Waiting means the topic arrives as a challenge rather than as information. And leaving it to the offer stage means it surfaces when the cost of a surprise is highest for both sides.

Edges and exceptions

The uncomfortable versions. Most of these have one correct answer and it is the plain one.

  • The real reason is bad
  • Still employed
  • An overlap in dates
  • They are hostile
  • A contract role
  • Two short roles ahead
  • Defending the pattern
  • What you would change

  1. What if the honest reason is one you would rather not give?

    Then find the part of it that is true, structural and sayable, and give that. Not a different reason, a narrower one. Inventing a reason is the failure mode, because an invented reason has no detail behind it and the second question finds the edge of it.

    Red flagConstructing a story. Every follow-up tests it, and the effort of maintaining it shows in the delivery.

  2. You are interviewing while employed. Does that change the tone?

    It helps, and it constrains you. A current job means you are choosing rather than needing, which is a good position. It also means nothing critical can be said about the place you are still at, because that is the most expensive sentence available.

    Red flagCriticising a current employer. It is heard as what you will say about them in a year, and with more force than any past-employer comment.

  3. Two roles appear to overlap in the dates.

    Explain it in one sentence and move on, because the explanation is almost always mundane. A notice period, a start date, a final year of study. What turns it into a problem is hesitation, since an unexplained overlap invites a much worse guess than the truth.

    Red flagHoping nobody reads the months. Somebody in the loop reads the months.

  4. The interviewer is openly sceptical about the job-hopping.

    Stay level and give the same answer. Matching their temperature is the error, and so is over-apologising. If it persists past a straight answer, that is information about how this place treats leverage, and you are allowed to use it.

    Red flagGetting defensive. The content of the answer matters far less than the fact that the answer did not rattle you.

  5. What if a role was always meant to be short?

    Then say so, and label it on the résumé. A fixed-term role that reads as a resignation is a self-inflicted problem. One word next to the dates removes a question that would otherwise take a minute.

    Red flagLeaving it ambiguous. The reader's default assumption is the unfavourable one.

  6. You have two offers and one is likely to be short again.

    Worth weighing honestly, because the next timeline is being written now. Another short role makes every future version of this conversation harder, and the compounding is real. That is an argument for staying somewhere long enough to own an outcome, not just a task.

    Red flagOptimising only the immediate move. The pattern is what gets priced, and each entry makes the next explanation more expensive.

  7. Defend the pattern.

    I would reframe it rather than defend it. Four employers is the surface. What actually happened is that each move bought more to own, and the titles moved with it. That is checkable from the résumé, and it is the only reading of the timeline that is a story.

    Red flagArguing that tenure does not matter. It does to them, and disputing the premise is a worse move than reframing it.

  8. What would you change?

    Write the sentence down and stop improvising it. Say it aloud until it is dull. And where the résumé can remove a question cheaply, label it. Every question you pre-empt is a minute spent on the work instead.

    Red flagTreating this as less worth preparing than the technical rounds. It is asked in every single one of them.

Probes to expect

  1. “Walk me through your résumé.” The opener in most loops. Set the frame here or somebody else sets it for you.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Lead with the thread rather than the timeline. LLM systems since the start of 2023, agents and retrieval and the evaluation around them, with two peer-reviewed papers across the same years. Then the roles in order, one sentence each, at an even pace. Research prototypes at Samsung, a shipped agentic debugger with layered validation at Innovaccer, and a platform owned end to end at CloudSufi one level up. Name the seven months yourself, in one sentence, and finish on what you are looking for now.

  2. “Seven months at Innovaccer. What happened?” The guaranteed question. Fifteen seconds, then stop and let the pause sit.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Three parts and no more. The fact, said plainly with no adjectives. A reason about the work rather than about people, which has to be one this job does not reproduce. Then what the move bought, which on this résumé is a higher level and a platform to own. Then stop. The silence afterwards feels long to you and normal to them, and anything said into it sounds like justification rather than information.

  3. “How do I know you will not leave in six months?” The question behind all the others, asked directly. Do not treat it as hostile.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Answer the thing being estimated rather than the thing being said. What makes a stay likely is something that takes years and exists here, so name it specifically. Then be honest about the pattern rather than disputing it, because disputing the premise is worse than reframing it. Each move bought more ownership, and the next thing I want is long enough to own an outcome rather than a task, which is not a promise about intent but a statement about what I am selecting for.

  4. “What are you looking for?” The same question wearing a friendlier hat. Consistency between this and the direct version is what gets checked.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Whatever you name here has to be the same thing your leaving reason implied, and it has to be something this company visibly has. Not growth and not impact, because those exist everywhere and therefore explain nothing about staying here. Something structural about scope, ownership or the kind of problem, and ideally something that takes more than a year to get, because an answer achievable in twelve months schedules your next departure out loud.

  5. “What are you working on outside work?” A softer question with a hard use. A concrete answer beats an adjective every time.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Name something that exists rather than something you intend. A curriculum you are actually working through has a shape and a syllabus and can be described in a sentence, which is more than any claim about being curious can do. It also answers the depth concern indirectly, because sustained study of one area is exactly what somebody worried about breadth is listening for.

Chapter 0017 drew a line between two words when describing what came of the teaching sessions. Name it, and apply it here.

Awareness rather than adoption. The rule was to claim only what you can point at, because the larger claim unravels on the first follow-up and the smaller one survives. The career answer has the same shape. Say what the move bought, which on this résumé is a level and a platform to own and is checkable from the titles. Do not claim a grander motive you cannot evidence, because every sentence in this answer is tested by the next question.
