The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“I've worked on quantization, pruning and distillation.” It flattens shipped, taught and never into one verb. The follow-up is always “which one, and on what,” and now you are retreating rather than disclosing. Say which is which first and the same list becomes credible.

The other trap is “quantization makes it four times faster.” It makes the weights four times smaller, and the speed-up follows from bandwidth rather than being a property of the arithmetic. Anyone who works on inference will hear the difference immediately.

Start here — the plain version

A model is a large pile of numbers. Running it on a phone or a television means that pile has to fit. The memory is already mostly spoken for. And the answer has to arrive before the person gives up.

There are three ways to make the pile smaller. Store each number in fewer bits. Throw away parts of the model. Or train a smaller model to imitate a bigger one. They are different operations with different costs, and confusing them is the most common way this line goes wrong.

Parameter
One number in the model. A billion-parameter model has a billion of them, and how many bytes each takes is the whole memory question.
Quantization
Storing each number in fewer bits. Sixteen-bit floats down to eight-bit or four-bit integers. The model is unchanged in shape and smaller in memory.
PTQ and QAT
Post-training quantization happens after training and is cheap. Quantization-aware training simulates the loss of precision during training, costs a training run, and holds accuracy better.
Outlier
A weight or activation far from the rest of its group. Outliers are why naive quantization hurts, because one extreme value stretches the scale for everything else.
KV cache
The stored keys and values for tokens already processed, so they are not recomputed. It grows with the conversation, and on a device it often costs more than the weights.
Layer pruning
Removing whole transformer blocks. Blunt, structured, and it actually speeds things up, unlike removing scattered individual weights.
Sparsity
Zeroing individual weights. It shrinks a model on paper and usually does not make it faster, because normal hardware still multiplies the zeros.
Knowledge distillation
Training a small student model on a large teacher's output distribution rather than on hard labels. The soft probabilities carry more information than the answer alone.
SLM
Small language model. Small enough to run where the data already is, which changes the privacy and latency story more than it changes the quality one.
Memory bandwidth
How fast weights can be moved to the compute units. Generation is usually limited by this rather than by arithmetic, which is why smaller weights are also faster.

The arithmetic you should be able to do out loud

Memory for weights is parameters times bytes per parameter. That is it. Say the numbers and the whole on-device conversation becomes concrete instead of vague.

A one-billion-parameter model at sixteen-bit precision is two bytes per parameter, so two gigabytes. At eight-bit it is one gigabyte. At four-bit it is half a gigabyte. Those are the numbers that decide whether something ships on a television.

Then add the part people forget. The KV cache is roughly two, for keys and values, times layers, times key-value heads, times head dimension, times bytes per element, per token. On a long conversation that can rival the weights, and unlike the weights it grows while the user talks.

The trap worth naming is using attention heads rather than key-value heads in that formula. With grouped-query attention those differ by a factor of four or more, and getting it wrong overstates the cache by the same factor.

Quantization, and what it actually buys

Smaller numbers mean less memory, and less memory to move means faster generation. The speed-up is a bandwidth effect, not an arithmetic one, and knowing that distinction is the question.

Generating a token means reading essentially every weight. Modern accelerators can do far more arithmetic than they can be fed, so generation is memory-bandwidth bound. Halving the bytes per weight roughly halves the data moved per token, and the throughput follows.

The cost is accuracy, and it is not spread evenly. Outliers are the reason: a single extreme value in a group stretches the quantization scale, so every ordinary value in that group loses resolution. That is why per-channel scales beat per-tensor ones, and why weight-only quantization is safer than quantizing activations too.

On device there is a second decision that matters more than people expect. Quantizing the KV cache is separate from quantizing the weights, and it is what buys context length rather than model size.

Pruning and distillation are not the same move

Pruning removes parts of a trained model. Distillation trains a new smaller model to imitate a big one. One is surgery, the other is teaching, and only one of them needs a training run.

Layer pruning drops whole transformer blocks. It works better than intuition suggests, because middle and later blocks often change the hidden state very little, so removing a few costs less than removing the same parameter count from everywhere. It is also structured, which is why it genuinely speeds things up.

Contrast that with unstructured sparsity, which zeroes scattered individual weights. The model is smaller on paper and usually no faster, because the hardware still multiplies by those zeros unless it has specific support.

Knowledge distillation is a different operation entirely. A large teacher produces a full probability distribution over tokens, and a small student is trained against that distribution rather than against the single correct answer. The soft targets carry information about what the teacher thought was nearly right, and that is the part that transfers.

What is actually behind each of the four

Two of these are shipped. One was presented to a division rather than deployed. One has nothing on the page at all, and there is a near-miss nearby that is easy to confuse it with.

Quantization is solid. The Samsung bullet says a quantized MobileLLM covering fifty-plus television functions. That is a shipped artefact with a real memory constraint behind it.

SLM deployment is the same evidence and equally solid. A small model running on a television is the definition.

Layer pruning is on the page, but as a technical session delivered across the engineering division rather than as something deployed. That is still a real claim and it is a different one. Presenting a technique to a division is evidence you understand it, not that you shipped it.

Knowledge distillation has nothing behind it. The nearby item is the three-stage preprocessing pipeline that distilled 150 GB of Confluence exports into pretraining data. That is data curation and it uses the same English word for a completely different operation. Letting those blur together is the single easiest way to get caught on this line.

  1. Walk me from FP32 down to INT4. What are they and what breaks?

    A model is billions of numbers, and this is how many bits each one gets. FP32 is four bytes a weight, the format models are usually trained in. FP16 and BF16 are two bytes. BF16 keeps FP32's full exponent range and gives up precision instead, which is why training is stabler in it. INT8 and INT4 drop to integers, with a shared scale factor per group of weights. What it buys is memory, directly. At FP32 a seven-billion-parameter model is 28 GB. At FP16 it is 14 GB, and at INT4 it is under 4 GB. That is a data-centre GPU against a laptop. What breaks first is outliers, because one extreme weight drags the scale for its whole group.

    Red flagSaying four-bit is four times faster. It is about four times smaller. Speed depends on whether you were bandwidth-bound, and often you were.

  2. What is GGUF?

    A file format, not a quantization method. It holds already-quantized weights together with everything needed to run them. The tokenizer, the architecture metadata, and the quantization levels used. Those run from Q2_K at the small and lossy end to Q8_0 at the large and faithful one. It also lets you split layers between CPU and GPU when the whole model will not fit. What it buys is one file that runs. No framework install, no separate config, no matching library versions. That is what made local models usable by people who are not ML engineers. Saying you quantized to GGUF is like saying you compressed to ZIP. The container is named. The setting is not.

    Red flagListing GGUF alongside GPTQ and AWQ as a third method. They answer different questions, and mixing them up is the tell.

  3. GPTQ, AWQ, bitsandbytes. When would you use each?

    GPTQ and AWQ are both post-training four-bit methods for serving. Both use a small calibration set to see which weights matter, and neither retrains the model. GPTQ works layer by layer, rounding weights while adjusting the rest to compensate for error already introduced. AWQ starts from the observation that a small fraction of weights matter disproportionately, finds them by activation magnitude, protects those, and quantizes the rest hard. What they buy is a fourfold size cut for minutes of calibration, with no training run and no labels. Bitsandbytes is the odd one out. It is for four-bit fine-tuning on a small GPU, not for deployment, and its inference speed is not competitive.

    Red flagNaming bitsandbytes as a serving format. It is the QLoRA library, and putting it in the deployment answer says you have read about this rather than done it.

  4. What is ONNX for, and where does it stop?

    A common file format describing a model as a graph of standard operations, plus a runtime that executes that graph. The point is decoupling. A model trained in PyTorch can run on a phone, in a browser or on an embedded board whose runtime has never heard of PyTorch. What it buys is one export and many targets, with no Python interpreter at the far end. It also unlocks hardware accelerators through the same interface. It stops at control flow. Export is clean for a fixed graph and awkward for anything that branches on data. An unsupported operator is a wall, not a slowdown, so it fails at export rather than degrading.

    Red flagTreating export as a formality. It is where on-device projects actually stall, and saying so shows you shipped one.

  5. What is paged attention, and what problem did it solve?

    Generating text requires keeping a cache of attention keys and values for every token so far. That is the KV cache, and it grows as the reply grows. Before vLLM it was one contiguous block per request, sized for the longest possible reply. You cannot know in advance how long an answer runs. So most of that reservation sat unused. Paged attention stores the cache in fixed-size blocks allocated on demand, exactly the way an operating system pages virtual memory. What it buys is far more concurrent requests on one GPU, because the memory reserved for replies that never got long is no longer wasted.

    Red flagDescribing vLLM as “faster inference.” It is a memory result that shows up as throughput, and the mechanism is the answer.

  6. What is LoRA, and what does QLoRA add?

    Full fine-tuning updates every weight. That needs memory for the model, its gradients and the optimiser state, usually several times the model size. LoRA freezes the original weights entirely and trains small low-rank matrices alongside them. Well under one percent of parameters are updated. QLoRA does the same while the frozen base sits in four bits, cutting memory again. What that buys is a training run that fits on one GPU, and an output measured in megabytes rather than tens of gigabytes. The adapter stays separate, which changes serving too. You can hold several against one base model and switch per request.

    Red flagCalling it cheaper full fine-tuning. It is a different thing with a rank you chose, and the rank is the knob an interviewer will ask about.

  7. You shrank a model. How do you know it still works?

    Perplexity first, because it is cheap. It measures how surprised the model is by text it has not seen, and lower is better. Informally it is how many equally likely options the model feels it is choosing between at each token. It needs no labels and no task, just held-out text. What that buys is a fast regression signal. If perplexity jumps, something is definitely broken, and you know before running a benchmark. Then a task benchmark, because perplexity can hold almost exactly while instruction following degrades. Quote both, with the sizes and the hardware. A compression claim without a quality number beside it is not a result.

    Red flagQuoting a size reduction alone. Anyone can make a model smaller. The claim is that it stayed useful, and that needs its own number.

  1. What is quantization, in one sentence?

    Storing each weight in fewer bits than it was trained in. Sixteen-bit floats become eight-bit or four-bit integers, with a scale factor to map them back. The model keeps its shape and its parameter count. Only the precision of each number changes.

    Red flagSaying it makes the model smaller without saying how. The mechanism is bits per number, and that is what every follow-up builds on.

  2. A one-billion-parameter model. How much memory for the weights?

    Parameters times bytes per parameter. At sixteen-bit that is two bytes each, so two gigabytes. At eight-bit, one gigabyte. At four-bit, half a gigabyte. Then add the KV cache separately, which is roughly two times layers times key-value heads times head dimension times bytes, per token.

    Red flagForgetting the cache. On a long conversation it can rival the weights, and unlike the weights it grows as the user keeps talking.

  3. Why does a smaller model generate faster?

    Because generation is memory-bandwidth bound rather than compute bound. Producing one token means reading essentially every weight, and the chip can do far more arithmetic than it can be fed. Halve the bytes per weight and you roughly halve the data moved per token.

    Red flagSaying the arithmetic is faster. On most hardware it is not the bottleneck, and naming bandwidth is what shows you have thought about it.

  4. Post-training quantization or quantization-aware training?

    Post-training first, because it is cheap and often good enough. It needs a small calibration set and no training run. Quantization-aware training simulates the precision loss during training so the model adapts to it. It holds accuracy better at low bit widths and costs a training run, so it is the answer when four-bit post-training is not acceptable.

    Red flagNaming the expensive one by default. The right answer is to try the cheap one and measure before paying for the other.

  5. What does quantization cost you?

    Accuracy, unevenly. Outliers are the reason: one extreme value in a group stretches the scale, so every ordinary value in that group loses resolution. That is why per-channel scales beat per-tensor ones. Weight-only quantization is safer than quantizing activations too, because activations have worse outliers.

    Red flagSaying it costs a little accuracy and stopping. Naming outliers is what separates having read about it from having done it.

  6. Layer pruning or unstructured sparsity?

    Layer pruning, on ordinary hardware. Removing whole blocks is structured, so the model genuinely runs faster. Unstructured sparsity zeroes scattered weights, which shrinks the model on paper while the hardware still multiplies by those zeros. Sparsity only pays off with specific hardware support.

    Red flagTreating parameter count as the goal. The goal is latency and memory, and one of those two techniques delivers both.

  7. What is knowledge distillation?

    Training a small student model against a large teacher's full output distribution instead of against hard labels. The soft probabilities say what the teacher thought was nearly right, and that extra signal is what transfers. It needs a training run and a teacher, which makes it much more expensive than quantization.

    Red flagDescribing it as training on the teacher's answers. The distribution is the point. Hard answers alone would be ordinary supervised learning.

  8. Why run a model on a device at all?

    Privacy and latency, in that order. The data never leaves the device, which for a television means what somebody says in their living room stays there. And there is no network round trip, so a short response is fast. It is not about quality, because the device model will be worse.

    Red flagLeading with cost. Inference cost matters at scale and it is rarely why a consumer device runs a model locally.

  9. What is the riskiest claim on this line?

    Knowledge distillation, because nothing supports it and there is a near-miss on the same résumé. The 150 GB preprocessing pipeline distilled Confluence exports into training data, which is data curation rather than model distillation. If those blur together in an answer, an interviewer will separate them for me.

    Red flagUsing the preprocessing work to cover it. Same word, completely different operation, and the conflation is exactly what gets caught.

  1. Why do per-channel scales beat per-tensor ones?

    Because one scale has to cover every value it is shared with. Per tensor, a single outlier anywhere stretches the scale for the whole tensor and everything else loses resolution. Per channel, the damage is contained to that channel. The cost is storing more scale factors, which is small next to the weights.

    Red flagSaying it is more accurate without the mechanism. The mechanism is that outliers only ruin the group they are in.

  2. Why is weight-only quantization safer than quantizing activations?

    Weights are fixed and can be inspected before deployment, so their distribution is known. Activations depend on the input and have much worse outliers, which vary run to run. Quantizing them risks a scale chosen from calibration data that does not cover what arrives in production.

    Red flagTreating them as the same operation. Static versus input-dependent is the whole difference in risk.

  3. When would you quantize the KV cache rather than the weights?

    When context length is the constraint rather than model size. Weight quantization is a fixed one-off saving. Cache quantization scales with conversation length, so on a device holding a long session it buys more. They are independent decisions and it is worth saying so, because people assume one implies the other.

    Red flagTreating quantization as a single switch. Weights and cache are separate, and they buy different things.

  4. Where does four-bit quantization hurt most?

    On anything needing precision rather than fluency. Arithmetic, exact recall of rare tokens, and long-context tasks where small errors compound. Conversational fluency survives four-bit surprisingly well, which is dangerous, because the model still sounds fine while getting specific things wrong.

    Red flagSaying accuracy drops uniformly. It does not, and the uneven damage is what makes evaluation after quantization necessary rather than optional.

  5. If you were pruning layers, how would you choose which?

    Measure how much each block changes the hidden state, by comparing its input and output representations. Blocks that barely move it are the cheapest to remove. In practice the middle and later blocks are usually the redundant ones, and the first few are not. The first blocks do the most structural work.

    Red flagProposing to prune uniformly. The whole finding that makes layer pruning viable is that redundancy is not evenly distributed.

  6. After removing blocks, what do you do?

    A short healing fine-tune, because the remaining blocks were trained expecting the ones that are gone. Without it the drop is much worse than the parameter reduction suggests. That is also why pruning is more expensive than quantization in practice, even though the removal itself is trivial.

    Red flagTreating pruning as free. Deletion is free and recovering the accuracy is not.

  7. Why does distillation use a temperature on the teacher's outputs?

    To soften the distribution so the student sees more than the top answer. A confident teacher puts almost all mass on one token, and the information worth transferring is the relative ordering of the rest. Raising temperature flattens it, exposing what the teacher thought was nearly right.

    Red flagConfusing it with sampling temperature at inference. Same word, different use, and knowing that distinction is the question.

  8. You need a model four times smaller. Which technique?

    Quantization first, because four-bit from sixteen-bit is exactly a factor of four and it costs no training run. Only if that fails accuracy would I look further, and then it depends on whether I have a training budget. Pruning needs a healing run and distillation needs a full one.

    Red flagReaching for distillation first. It is the most expensive option and it is rarely the first thing to try.

  9. Why does batching not help on a device?

    Because there is one user and one request. In a server, batching amortises the cost of reading weights across many sequences, so bandwidth is shared. On a device every token pays the full weight read. That is exactly why quantization matters more on device than it does in a datacentre.

    Red flagMissing the connection. The absence of batching is the reason the bandwidth argument is so much stronger on device.

  10. What does sustained load do to an on-device model?

    Heat, then throttling. A benchmark run cold gives a latency number the device cannot hold. A smaller model helps twice here: less data moved per token, and less sustained power, so the throttle point arrives later. It is a real constraint that server-side work never has.

    Red flagQuoting only cold latency. On a consumer device the sustained number is the one users experience.

  11. When would you not shrink a model?

    When the task needs precision the small model cannot hold, and the data is allowed to leave the device. Then a server call with a larger model is simply better. The on-device argument is privacy and latency, and if neither is binding, the constraint is self-imposed.

    Red flagAssuming on-device is always the goal. It is a constraint with benefits, not a virtue.

You quantize weights from sixteen-bit to four-bit on a memory-bound device. What is the primary win?

  1. Weights now occupy roughly a quarter of previous memory
  2. Arithmetic runs four times quicker on the same silicon
  3. Accuracy rises because the model generalises a bit better
  4. The usable context window grows by exactly that factor

A quarter of the memory, and a speed-up that follows from it. Generation is memory-bandwidth bound, so moving a quarter of the bytes per token makes it faster. The arithmetic itself is not the bottleneck, which is why the second option is the tempting wrong one.

Context length is a separate decision. The KV cache is not the weights, and quantizing it is an independent choice that scales with conversation length rather than model size.

Layer pruning removes entire transformer blocks and often costs less accuracy than expected. Why?

  1. Deeper blocks barely change the hidden state they receive
  2. Early blocks hold almost none of the model's knowledge
  3. Removing blocks lets the rest be retrained far faster
  4. The attention heads are duplicated across all the blocks

Redundancy is not evenly spread. Measuring how much each block moves the representation between its input and output shows that many middle and later blocks move it very little, so removing a few of those costs less than removing the same parameter count from everywhere.

The early blocks are the opposite: they do the most structural work and are the worst candidates. That is why pruning is chosen by measurement rather than applied uniformly, and why a short healing fine-tune follows it.

  1. Your quantized model sounds fine and gets specific facts wrong. What happened?

    Exactly what quantization does. Fluency is robust to precision loss and exact recall is not, so the model keeps its register while losing rare tokens and precise values. It is the dangerous failure, because nothing looks broken. It is only caught by task-specific evaluation.

    Red flagCalling it a regression to debug. It is the expected shape of the damage, and expecting it is the answer.

  2. Post-training quantization goes badly. First thing you check?

    The calibration set. Scales are chosen from it, so if it does not resemble production inputs the ranges are wrong from the start. After that, whether scales are per tensor rather than per channel, and whether activations were quantized along with the weights.

    Red flagJumping to a different bit width. The usual cause is calibration, and changing the width hides it rather than fixing it.

  3. A quantized model has to choose between fifty similar functions. What is the risk?

    This is the worst case for quantization. Discriminating between near-identical options depends on small differences in the output distribution, and that is exactly the resolution you spent. The model still produces a confident choice, just a slightly wrong one. It is why the evaluation has to be the function-selection task itself.

    Red flagAssuming a structured output format protects you. A schema guarantees a valid function name, not the right one.

  4. The KV cache grows past the device's memory mid-conversation.

    Something has to go, and the choice is a product decision rather than a technical one. Evict the oldest turns, summarise them, or refuse to continue. Quantizing the cache buys headroom and does not remove the ceiling. The failure to avoid is silently dropping context while continuing to answer confidently.

    Red flagOnly naming a bigger cache. It grows without bound and the interesting answer is what you drop and whether the user knows.

  5. The interviewer asks what you distilled.

    Say plainly that I have not shipped model distillation. I understand it well, including why the temperature on the teacher's outputs matters. What is on my résumé nearby is data distillation, which is filtering and deduplicating a corpus. Same word, different operation, and I would not let them blur.

    Red flagAnswering with the preprocessing pipeline as though it were the same thing. That is the specific trap this line sets.

  6. Is presenting a technique to a division the same as having used it?

    No, and I would draw the line myself. Preparing a division-wide session on layer pruning means I understood it well enough to teach it and answer questions. It does not mean I pruned a production model. Both are real, and saying which one is which is what keeps the rest of the list credible.

    Red flagBlurring them. Teaching is genuinely strong evidence of understanding, and it is undermined by being passed off as deployment.

  7. Why is the word “distilled” a problem on this résumé specifically?

    Because it appears twice with two meanings. The skills line names knowledge distillation, a model technique. The Samsung bullet says a pipeline distilled 150 GB into pretraining data, which is corpus curation. A reader who connects them concludes the first is evidenced, and it is not.

    Red flagNot having noticed. An interviewer reading both lines will, and being the one who points it out is much better.

  8. What would you change about this line?

    Drop knowledge distillation, and mark layer pruning as taught if the format allows it. That leaves quantization and small-model deployment, both shipped and both defensible in detail. I would also rather the Samsung bullet said which quantization, because the bit width is the interesting part.

    Red flagKeeping four items for breadth. Two defended items read stronger than four where half collapse under one question.

    About 3.5 gigabytes for the weights, since four-bit is half a byte per parameter. Then the KV cache on top, which is two for keys and values, times layers, times key-value heads, times head dimension, times bytes per element, per token. On a long conversation that can rival the weights. The trap is using attention heads rather than key-value heads, which overstates it by four or more under grouped-query attention.

  2. “Why does quantization speed things up?” Testing whether you understand the bottleneck. The wrong answer is that the arithmetic is faster, and it is very common.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Bandwidth. Generating a token means reading essentially every weight, and modern accelerators can do far more arithmetic than they can be fed. Halving the bytes per weight roughly halves the data moved per token, so throughput follows. On a device the effect is stronger still, because there is one user and no batching to amortise the weight read across sequences.

  3. “What did quantization cost you in accuracy?” Looking for whether you measured it and on what. A general benchmark answer is the weak one here.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The honest answer is that the damage is uneven, so the number depends entirely on what you measure. Fluency survives low bit widths well, which is dangerous because the model still sounds right. What degrades is precise discrimination, and choosing between fifty similar functions is exactly that. So the evaluation had to be the function-selection task rather than perplexity, which barely moves.

  4. “Tell me about the distillation you did.” The trap question on this line, because a plausible-sounding wrong answer is sitting right there on the same résumé.
    Model answer — say yours first

    I have not shipped model distillation, and I would rather say that than reach for something adjacent. I understand it: a student trained against the teacher's softened output distribution rather than hard labels, where the value is in what the teacher thought was nearly right. The pipeline on my réésumé that distilled 150 gigabytes was corpus curation, which shares the word and not the operation.

  5. “Pruning or quantization, if you had to pick one?” A judgment question. The right answer considers the training budget, which most candidates skip entirely.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Quantization, almost always first. Sixteen-bit to four-bit is exactly a factor of four, it needs no training run, and it is reversible if the accuracy is unacceptable. Pruning removes blocks and then needs a healing fine-tune, because the remaining blocks were trained expecting the ones you deleted. So the deletion is trivial and recovering the accuracy is not, which makes it the second thing to try rather than the first.

The lessons behind this chapter

System Design School teaches these mechanisms from first principles and breaks them in a lab. This chapter defends what you did with them. An interviewer moves between those two registers without warning.
