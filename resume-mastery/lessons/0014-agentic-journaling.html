Agentic journaling and semantic routing

Routing over somebody's own timeline is retrieval where the corpus is a person. That changes what a wrong answer costs, and it changes what “relevant” means — because recency matters in a journal in a way it never does in a document store.

The win

Say what the router routes between, and why a journal is a harder retrieval target than a document set — then answer the question that follows: what happens when it recommends something based on a bad month?

The line being defended
“Built a GenAI journaling application using agentic workflows and semantic routing over user timelines, generating personalized recommendations and trip/event plans.”
Samsung Research · bullet 2 of 5
Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

For “tell me about the journaling application.” The distinguishing idea is that the corpus is a person, so get there early and let the retrieval design follow from it.

  1. Situation

    Somebody keeps a journal, and the application reads it back to offer things. What happened, what they might enjoy, and plans built from both.

    The material is the person's own history, which changes the problem more than it sounds.

  2. Mechanism

    A router picks a mode rather than a topic. Is this asking what happened, asking what to do, or asking for a plan.

    Each needs different retrieval and a different output shape. A recollection cites the entry it came from. A plan is a structured itinerary.

  3. What I did

    The part I would defend is the relevance function. In a document store relevance is similarity. In a journal it is similarity, recency and frequency together.

    The most similar entry might be four years old from a city they have left. And something mentioned once is a data point, where something mentioned monthly is a preference.

  4. What it cost

    Two things I would rather say myself. Only the planning branch is genuinely agentic, because scheduling feeds back into option selection. The other two are one retrieval and one answer.

    And the worst failure here is not inaccuracy. A recommendation built on a misread of someone's history is intrusion, and people stop using things that misunderstand them.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“It uses agentic workflows and semantic routing to personalise recommendations.” Three abstractions and no design. The follow-up is “routing between what?” and if the answer is topics, the whole thing collapses into a classifier. Say “it routes between modes, and only the planning branch is agentic” and both claims become checkable.

The other trap is treating this as ordinary RAG. The moment you say “retrieval over the user's timeline” without mentioning recency, an interviewer who has built anything personal will ask about the four-year-old entry. Get there first.

Explain it · one picture

The picture. A search engine against a friend who has known you for years. The search engine finds whatever is most like what you said. The friend knows that the thing you loved four years ago was four years ago, and that you have mentioned pottery every month since spring.

Behavioural · the story for this bullet

For “tell me about a time the obvious technical answer was wrong for the product” or “a user-facing consequence you had to design around.”

  1. Decision

    Whether relevance over a personal timeline should be semantic similarity, as in every retrieval system. Or something that weights time as heavily as meaning.

  2. Situation

    The application suggested things based on what somebody had written. Pure similarity surfaces the closest entry regardless of when it was written, and a journal spans years of a person changing.

  3. Action

    I made recency and frequency part of the relevance function rather than a filter applied afterwards. Recency as a decay rather than a cutoff, so nothing vanishes abruptly. Frequency counted over distinct entries, so one long entry could not look like a habit.

    The weights differ per mode, because a recollection wants similarity dominant and a recommendation does not.

  4. Result

    Suggestions stopped being driven by whatever was semantically closest in the whole history. That was often something from a life the person had moved on from.

  5. What it cost

    A decay rate I chose rather than measured. That is the same weakness as the alpha in the forecasting layer. It can also still surface a period somebody would rather not revisit. No weighting knows which period that is. The only honest fix is user control, which is a product feature rather than a model change.

Start here — the plain version

Somebody writes entries about their days. The application reads them and offers things back: a suggestion, a plan for a weekend, a reminder of something they enjoyed. The material it works from is the person's own history.

Two mechanisms carry that. Routing decides what kind of request this is, and which part of the history to look at. Agentic workflow means the answer is assembled in steps rather than produced in one call. A trip plan needs several different things fetched and combined.

Timeline
The ordered set of a person's entries. Ordered is the important word, because when something happened is part of what it means.
Semantic routing
Choosing a branch by meaning rather than keyword. Here it decides whether a request wants a recollection, a recommendation or a plan.
Recency weighting
Preferring recent entries over older ones. In a journal this is not a tie-break, it is part of relevance, because people change.
Agentic workflow
Assembling an answer in steps, where later steps depend on earlier results. A trip plan needs preferences, then options, then a schedule.
Personalisation
Using someone's history to shape output. The whole value and the whole risk, because being wrong about a person is worse than being wrong about a fact.
Cold start
The state where a new user has almost no history. Personalisation has nothing to work with, and the system has to be useful anyway.
Sentiment drift
A person's mood or circumstances changing over time. An average over a whole timeline can describe somebody who no longer exists.
Grounding
Tying a suggestion back to the entry it came from, so the person can see why it was offered.

What the router actually chooses between

Not topics. Modes. A request can be asking what happened, asking what to do next, or asking for something planned. Those need different retrieval and different output, so they are different branches.

Be concrete here, because “semantic routing” on its own is a phrase rather than a design. A recollection needs a narrow slice of timeline and a faithful answer. A recommendation needs a broad read of preferences and a suggestion that may have no precedent in the timeline. A plan needs both, plus external information the journal does not contain.

The output shapes differ too. A recollection should cite the entry it came from. A plan is a structured itinerary. If one branch produced all three, the schema would have to be loose enough to hold all of them, which means loose enough to be wrong in all of them.

In plain words

Relevance is the score that picks which old entries the app reads before it answers. Scoring on meaning alone can pick a glowing entry about a restaurant in a city the person left years ago. So the score also counts how recent an entry is and how many separate entries mention the same thing, all at once rather than as a date filter afterwards.

Why a timeline is harder than a document store

In a document corpus, relevance is about meaning. In a journal it is about meaning and time together, and the two can disagree. The most similar entry may be the least useful one.

Someone asks for a restaurant suggestion. The most semantically similar entries are every restaurant they ever wrote about, including one from four years ago in a city they left. Pure similarity surfaces it. Recency says ignore it.

That is why recency weighting is not a refinement here. It is part of the relevance function. The same applies to frequency: something mentioned once is a data point, and something mentioned monthly is a preference, and similarity search cannot tell them apart.

And the timeline is small. A person might have a few hundred entries, not millions of documents. That changes the engineering: exhaustive scoring is affordable, and the interesting work moves from index structures to what you score on.

In plain words

“Agentic” means the system works in steps and chooses each next step from what the last one found. Planning a trip fits: a clash found while building the schedule can knock out an option picked earlier. So say that only the planning part is agentic, because calling the whole app agentic is the claim most likely to be picked apart.

What makes it agentic, honestly

A recollection is one retrieval and one answer, and calling that agentic would be overstating it. A trip plan genuinely needs steps, because each one depends on the last.

Apply the rule from chapter 0009. An agent earns its name when the path depends on what is found. Planning a weekend needs preferences from the timeline, then candidate options, then a schedule that fits them together, and a constraint discovered at step two changes step three.

So the honest description is that one branch is agentic and the others are not, and the router is what decides which. That is a better answer than claiming the whole application is agentic, and it is the one that survives a follow-up.

The failure that matters most

Being wrong about a fact is recoverable. Being wrong about a person is not, in the same way. A recommendation built on a misread of somebody's history reads as not being understood.

This is the part to volunteer, because it is what separates a product answer from a retrieval answer. If the system suggests something based on a period the person would rather forget, the failure is not inaccuracy. It is intrusion.

Two mitigations follow and neither is a model change. Ground the suggestion in the entry it came from, so the person can see why and correct it. And weight recency hard enough that a difficult period stops driving recommendations once it has passed.

  1. What is semantic routing, and how is it different from a classifier?

    Both decide which handler should answer a request. A classifier is trained on labelled data and outputs a calibrated probability. Routing skips the training. You write a handful of example utterances per route and embed them once. An incoming request is embedded and sent to whichever route's examples it lands nearest. What that buys is a new capability costing three example phrases, rather than a labelling round and a retrain. For a product still finding its shape that is a day against a sprint. It is weaker on confidence, because there is no calibrated threshold at which to say you are unsure. And two routes with overlapping examples degrade quietly.

    Red flagUsing the two words interchangeably. One is a nearest-neighbour lookup and the other is a trained model, and they fail in different ways.

  2. What is actually embedded and searched here?

    The user's own timeline entries, one index per person. An embedding is a fixed-length list of numbers a model produces from text, arranged so similar meanings land close together. What that buys is matching on meaning rather than wording. An entry about “that place by the river we kept going back to” is findable from a query about a favourite restaurant. The per-user part is unusual for retrieval work. The corpus is small, perhaps a few thousand entries, so exact search is affordable and the approximate-index question mostly disappears. What arrives instead is operational. You now have one index per user to build, back up and delete on request.

    Red flagDescribing it like a document corpus. Per-user indexes change the cost model and the deletion story, and that is the interesting half.

  3. How does recency weighting work, and what does it cost you?

    Retrieval by similarity alone treats a note from three years ago exactly like yesterday's. Recency weighting multiplies each score by a factor that shrinks with age, normally an exponential decay with a chosen half-life. A journal is a time series, so for most questions last week should outrank last year. What it buys is answers reflecting who the user is now. Without it an old and strongly-worded entry outranks a recent, more relevant one forever. The cost is that the half-life is a product decision dressed as maths. Too short and it forgets a standing preference. Too long and it recommends what they stopped liking. There is no correct value, only a chosen one.

    Red flagPresenting the decay constant as tuned when it was picked. Saying you chose it by feel is fine. Implying it was fitted is not.

  4. What makes a workflow agentic, and is this one?

    Agentic means the model chooses the next step, something executes, the result comes back, and the model decides again with it in view. The defining property is that the sequence is not known in advance. A fixed chain of prompts is not that, however many links it has. What the loop buys is the ability to follow evidence, because a second retrieval may only make sense once the first came back. Planning a trip across several dependent retrievals qualifies. Routing and then generating is two steps in a fixed order. Narrow the claim to the part that actually loops, because that is the part of this line most likely to be pulled apart.

    Red flagDefending the whole system as agentic. One branch qualifies. Saying which one is stronger than defending all of it.

  5. The corpus is somebody's diary. What does that change?

    It moves storage location, retention and deletion out of compliance and into architecture. A journal is close to the most sensitive corpus a person owns. Where the index lives is a design decision. Who can read it is a design decision. What naming this buys is a defensible design, because a system handling diaries with no answer here is not an achievement whatever its retrieval quality. Deletion is the hard one. Removing an entry has to remove its vector, its summaries, any cached retrieval results, and anything else derived from it. Derived artefacts are exactly what people forget, and they are still personal data.

    Red flagAnswering with encryption at rest. That is table stakes. The question is about what happens to derived data when a user asks to be forgotten.

  6. What is cold start here, and what do you do about it?

    A brand new user has an empty timeline, so a system that personalises from history has nothing to work with. Every quality claim on this line is implicitly about users who have already written a great deal. It matters more than it sounds, because new users are exactly the ones deciding whether to keep the app. What having an answer buys is a product that stays usable during that window. The options are a generic non-personalised path, an explicit onboarding question or two, or defaults drawn from the population until history builds. Any of the three is fine. Pick one deliberately and say which.

    Red flagTreating it as an edge case. It is the first experience every single user has, and a personalisation system with no answer for it is broken at the front door.

  7. How would you know the recommendations are any good?

    Two ways, and they answer different questions. Offline, you replay recorded history and check whether the system would have surfaced what the person actually went on to choose. That is cheap and repeatable. Online, you put it in front of real people and measure whether suggestions get accepted. What offline evaluation buys is daily iteration without shipping anything, which is the only way to tune retrieval and weighting at reasonable speed. Quote the offline number carefully. It can only score what the user was shown and chose. It is blind to the recommendation they would have loved and never saw, which is the exact failure personalisation is prone to.

    Red flag“Users liked it.” If there is no number, say there is no number. Inventing a satisfaction figure is the fastest way to lose the room.

  1. In two sentences, what does this application do?

    Somebody keeps a journal, and the application reads it back to offer things. Recollections of what happened, recommendations shaped by what they seem to enjoy, and plans for trips or events built from both.

    Red flagCalling it a chatbot over notes. The output shapes are different per request type, and that difference is the design.

  2. What does the semantic router choose between?

    Modes, not topics. Is this asking what happened, asking what to do, or asking for a plan. Each needs different retrieval and produces a different output shape. A recollection wants a narrow slice and a faithful answer. A plan wants preferences plus outside information.

    Red flagSaying it routes by subject. Subject routing is a topic classifier and it would not change the output shape.

  3. Why not handle everything in one branch?

    Because the output schemas differ. A recollection cites the entry it came from. A plan is a structured itinerary. One branch would need a schema loose enough to hold both. And a loose schema cannot catch a wrong answer in either shape.

    Red flagCiting prompt length. The real cost is a schema that stops constraining anything.

  4. Recency and frequency both matter. What does each tell you?

    Recency tells you whether something is still true of the person. Frequency tells you whether it is a preference or a one-off. Something mentioned once is a data point and something mentioned monthly is a pattern, and pure similarity search cannot distinguish them.

    Red flagOnly naming recency. The frequency signal is what separates a habit from an incident, and it needs counting rather than matching.

  5. What makes this agentic rather than a single call?

    Only the planning branch, and I would say so. A recollection is one retrieval and one answer. A trip plan needs preferences, then options, then a schedule, and a constraint found at one step changes the next. That is a path that depends on what is found, which is the test.

    Red flagClaiming the whole application is agentic. Conceding that one branch is and the others are not is both true and more convincing.

  6. A brand new user has three entries. What happens?

    Personalisation has nothing to work with, and the honest design admits that rather than inventing preferences from three data points. The system should fall back to generic suggestions and say it is still learning. A confident personalised recommendation from three entries is worse than an obviously generic one.

    Red flagClaiming it personalises anyway. Three entries cannot support it, and pretending they can is the failure the next question is about.

  7. Why ground a suggestion in a specific entry?

    So the person can see why it was offered and correct it. It converts an opaque recommendation into an inspectable one. It also gives the system a cheap correction signal, because a person disagreeing with the entry tells you more than a thumbs-down on the suggestion.

    Red flagFraming it only as transparency. The correction signal is the engineering benefit and it is the more interesting half.

  8. What is the riskiest claim in this bullet?

    “Agentic workflows” as a blanket description, since only the planning branch genuinely qualifies. And “personalized” invites a question about how personalisation was evaluated. That is hard for a system where the ground truth is a person's own preference.

    Red flagNaming semantic routing. That is the strongest and most specific part of the bullet.

  1. How should the router's output be shaped?

    As a typed literal over the modes, with a confidence. That mirrors the screener in chapter 0005. A mode parsed out of prose can come back as a fourth value. There would be no branch behind it. Three named modes and nothing else means every possible output has code waiting for it.

    Red flagLetting the router answer in prose. It is control flow, so it needs the same discipline as any other routing decision.

  2. How do you combine similarity, recency and frequency?

    A weighted score, with recency as a decay rather than a cutoff, so nothing disappears abruptly. Frequency counts distinct entries rather than mentions. One long entry repeating a place should not look like a habit. The weights are per mode. Recollection wants similarity dominant, and recommendation wants recency and frequency to matter more.

    Red flagOne global ranking. The modes want different weightings, which is another reason routing comes first.

  3. A person has a few hundred entries. What does that change?

    Almost everything about the engineering. Exhaustive scoring over a few hundred items is trivial, so approximate indexes and their recall trade-offs are irrelevant. The work moves entirely to what you score on. It also means per-user data is small enough to keep on a device, which matters for this product.

    Red flagReaching for a vector database. At this size it adds infrastructure and buys nothing.

  4. Why extract structured facts rather than matching raw text?

    Because places, people and dates are what queries are actually about, and they are buried in prose. Extracting them once gives you fields to filter and count on. Frequency of a place is countable only if the place was extracted. Raw similarity cannot answer “how often” at all.

    Red flagSaying embeddings capture it. They capture similarity, and counting needs an entity rather than a direction in space.

  5. How do you chunk a journal?

    By entry, because the entry is the natural unit and it carries its date. Splitting one entry loses the date association unless every chunk carries it. Merging entries destroys the boundary that makes recency meaningful. This is the case where the document's own structure obviously beats a token window.

    Red flagProposing a fixed token size. The entry boundary carries the timestamp, which is half the relevance signal.

  6. Walk me through the planning branch.

    Read preferences from the timeline. Generate candidate options against those. Then build a schedule that fits the candidates together, checking constraints like travel time or opening hours. Step three regularly invalidates a candidate from step two, which is why it is a loop rather than a pipeline.

    Red flagDescribing it as three sequential calls. The feedback from scheduling into selection is what makes it agentic.

  7. A step in the plan fails. What does the user get?

    A partial plan with the gap named, rather than nothing. If options were found and scheduling failed, showing the options is still useful. The failure to avoid is a plan that silently omits what could not be arranged. The person cannot tell the difference between not suggested and not possible.

    Red flagReturning an error. A partial answer with an honest gap is more useful than nothing.

  8. How do you evaluate a personalised recommendation?

    Not against a ground truth, because there is not one. What you can measure is whether the retrieved entries supported the suggestion, which is a grounding check. And whether the person accepted it. The first is checkable offline and the second needs users, so the offline metric is about faithfulness rather than quality.

    Red flagProposing a labelled test set. Somebody else's judgment of what this person would like is not ground truth.

  9. What feedback signal would you want?

    Disagreement with the grounding rather than with the suggestion. If a recommendation cites an entry and the person says that entry does not represent them any more, that is a precise correction. A thumbs-down on the output tells you something was wrong and not which part.

    Red flagCollecting ratings on the suggestion. It is the coarsest possible signal and it is what everyone builds first.

  10. Somebody's circumstances change completely. How does the system catch up?

    Through recency decay, and the speed of that decay is the whole design question. Too slow and it recommends based on a life the person has left. Too fast and it forgets stable preferences that took years to establish. It is the same trade as the alpha in chapter 0007, and I would measure it the same way.

    Red flagTreating it as a data freshness problem. It is a weighting decision with a cost on both sides.

  11. Should this run on the device?

    There is a strong argument. The corpus is one person's private journal, it is small enough to fit, and the retrieval is cheap at a few hundred entries. What pushes against it is the planning branch, which needs outside information, so that branch cannot be local anyway. A split design is the honest answer.

    Red flagAnswering all or nothing. The modes have different data requirements, so they can sit in different places.

  12. Where does this break as a user's history grows?

    Not on retrieval cost, because a decade of daily entries is still only a few thousand. It breaks on drift: a ten-year timeline describes several different people, and any single weighting is wrong for some of them. That is when you need segmentation by period rather than a single decayed score.

    Red flagAnswering with index scaling. The corpus stays small and the semantic problem is what grows.

The semantic router misclassifies a request. What is the consequence?

  1. Sends the request down the wrong retrieval path
  2. Returns text that fails to parse into anything
  3. Costs more tokens than a keyword match would
  4. Needs retraining whenever a new entry is added

Wrong branch, wrong everything after it. A recommendation request routed as a recollection retrieves a narrow slice and answers faithfully about the past, when the person wanted a suggestion. The output is well-formed and answers a different question.

Parsing is not the risk if the router returns a typed literal, which is exactly why it should. And routing by retrieval rather than by a trained classifier is precisely what avoids retraining when entries are added.

Retrieving over a personal timeline needs a signal that document retrieval usually ignores. Which?

  1. Recency, because journals are mostly about recent events
  2. Alphabetical order, so that the entries stay predictable
  3. Entry length, because longer entries hold more detail
  4. Random sampling, to avoid one unusually narrow period

Time is part of relevance rather than metadata. The most semantically similar entry might be four years old, from a city the person has left. In a document corpus that is a good match. In a journal it is the wrong answer.

Frequency matters for the same reason: something mentioned once is a data point and something mentioned monthly is a preference. Similarity search alone cannot tell those apart, which is why the scoring function has three terms rather than one.

Edges and exceptions

Personal data changes what a failure is. Several of these have no technical answer, and saying so is correct.

  • A difficult period
  • Contradictory entries
  • Someone who stopped writing
  • A suggestion with no precedent
  • Other people in the entries
  • Deleting an entry

  • What you would change

  1. The timeline contains a period the person would rather not revisit.

    Then retrieval has to be able to avoid it. No purely semantic system knows which period that is. The workable answer is user control. Let entries or ranges be marked as not for suggestions. That is a product feature rather than a model improvement, and it is the only honest mitigation.

    Red flagProposing sentiment detection. Inferring that somebody is sad and silently excluding it is a decision the system should not make alone.

  2. Two entries contradict each other about a preference.

    People are inconsistent, so this is normal rather than an error. Recency weighting resolves most of it by preferring the later statement. Where both are recent, the honest output acknowledges both. Picking a side sounds confident about a person who is not.

    Red flagTreating it as data to clean. The contradiction is the truth about the person.

  3. Somebody wrote daily for a year, then stopped six months ago.

    Recency decay makes everything stale at once, so the system has very little weight anywhere. That is arguably correct, since it genuinely does not know what has happened since. The right behaviour is to say so rather than to confidently recommend from six-month-old data as if it were current.

    Red flagFalling back to the whole history unweighted. That is exactly the case where the system is most likely to describe somebody who has changed.

  4. Should a recommendation ever be something with no precedent in the timeline?

    Yes, and it is the interesting half of recommendation. A system that only suggests what somebody already did is a search engine over their past. The value is generalising from preferences to something new. The cost is that it cannot be grounded in an entry, so it has to be presented differently.

    Red flagInsisting everything is grounded. That makes recollection safe and recommendation useless.

  5. The entries mention other people by name.

    Then the journal contains third-party personal data that those people never consented to. It constrains what can leave the device and what can be used for anything beyond this user. It is a genuine reason to favour on-device processing, and it is a legal question rather than only a technical one.

    Red flagTreating it all as the author's data. Other people appear in it and did not agree to anything.

  6. A user deletes an entry. What has to happen?

    It leaves the index, any extracted structure derived from it, and any cached preference that used it. Deletion in a derived system is harder than deletion in a store. The entry's influence has spread. If a preference was computed from it, that preference has to be recomputed rather than merely orphaned.

    Red flagOnly deleting the row. The derived artefacts are where the data actually survives.

  7. Defend “agentic workflows” in this bullet.

    Partially. The planning branch is genuinely agentic, because scheduling feeds back into option selection and the path depends on what is found. Recollection and recommendation are single retrievals with one generation. I would describe it as one agentic branch behind a router rather than an agentic application.

    Red flagDefending all three branches. Conceding two of them makes the third claim believable.

  8. What would you change?

    Make the grounding visible on every recollection and recommendation, so corrections are precise rather than a thumbs-down. Add user control over which periods feed suggestions. And tune the recency decay against real behaviour rather than choosing it, which is the same experiment as tuning alpha in the forecasting layer.

    Red flagProposing a better model. Every one of the real problems here is about weighting, control and feedback.
