Teaching it: pruning, KG-RAG, multimodal

This is the one bullet on the résumé that claims communication. It gets tested by being asked to teach one of the three topics on the spot, which means the bullet is only as good as the content still in your head.

The technology — what each named thing is

The primer above defines what each session covered. This one defines the specific named things inside those topics, starting from the ground. Teaching a subject claims you know it to the depth of the follow-up question, so the follow-ups are the point here.

Every entry answers four things. What it actually is, in plain words. Where it sat in the sessions. What it buys, meaning what goes wrong without it. And the follow-up an interviewer asks once you have said the name.

Layer pruning
the depth kindMost pruning removes individual weights. This removes entire transformer blocks. The surprising finding behind it is that in a deep model many blocks change the hidden state very little, so you can measure the similarity between each block's input and its output and delete the ones that barely move it. In practice the middle-to-late layers are the quietest. In the app. It was session one. What it buys. A genuine speedup, because you are removing whole computations rather than making a matrix sparse. Fewer layers means proportionally less work, on any hardware, with no special kernels. The follow-up. Healing. Accuracy drops on removal and comes back with a short fine-tune afterwards, usually LoRA. A pruning claim with no healing step described is only half the method, and the half that does not work.
Structured and unstructured sparsity
why pruning shapes matterUnstructured pruning sets individual weights to zero wherever they are small. The matrix has fewer non-zero values but the same shape, and a GPU still multiplies the whole thing, because dense matrix multiplication is what the hardware does. Structured pruning removes entire rows, attention heads or layers, so the matrix genuinely shrinks. In the app. It is the distinction that makes the session practical rather than academic. What it buys. Understanding which kind of pruning produces a number you can put on a latency chart. The follow-up. Fifty percent unstructured sparsity typically buys nothing at all at inference time. Being able to say that, unprompted, is what separates having read the papers from having run them.
GraphRAG
Microsoft's, specificallyAn alternative to embedding-based retrieval. Instead of chunking documents and embedding them, you run a model over the whole corpus to extract entities and the relationships between them, assemble those into a knowledge graph, cluster the graph into communities of related entities, summarise each community with a model, and answer questions from that hierarchy of summaries. In the app. It is what “knowledge-graph RAG” on the résumé names. What it buys. Answers that require synthesis across many documents, which chunk retrieval structurally cannot produce. The follow-up. The cost, and it is large. Indexing runs a model over the entire corpus several times, so it is dramatically more expensive to build than a vector index and only pays back on questions vector search genuinely cannot answer.
Leiden
the clustering algorithmAn algorithm for finding communities in a graph: groups of nodes more densely connected to each other than to the rest. It is run recursively here, so communities contain sub-communities and you get a hierarchy rather than a flat partition. Its specific improvement over the older Louvain algorithm is a refinement phase that guarantees every community it returns is internally connected. In the app. It produces the hierarchy that the community summaries hang on. What it buys. A summary tree you can query at different resolutions, from a handful of top-level themes down to specific clusters. The follow-up. Why internal connectivity matters at all. A community that is internally disconnected is two unrelated topics in one bag, and summarising it produces confident nonsense that nothing downstream will catch.
Local and global search
the two query pathsGraphRAG answers questions in two quite different ways. Local search finds the entities that match the question and walks outward through their neighbourhood, gathering connected facts. Global search ignores individual entities and instead combines the community summaries at a chosen level of the hierarchy into an answer. In the app. Global search is the reason to build the graph at all. What it buys. Corpus-level questions become answerable. The follow-up. Global is precisely what vector RAG cannot do. “What are the main themes across this corpus” has no single passage that answers it, so retrieval has nothing to retrieve. That question is the entire argument for the approach, and it is the one to lead with.
Vision encoder
CLIP, SigLIP, ViTThe component that turns an image into vectors a language model can consume. A vision transformer chops the image into fixed-size patches, treats each patch like a token, and runs attention over them. CLIP and SigLIP are trained on image and caption pairs so that a picture and its description land close together in the same space, which is what makes the resulting vectors mean something a text model can use. In the app. Session three. What it buys. Images and text sharing one representation, which is the whole basis of multimodal models. The follow-up. Resolution, which is where all the engineering is. Patching a high-resolution image produces far too many tokens for the context window, and everything clever in this area is a scheme for managing that trade.
Projector
the LLaVA-style bridgeThe vision encoder outputs vectors in its own space and the language model expects vectors in a different one. A projector is a small trained network, often just a couple of linear layers, that maps between them. After it, image patches arrive at the language model looking like tokens it already understands. In the app. It is the cheapest known route to making a text model multimodal. What it buys. Multimodality for the cost of training a tiny network. Both large models stay frozen, so no expensive training run is needed and the language model's abilities are preserved exactly. The follow-up. What actually gets trained. Usually the projector alone, with the vision encoder and the language model both frozen, and that economy is the entire reason the approach became standard.
Resampler
the other bridgeThe alternative to a projector. Rather than mapping every image vector through, a resampler uses cross-attention with a fixed set of learned queries to compress hundreds of image vectors into a small fixed number of output tokens. Flamingo's Perceiver Resampler and BLIP-2's Q-Former are the two named examples. In the app. It is the design that controls token count directly. What it buys. A fixed token budget regardless of image resolution, so a higher-resolution image costs the same in context as a low one. The follow-up. The trade against a projector. A projector is simple and lets token count grow with resolution. A resampler pins the budget and pays for it in extra parameters and a harder training setup.
Division-wide
the claim about scaleNot a technology at all, but the word on this line most likely to be probed. It describes an audience rather than a topic, and unlike everything above it there is no definition to fall back on. In the app. Everything else on this line is checkable and this is the part that is not. What it buys. Nothing, unless you can put numbers behind it, which is exactly why it is listed here among the technologies. The follow-up. How many sessions, how many people attended, and whether the material still exists anywhere. One recorded deck you can point at is worth considerably more than the adjective.

Layer pruning, in one minute

Delete whole transformer blocks from a trained model, then fine-tune briefly to recover. It works because the middle of a deep model changes the hidden state surprisingly little from one block to the next.

The selection rule is measurable. Compare the hidden state entering a block with the hidden state leaving a run of blocks. Where that distance is small, the blocks are doing little, and a contiguous run of them can go.

The deeper blocks are the redundant ones, except for the last one or two. Those matter a great deal and should be left alone. Cut a contiguous run rather than scattered blocks, because the model was trained as a stack.

Then heal. A short low-rank fine-tune recovers most of what the cut cost, and it is cheap because only the adapter trains. Without healing the drop is much larger than the numbers people quote.

Plain meaningThe middle layers of a big model are doing less than you would think. Find a stretch that barely changes anything, cut it out, and patch the model up afterwards.

Why this kind of pruning pays and other kinds often do not

Zeroing individual weights gives you a sparse matrix. It is the same size, with the same number of operations. You only win if the hardware can skip the zeros, and usually it cannot.

Layer pruning has no such problem. The blocks are gone, so they are never executed. Memory drops, prefill drops, per-token latency drops, and the key-value cache shrinks by the same fraction. Nothing has to cooperate.

That is the honest version of why it is the first thing to reach for. It is also why the quoted numbers are usually about parameters rather than about what it costs you, and those two are the same here and not elsewhere.

Where it breaks is the part worth volunteering. Knowledge questions hold up well past the point where multi-step reasoning falls over. Depth is what composition uses, so cutting depth hurts the composed answers first.

Plain meaningSetting weights to zero saves nothing unless the chip cares. Deleting layers saves everything, because you just do not run them.

Knowledge-graph RAG, in one minute

Ordinary retrieval is good at what a corpus says about one thing. It is bad at how two things connect. That answer is not sitting in any single passage, so there is nothing to retrieve.

So you build a graph first. A model reads the corpus and extracts entities and the relations between them. Those become nodes and edges, and the text they came from stays attached as evidence.

Then two ways to ask. A local question starts at an entity and walks the neighbourhood. A broad question uses pre-written summaries of densely connected regions, combined at answer time, because no chunk covers the whole thing.

The cost is real and it is where the conversation should go. Building the graph is a model pass over the entire corpus. That turns indexing from cheap into a line item, and it has to be redone when the corpus changes.

Plain meaningNormal search finds passages. This builds a map of who relates to what first, so a question that crosses documents has somewhere to be answered from.

Multimodal, in one minute

An image becomes tokens. A vision encoder turns the picture into vectors. A small projector maps those into the language model's token space. From there the model cannot tell them apart from words.

Training happens in stages. Freeze the encoder and the language model and train only the projector on image-caption pairs. Then instruction-tune on tasks. The projector is small, which is why this is affordable at all.

The engineering consequence is the one people miss. An image costs tokens, often hundreds of them, and at higher resolution many more. That dominates the context budget and the prefill cost before it does anything else.

Which is why resamplers exist. They emit a fixed number of tokens whatever the image size, trading some detail for a bounded cost. On a device with a small context, that trade is not optional.

Plain meaningA picture gets translated into the same kind of tokens as words. It just turns into a lot of them, and that is what you pay for.

What a division-wide session is actually for

Most of the room will never implement it. Designing the talk for the few who might is the common mistake, and it loses everyone else in the first five minutes.

The useful goal is narrower. Leave one idea behind that changes how somebody thinks about a problem they already have. For layer pruning that idea is that the middle of a model is doing less than you assume.

The shape that works is four moves. Why you should care, the smallest true mechanism, where it breaks, and when not to use it. The last move is the one that gets skipped and the one that earns trust.

And the measure of whether it landed is not attendance. It is somebody coming back a month later having tried it, or arguing with you about it. Both mean the idea survived the walk back to their desk.

  1. In layer pruning, how do you choose which layers to remove?

    Most pruning removes individual weights. Layer pruning removes entire transformer blocks. The surprising finding behind it is that in a deep model many blocks change the hidden state very little. So you measure the similarity between each block's input and its output, and delete the ones that barely move it. In practice the middle-to-late layers are the quietest. What that buys is a real speedup, because you removed whole computations rather than making a matrix sparse. Then you heal. Accuracy falls on removal and comes back with a short fine-tune, often LoRA. A pruning result with no healing step described is half a method, and the half that does not work.

    Red flagRemoving layers by position because a paper did. The measurement is the method, and it differs by model.

  2. Why does the shape of sparsity matter?

    Because hardware cannot use the unstructured kind. Unstructured pruning sets individual weights to zero wherever they are small. The matrix has fewer non-zero values and exactly the same shape, so a GPU still multiplies the whole thing. Dense matrix multiplication is what the hardware does. Structured pruning removes entire rows, attention heads or layers, so the matrix genuinely shrinks and the model genuinely gets faster. What knowing the difference buys is being able to say which kind of pruning produces a number you can put on a latency chart. Half your weights set to zero can buy nothing at all at inference.

    Red flagQuoting a sparsity percentage as a speedup. They are only related when the sparsity is structured, and that is the point of the distinction.

  3. What does GraphRAG actually build?

    A knowledge graph, extracted from the corpus by a model. Instead of chunking documents and embedding them, you run a model over everything to pull out entities and the relationships between them. Entities become nodes and relations become edges. The graph is clustered into communities of related entities, and each one gets a written summary. Queries are answered from that hierarchy rather than from raw passages. What it buys is answers requiring synthesis across many documents, which chunk retrieval structurally cannot produce. Indexing is expensive, because it runs a model over the whole corpus several times. That cost is the first thing to volunteer.

    Red flagPresenting it as better RAG. It is more expensive RAG that answers a different class of question, and the cost is the first thing an interviewer probes.

  4. What does Leiden give you that Louvain does not?

    A guarantee that every community is internally connected. Both find communities in a graph, meaning groups of nodes more densely connected to each other than to the rest. Leiden is run recursively here, so communities contain sub-communities and you get a hierarchy rather than a flat partition. Louvain can produce a cluster whose members are not actually linked to each other. Leiden adds a refinement phase that rules that out. What the hierarchy buys is a summary tree you can query at different resolutions. What the guarantee buys is summaries that mean something. A disconnected community is two unrelated topics in one bag, and it summarises into confident nonsense.

    Red flagNaming the algorithm as a detail. The connectivity guarantee is exactly why the summaries are readable, so it is the substance.

  5. What can global search answer that vector RAG cannot?

    Questions with no single passage behind them. GraphRAG answers in two ways. Local search finds the entities matching the question and walks outward through their neighbourhood, gathering connected facts. Global search ignores individual entities and combines community summaries at a chosen level of the hierarchy. So ask what the main themes across a corpus are, and vector retrieval has nothing to retrieve, because the answer exists in no one document. What global search buys is corpus-level questions becoming answerable at all. That is the entire argument for building the graph, and it is the one to lead with.

    Red flagJustifying GraphRAG on accuracy. On passage-level questions vector search is usually fine and much cheaper. The case is the question type.

  6. How does an image become something a language model can read?

    A vision encoder cuts it into fixed-size patches, treats each patch like a token, and runs attention over them. That encoder is usually a vision transformer. It is trained the CLIP or SigLIP way, on image and caption pairs, so a picture lands close to its description. What that training buys is vectors that mean something to a text model, rather than arbitrary numbers. Then a bridge maps those vectors into the language model's own embedding space, and the model sees them as tokens. Resolution is the constraint that drives all the engineering. More pixels means more patches, which means more tokens, and the context window is finite.

    Red flag“It understands images.” It receives vectors that were trained to sit near text meaning. Saying the mechanism is what makes the answer credible.

  7. Projector or resampler. What is the trade?

    A projector is a small trained network, often a couple of linear layers, mapping image vectors straight into the text embedding space. What it buys is multimodality for the cost of training a tiny network. Both large models stay frozen, so there is no expensive training run and the language model's abilities are preserved exactly. A resampler uses cross-attention with a fixed set of learned queries to compress hundreds of image vectors into a small fixed number of tokens. What that buys is a fixed token budget whatever the resolution. The trade is straightforward. A projector is simple and lets token count grow with resolution. A resampler pins it and costs parameters and training complexity.

    Red flagKnowing only one of them. Naming both and the token-budget reason is what shows you taught the session rather than attended one.

    It becomes tokens. A vision encoder turns the image into vectors. A small projector maps those into the language model's token space, and after that the model cannot tell them from words. Training is staged. Freeze both big parts, train only the projector on captions, then instruction-tune.

    Red flagDescribing it as the model seeing the image. Nothing sees anything. An encoder produces vectors and a projector puts them in the same space as text.

  4. Why does layer pruning speed a model up when zeroing weights often does not?

    Because the blocks are gone and never execute. Memory, prefill, per-token latency and the key-value cache all drop by the same fraction, and nothing has to cooperate. Zeroed weights leave a matrix the same size with the same operation count. Skipping the zeros needs hardware support that usually is not there.

    Red flagTreating parameter count as the measure. Parameters removed and latency saved are the same number here and very different numbers elsewhere.

  5. Which blocks do you cut, and how many?

    A contiguous run from the deeper part of the stack, but never the last one or two. Those carry a lot. How many is empirical. Sweep the run length, measure after healing, and look for where it falls off. The cliff arrives far earlier on multi-step reasoning than on factual recall.

    Red flagScattering the cuts. The model was trained as a stack, and a contiguous run is what the distance measure is actually telling you about.

  6. When would you not build a graph?

    For single-hop factual lookup, which is most questions. Chunk retrieval answers those well and costs almost nothing to index. A graph needs a model pass over the whole corpus to build and a rebuild when the corpus changes. If nobody is asking questions that cross documents, that is a large bill for nothing.

    Red flagPresenting it as the better RAG. Knowing when a technique is not worth it is the part that makes the session useful rather than promotional.

  7. What does an image actually cost at inference time?

    Tokens. Often hundreds, and many more at higher resolution. They occupy the context window and they all go through prefill, so the cost lands before any generation happens. Resamplers exist for exactly this. They emit a fixed token count whatever the image size and trade detail for a bounded bill.

    Red flagAnswering on model size. The encoder is small. The context and prefill cost is what actually hurts, especially on a device.

    Mostly people who will never implement it. A handful might, and designing for that handful loses the room in five minutes. The goal is to leave one idea behind that changes how somebody thinks about a problem they already have. The implementers get a pointer to the paper afterwards.

    Red flagDescribing it as a tutorial. A tutorial for a mixed audience serves neither half of it.

  9. How do you know a session landed?

    Not from attendance or a feedback form. From somebody coming back weeks later having tried it, or arguing with you about it. Both mean the idea survived the walk back to their desk. That is the only signal I would put weight on.

    Red flagQuoting attendance. It measures calendar invitations, and everybody in the room knows that.

  1. What exactly do you measure to choose the blocks?

    The angle between the hidden state going into a run of blocks and the state coming out. Angle rather than magnitude, because the scale of these states drifts with depth and the direction is what carries meaning. Small angle means those blocks moved the representation very little.

    Red flagMeasuring weight magnitude. That is what unstructured pruning uses, and it says nothing about whether a block is doing work.

  2. Pruning or quantization first?

    Quantization almost always. It is nearly free and it does not change the architecture. Pruning is what you reach for when quantization has run out and the model is still too big. They compose, but the combined loss is worse than either alone. Measure the pair rather than adding the two numbers.

    Red flagAssuming the losses add. Two lossy compressions interact, and the interaction is usually in the wrong direction.

  3. What does healing involve, and how much does it matter?

    A short fine-tune after the cut, usually a low-rank adapter so only a small set of parameters moves. It matters enormously. The quoted results for this technique assume it. Without healing, the drop is large enough that the method looks much worse than it is.

    Red flagTreating it as optional polish. It is part of the method, and reporting a pruned model without it is reporting a different experiment.

  4. Where does the cost of a knowledge graph actually land?

    At index time, not query time, and people get that backwards. Building it means a model reads every document to extract entities and relations. On a large corpus that is a real bill, and it comes back every time the corpus changes enough to matter.

    Red flagComparing query latency. Query time is comparable. The difference is an index you have to pay a model to build.

  5. The extractor invents a relation that is not in the text. What happens?

    It becomes an edge, and an edge is asserted rather than retrieved. That is worse than a bad chunk. A bad chunk sits next to its own text, so a reader can see it is wrong. Chapter 0006 gave the rule: a schema constrains the shape of an output and never its truth.

    Red flagTrusting the graph because it is structured. Structure is a claim about form, and the edge is still a model's guess.

  6. How would you evaluate whether the graph helped?

    On the questions it was built for, which means multi-hop questions that cross documents. Comparing on single-hop lookups will show it is no better, because it is not meant to be. And the comparison has to include index cost, since that is the thing being traded away.

    Red flagRunning a generic RAG benchmark. Most of those are single-hop, so they measure the case the graph was not built for.

  7. Linear projector or something cleverer?

    A linear map is the simplest and it works surprisingly well. The alternative is a resampler with learned queries, which outputs a fixed number of tokens whatever the image size. You pick the second when context budget is the binding constraint, which on a device it always is.

    Red flagChoosing on quality alone. The reason resamplers exist is the token bill, not accuracy.

  8. Somebody asks three questions about the same image. What can you avoid?

    Recomputing the image tokens and their attention state. The image sits at the front of the prompt, so it is a shared prefix. Its key-value cache can be reused across all three turns. That is the prefix-caching argument from any long system prompt, and here the prefix is enormous.

    Red flagRe-encoding per question. The encoder pass is the cheap half. The prefill over hundreds of image tokens is the expensive half.

  9. What is the characteristic failure of a multimodal model?

    Describing what is plausibly in an image rather than what is in it. Ask about small text, or a detail at the edge. It will answer confidently from the prior. And it has no good way to say the image is too low-resolution to tell.

    Red flagCalling it hallucination and moving on. The specific shape here is the prior overriding the pixels, and it gets worse as resolution drops.

  10. Why those three topics?

    They are the three walls the division's work was about to hit. Models too big for the device. Retrieval that could not answer across documents. Inputs that were not text. A session is worth running when people already have the problem and no name for it.

    Red flagAnswering that they were interesting. A talk chosen by interest gets attendance, and a talk chosen by an existing problem gets used.

  11. How many sessions, and how many people?

    This needs a real number and the bullet does not have one. “Multiple” and “division-wide” are both doing work that a count would do better. Three sessions to forty engineers is a specific claim. The vaguer version reads as larger and is believed less.

    Red flagLeaving it vague on purpose. A number that sounds modest beats an adjective that sounds inflated.

  12. Did anything change because of them?

    The honest answer is the one to give, whatever it is. If somebody tried layer pruning afterwards, that is the sentence worth having. If nothing traceable happened, say the sessions were awareness and not adoption. Claiming influence you cannot point at is the failure mode here.

    Red flagImplying adoption. It is the easiest claim to make and the easiest to unravel with one follow-up.

Layer pruning speeds a model up where zeroing individual weights often does not. Why?

  1. Dropping whole blocks means fewer layers actually run
  2. Sparse weights need hardware support to pay off
  3. Removing parameters always makes a model run faster
  4. Smaller checkpoints load from disk more quickly now

The blocks are gone, so they are never executed. Memory, prefill, per-token latency and the key-value cache all drop by the same fraction, and nothing in the stack has to cooperate for that to happen.

The second option is true and it is the reason the other method disappoints, not the reason this one works. Load time is a one-off and has nothing to do with per-token cost.

When does knowledge-graph RAG earn its much higher indexing cost?

  1. When the answer connects facts across several documents
  2. When the corpus outgrows a single embedding index
  3. When exact keyword matching returns too many results
  4. When the documents change faster than nightly rebuilds

Chunk retrieval answers what a corpus says about one thing, and it is bad at how two things connect, because that answer sits in no single passage. A graph gives those questions somewhere to be answered from.

A corpus that changes fast is an argument against a graph rather than for one, since the extraction pass has to be redone. Index size and keyword noise are both solved more cheaply elsewhere.

Edges and exceptions

Half of these are about teaching and half are about the content. The interviewer will move between them without warning, which is the point.

  • A hostile question
  • Not knowing
  • A mixed room
  • Pruning a small model
  • A graph that is wrong
  • Video
  • Teaching against yourself
  • What you would change

  1. Somebody in the room challenges the technique, publicly and correctly.

    Concede it and use it. A correct challenge is the best material in the session. It is the “where it breaks” move arriving free, with more credibility than you could give it. Defending a technique you are teaching is how a session turns into a sales pitch.

    Red flagDeflecting to a follow-up conversation. The room learns more from the disagreement than from the slide it interrupted.

  2. You are asked something you do not know.

    Say so, say what you would check, and move on. This matters more in a session than in a meeting, because the room is calibrating how much to trust everything else you said. One admitted gap makes the rest more believable rather than less.

    Red flagReasoning out loud towards a guess. In a teaching context that is indistinguishable from an answer, and it gets repeated.

  3. Half the room has built with LLMs and half has not.

    Pitch the mechanism at the half that has not, and put the depth in the questions. That way the experienced half gets what they came for through the discussion rather than the slides. A talk aimed at the middle reliably loses both ends.

    Red flagSplitting the difference. The middle of a mixed room is nobody, and aiming there is how you lose everyone at once.

  4. Would you layer-prune a model that is already small?

    Much more carefully. The redundancy this relies on comes from depth, and a small model has less of it to give. The measured distances will be larger everywhere, which is the method telling you there is no cheap run to cut. Quantization is the better lever there.

    Red flagApplying the published ratios. Those came from large models and the redundancy does not scale down with the parameter count.

  5. The graph contains a relation that is simply false.

    Then the system will answer from it with structural confidence, which is worse than a wrong chunk. The mitigation is to keep the source text attached to every edge and cite it. An edge you cannot trace back to a sentence should not be usable in an answer.

    Red flagProposing a validation pass by another model. It is the same extractor grading itself, which is the problem from chapter 0012 in a different costume.

  6. Does any of the multimodal story change for video?

    The token problem stops being a problem and becomes the problem. Every frame is an image, so a few seconds of video exceeds any context you have. Everything interesting about video models is therefore about which frames to keep, which is a sampling question rather than an architecture one.

    Red flagTreating video as more images. It is a different problem because the cost grows with duration and the context does not.

  7. You are teaching something you would not use yourself.

    Then say that, and say why, and the session gets better. Knowledge-graph RAG is a good example. It is genuinely worth knowing and it is the wrong choice for most retrieval problems. A session that ends with “here is when not to use this” is the one people trust next time.

    Red flagTeaching it as a recommendation. Explaining a technique and endorsing it are different acts, and blurring them costs you the next session.

  8. What would you change?

    Put a number in the bullet, since “multiple” and “division-wide” both read as vaguer than the truth. Name one thing that happened afterwards, if one did. And keep a page of notes per session, because the bullet is tested by being asked to teach the content a year later.

    Red flagAdding more topics. Three is already more surface area than most people can defend cold.

Probes to expect

  1. “Teach me layer pruning.” The most likely reply to this bullet. Two minutes, one mechanism, one cost.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Delete whole transformer blocks from a trained model, then fine-tune briefly to recover. It works because the middle blocks change the hidden state very little, and you can measure that directly by comparing the state going into a run of blocks with the state coming out. Small angle means those blocks are doing little, so cut a contiguous run from the deeper part and keep the last one or two. Then heal with a low-rank fine-tune, which is cheap and is part of the method rather than polish. The speedup is free because the blocks never execute, and the cost is that multi-step reasoning falls over long before factual recall does.

  2. “Why not just prune weights?” A good follow-up, and the answer is a hardware one that most candidates miss.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Zeroing individual weights leaves a matrix the same size with the same number of operations. You only get a speedup if the hardware can skip the zeros, and outside of a few structured sparsity patterns it cannot. Layer pruning has no such dependency. The blocks are gone, so memory, prefill, per-token latency and the key-value cache all drop by the same fraction on any hardware. That is the reason it is the first structured pruning to reach for.

  3. “When is a knowledge graph actually worth it?” Checking whether you can argue against a technique you taught.
    Model answer — say yours first

    When the questions cross documents. Chunk retrieval answers what a corpus says about one thing and is bad at how two things connect, because that answer is in no single passage. The graph gives those questions somewhere to be answered from. But most questions are single-hop, and the cost lands at index time as a model pass over the whole corpus that has to be redone when the corpus changes. If nobody is asking multi-hop questions, that is a large bill for nothing.

  4. “How does an image get into a language model?” Straightforward, and the token cost is the half that shows engineering rather than reading.
    Model answer — say yours first

    A vision encoder turns the image into vectors and a small projector maps them into the language model's token space. After that the model cannot tell them from words. Training is staged: freeze both big parts and train only the projector on image-caption pairs, then instruction-tune. The consequence people miss is that an image costs tokens, often hundreds, and more at higher resolution. They fill the context and they all go through prefill, which is why resamplers exist. They emit a fixed token count whatever the image size.

  5. “How do you know the sessions were useful?” The question the bullet is actually about. Honesty here is worth more than a metric.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Not from attendance, and not from a feedback form. The signal I trust is somebody coming back weeks later having tried it, or arguing with me about it, because both mean the idea survived the walk back to their desk. I would describe the outcome as awareness rather than adoption. If I could point at a thing that shipped because of a session I would say so, and I would rather give you the lower claim than one that unravels on the next question.

Chapter 0010 put layer pruning in a particular category. Which, and what does this chapter add to it?

Taught but never deployed. Chapter 0010 flagged that no pruned model appears in any shipped bullet, so the claim has to be stated as teaching or it reads as production experience. What this chapter adds is the content behind it, because a teaching claim is tested by being asked to teach. The selection rule, the healing step, and the fact that the speedup is free are what make the teaching claim defensible.

Chapter 0010 · shipped, taught, or neither
Chapter 0006 gave a rule about what a schema can and cannot do. Apply it to an extracted knowledge graph.

A schema constrains the shape of an output and never its value. A graph is the same argument made larger. The nodes and edges are well-formed by construction, which says nothing about whether the relation is in the source text. A fabricated edge is worse than a bad chunk, because a chunk sits next to its own text and an edge is an assertion. The mitigation is to keep the source sentence attached to every edge and refuse to use one that cannot be traced back.
