0001 · Owning a Vendor-Evaluation Platform

From lesson 0001.

  1. “Who does the arithmetic — the model or your code?” The highest-signal question in this chapter, and it is asked of every AI-scoring system. A candidate who lets the model produce the final number has built something unauditable and usually has not noticed.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The model proposes a band and an integer with evidence quotes. Everything downstream is code: clamp into the model's own band, drop unasked questions, exclude rather than zero an unanswered one, unweighted mean per KPI, then two totals. The reason is that a vendor may lose a contract over this number and will ask why — so it has to decompose into a question, a band, and a line of prose in a named rubric version.

  2. “Why is the research pass blind? You have the transcript right there.” Tests whether you think adversarially about your own users. The performance-flavoured answer is wrong and reveals you have not.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Because if every search is derived from what they said, the vendor picks the evidence base by choosing what to claim, and anything they never mention is never checked. The blind pass is the half they cannot steer — it is what finds the controversy nobody raised.

  3. “What happens if your search provider is down?” They are usually fishing for “we retry.” The real answer here is much more interesting and you should volunteer it.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The interview still gets its scorecard, because the Scout is deadline-wrapped and soft-fails — research failing must never cost a scorecard. The uncomfortable part is what the report looks like: confident and empty, because search soft-fails to no results, and nothing downstream can distinguish that from a vendor with no public footprint. It has to be caught at the client, because by the time it is a report the information is gone.

  4. “Your system has a model judging claims. How do you know the judge is right?” The question that follows “LLM-as-a-judge” every single time. Have a metric that needs no human labels ready, because that is what separates a measured system from a hopeful one.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Three label-free metrics from a replay harness that fixes the evidence and varies only the model: citations naming a URL not in the evidence, verified claims lacking independent support, and verdict instability across repeated runs of the same arm. That last one is how we found that roughly a fifth of verdicts flip on identical evidence — a result we would never have got from eyeballing outputs.

  5. “How would you take this to a hundred times the volume?” Watch for the reflex answer. The bottleneck here is not compute, and naming the right one before proposing anything is most of the grade.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Outbound research breaks first, not the pods or the database — every interview fans out to third-party search and fetch with their own rate limits. More replicas multiply exactly the thing that is constrained. So: admission control on the pipeline, a shared budget across replicas rather than per-process caps, and probably an evidence cache keyed on the vendor, since the blind pass for one company is identical across interviews.

0002 · The Gateway and the Move to Streaming

From lesson 0002.

  1. “You said 2.2 s median. Give me p95 and p99.” The most predictable follow-up on your entire résumé. A median quoted against a hard budget is what people say when the tail embarrasses them — and here it does not, so leading with the tail is free.
    Model answer — say yours first

    p95 4703 ms, p99 5205, max 5678, against p50 2504 and p90 4468 — 86 Host turns across six interviews. And the honest caveats, both of which I would rather give you than have you find: that is a dev-window sample, and a deliberate two-second settle beat is inside every one of those numbers.

  2. “Walk me through the Gemini Live integration.” Asked innocently, and there is no good improvised answer if you let the résumé's wording stand. Correct it in the first sentence.
    Model answer — say yours first

    I should correct my own wording — it is SSE over Gemini's OpenAI-compatible REST endpoint with stream set true, not the bidirectional Live API. The change was buffered REST to streaming REST. The live-media piece in the product is the avatar vendor's SDK on the frontend, which is a different layer from our inference path.

  3. “Is there anything in this system you made deliberately slower?” Rare and extremely revealing. Most candidates have nothing. You have the best possible answer, so do not let this one pass.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The settle beat, from half a second to two. It stops the Host interrupting a vendor who was only pausing mid-thought. It is the second-largest bar in the latency breakdown, it is inside every number I quote, and deleting it tomorrow would improve my median and make the product worse.

  4. “Tell me about a bug that took too long to find.” Tell it as an observability failure, not a coding one. The insight — that a 100% failure rate can be quieter than a rare one — is what makes it a senior answer.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Four days of every interview running slow. Model aliases plus a substring test meant every turn was misdiagnosed as a model outage, silently retried on the fallback, and still returned 200. 236 warnings, a hundred percent of turns, no alert — because nothing alerts on warnings. I pinned the models and split one retry policy into three explicit failure classes, and I would now alert on fallback-model usage directly.

  5. “How do you ship a new version with live interviews in progress?” The stateful-deploy question. The trap is that this service looks stateless per request, so the wrong answer is very natural.
    Model answer — say yours first

    A turn is short but a session is long and state is server-side per interview, so a plain rolling update drops conversations. The requirement is session draining — stop admitting new interviews to a pod, let the in-flight ones finish, then cycle. The migrate job already gates the release on schema, and version pinning per session is the next thing I would add.

0003 · Five-Stage Multi-Agent Schematization

From lesson 0003.

  1. “How do you know the generated mapping is correct?” The question this whole bullet exists to answer, and the one most candidates answer with a confidence score. The interviewer is listening for whether an oracle exists and whether you used it.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Two different checks, because they answer different questions. Validation runs the mapping through the real processor as a subprocess and asks whether data rows came out. That is a well-formedness guarantee and it is deterministic. Evaluation compares the result against a hand-written mapping and gives PV accuracy, which is the semantic check. A dataset with no hand-written mapping can only ever get the first one.

  2. “Why is it agents rather than five functions?” The standard challenge to any multi-agent claim. A candidate who cannot answer it has usually adopted a framework rather than chosen one.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The five stages genuinely are a sequence and could be functions. The loop is where the agent framework earns itself. Ten sub-agents run in order, any of them can raise escalate to break out, and session state carries the feedback between iterations. I would be honest that the outer pipeline is orchestration and the framework matters most inside the loop.

  3. “Your accuracy on some domains is under three percent. Is this working?” Designed to see whether you defend, deflect, or explain. The analysis makes explaining available, which is rare and worth using.
    Model answer — say yours first

    On those domains it is producing a weak draft rather than a good one, and I know why. I tested seven structural hypotheses and all of them failed. The driver is coverage of that domain in the target vocabulary, which is a 39.3% against 2.6% gap. No prompt fixes a missing property. The fix is schema coverage, and until then the pipeline should flag the tier rather than hand over a draft silently.

  4. “Where did three hours to thirty minutes come from?” The number in the bullet, so it will be asked. The only losing move is to invent a methodology on the spot.
    Model answer — say yours first

    It is the team's estimate of the hand-mapping work it replaces, not a study I ran. What I measured is about 45 seconds of processing per dataset, PV accuracy against ground truth, and a comparison against raw Gemini at 26.8% versus 8.1%. I would rather give you the measured numbers and be clear which one is an estimate.

  5. “You said the ground truth was broken. Does that not invalidate your benchmark?” A good interviewer will push on this, because it is the strongest thing you said and they want to see whether you overclaim from it.
    Model answer — say yours first

    It weakens it in both directions, and I would not use it only as an excuse. Twenty-eight of 49 answer keys validate, and human-written maps pass at about 20%. That means our accuracy understates the pipeline on some datasets, and it also means no single percentage from this benchmark is settled. The right next step is cleaning the keys, which is cheap, before doing anything else with the numbers.

0004 · Hybrid Deterministic-LLM Lineage Extraction

From lesson 0004.

  1. “99.2% of what, exactly?” The single most likely question on this bullet, because the number is the most specific thing in it. A candidate who cannot decompose a metric they quoted is assumed to have inherited it.
    Model answer — say yours first

    A weighted composite over four dimensions. Node accuracy and edge accuracy at 0.25 each, field mapping accuracy at 0.30, structural accuracy at 0.20, with a pass threshold of 0.85. Inside the field term there are three sub-metrics: count accuracy, a cosine over the mapping-type distribution, and a sample match rate. So it is not 99.2% of columns individually verified, and I would rather say that than have you find it.

  2. “How do you stop the model hallucinating a column that does not exist?” Asked of every LLM extraction system. Most answers are about prompting or validation. The strong answer is that the model was never given that job.
    Model answer — say yours first

    By not asking it. The field mapping tables are parsed deterministically by code, so the 516 mappings are extracted rather than generated. There is no step where a model proposes a column name. The model only handles object identification and cross-reference classification, and those answers pass guardrails before they reach the graph.

  3. “Why is this agentic at all? It sounds like a parser and one model call.” A fair challenge, and the honest answer is more impressive than a defence. This is where overclaiming gets punished.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Largely fair. It is a two-stage sequential agent, and the framework is buying tool orchestration and state rather than autonomy. The one place the agent framing earns itself is the before-tool callback, which is a framework hook I used to make a precondition unskippable. I would not describe this as a swarm, and the résumé does not.

  4. “Tell me about a failure this system had.” An invitation. The zero-mappings bug is the best answer in the chapter because it is silent, specific, and fixed structurally.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Some runs produced a graph with zero field mappings and no error at all. The cause was tool-calling order: the model sometimes called merge before the deterministic extraction had run, so there was nothing to merge. The result looked complete, which is the dangerous kind. I fixed it with a before-tool callback that runs extraction if it has not run, rather than with prompt wording.

  5. “Five documents is a small evaluation set. Does the number mean anything?” Testing whether you will defend a sample size you should concede. Conceding it and saying what it is good for is the winning move.
    Model answer — say yours first

    It means something narrow. Five documents with hand-written expected outputs is a regression gate: if a change moves the composite below 0.85 on those five, something broke. It is not a population estimate, and I would not present it as one. To claim general accuracy I would need sampled per-mapping verification across many more documents, which is the first thing I would build next.

0005 · Agentic Pipeline-Failure Debugging

From lesson 0005.

  1. “What stops this from breaking production?” The question every autonomous system gets. The gating here is genuinely good, so the only way to lose is to be vague about it.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Four gates, in order. The screener has to route to investigate rather than retry or overlook. The solution has to come back with its action field set to apply rather than manual intervention required. The action router has to agree. And a feature flag has to be on, with a threshold that defaults to a single retrigger per failure. I would also tell you what is missing: a validation layer exists and its flag defaults to off.

  2. “In parallel? Walk me through the dispatcher.” If the interviewer is technical they will ask this, and the résumé's wording does not survive it. Volunteer the correction before the question arrives.
    Model answer — say yours first

    I would correct my own wording there. The dispatcher branches on USE_LEAD_MANAGER, which defaults to true. On that path it is one agent with five tools, reasoning across them in sequence, capped at ten iterations. The other branch uses a three-worker thread pool over confluence, code and file, which is genuinely parallel, and it is not the default. Infrastructure is a tool rather than a crew in either case.

  3. “Which of your three categories is the dangerous one?” A good question, and the answer reveals whether you have thought about asymmetric error costs or just built a classifier.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Overlook. A wrong retry is largely self-correcting, because the job runs again and fails again, and the threshold bounds it. A wrong overlook closes the incident with no action and no rerun, so a real failure disappears and downstream jobs break for reasons nobody connects back to it. If I could measure one thing about this system it would be overlook precision.

  4. “Your retriever always returns five results. What if nothing is actually similar?” Tests whether you know how top-k behaves. Most candidates assume a relevance filter that is not there.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Then it returns the five least dissimilar things, and the screener still has to choose a category. There is no similarity floor and no nothing-matched branch, which is a real gap. The fix I would want is a distance threshold below which it routes to investigate by default, so an unfamiliar failure fails towards spending money rather than towards silence.

  5. “Twenty pipelines fail at once. What does your system do?” A scale question disguised as a correctness question. The honest answer names a missing feature.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Twenty independent investigations, because nothing correlates them. Each sees only its own log. They will mostly reach the same conclusion at twenty times the cost, and twenty retriggers may hit an upstream that is still down. The shape I would add is clustering failures inside a time window before triage, so one investigation covers the incident and the rest reference it.

0006 · Layered Validation on an Autonomous Path

From lesson 0006.

  1. “What does EXPLAIN actually prove?” The highest-signal question in this chapter. Most candidates say it validates the query and cannot go further, which reveals the check was adopted rather than understood.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Two things: the query parses, and it binds. Every table exists, every column exists on the table it is attributed to, and the predicate types are compatible. The planner is the authority so the check cannot go stale. It proves nothing about meaning, because it never runs. A query can plan perfectly and return entirely the wrong rows, and that is the honest limit of the layer.

  2. “Can a fix pass all your checks and still break production?” Testing whether you will claim safety you do not have. The right answer names the missing axis rather than defending the existing layers.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Yes, and the realistic case is an over-broad predicate. A statement that parses, binds and touches ten million rows instead of ten passes every layer here, because every layer asks whether the fix is well-formed and none asks how much it changes. The missing layer is blast radius: estimate affected rows before applying. That is the one I would add next.

  3. “How do you prevent SQL injection?” A security-minded interviewer will ask, and the résumé wording points them at the weaker control. Lead with the allowlist.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The barrier is an allowlist: the connector path executes only SELECT and EXPLAIN, so anything else never runs whatever it contains. Behind that there is a keyword denylist for DROP, DELETE, TRUNCATE, ALTER and CREATE, and I would not rely on that half, because a denylist only covers what somebody remembered. I would also correct my own bullet, which names table-name checks.

  4. “Your evaluation scores generated code by similarity. Is that sound?” A sharp question. The strong answer concedes it and connects to machinery already in the system.
    Model answer — say yours first

    For the prose fields it is defensible, since an investigation has no exact match to test against. For the corrected-code field it is the weakest part of the harness. We already have an oracle on the production path: EXPLAIN for SQL and a parse for Python. Scoring the code field with the validator instead of a cosine would make that part deterministic, and it is reusing code that already exists.

  5. “Was this validation actually on?” If the interviewer has read the config, this is coming. Getting there first is worth more than any answer you can give afterwards.
    Model answer — say yours first

    ENABLE_PIPELINE_VALIDATIONS defaults to false, so on a default deployment the layer is not in the path. That is the ship-dark pattern and it is a reasonable way to introduce a check that can block work, but leaving it there is not. Worth separating what was still live: the statement allowlist is an independent control and it is always on, so the destructive path stayed closed even with validation off.

0007 · Execution Forecasting and Dependency Resolution

From lesson 0007.

  1. “What happens to a job that only runs on weekdays?” The single most likely question on this bullet. Anyone who has run a scheduler thinks of it within seconds, and getting there first is the whole performance.
    Model answer — say yours first

    It predicts a weekend, and I would rather tell you than be asked. The intervals are one, one, one, one, three, and their weighted mean is about 1.4, rounding to one. Friday plus one is Saturday. An interval average has no calendar in it, so it cannot express “not at weekends.” That is exactly the gap a seasonality-aware method fills, and it is why the design has a second strategy slot.

  2. “Why alpha equals 0.5?” Testing whether a parameter was chosen or inherited. Conceding it and describing the experiment is stronger than any justification.
    Model answer — say yours first

    It was a default rather than a derived value, and I would not pretend otherwise. What it buys is fast adaptation: the newest gap carries half the weight, so a genuine cadence change is tracked in a few runs, at the cost of being pulled around by one unusual gap. To justify it properly I would backtest, holding out each pipeline's last run and measuring error in days across several alphas. The history is already there, so it is about a day of work.

  3. “Tell me about the Prophet part.” A direct question about a named library in a bracket. There is one honest answer and it is better than the dishonest one.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Prophet is in the design and not in the implementation. There is an analysis-method enum and a branch that dispatches to _analyze_with_prophet, and that method is never defined. EWMA is the default and the only one that runs. The reason the slot exists is the weekday failure, which needs seasonality rather than a better average. I would also say a day-of-week histogram would cover most of that case far more cheaply than the library would.

  4. “What is the worst thing a wrong prediction causes?” Looking for whether you understand the asymmetry. The design already leans one way and you should be able to say which.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Running something that should not have run. A missed prediction is recoverable because the due test is greater than or equal, so anything overdue stays on the plan until it runs. A spurious prediction puts real work into production early, and for a pipeline with side effects that is the expensive direction. The parent check exists to stop the most likely version of that, which is a child being scheduled when its parent will trigger it anyway.

  5. “A pipeline gets decommissioned. What does your planner do?” A quiet question with an uncomfortable answer, and the uncomfortable answer is the right one.
    Model answer — say yours first

    It keeps predicting it forever. The last interval still exists, the predicted date passes, and because the test is greater than or equal it stays due permanently. So the plan slowly accumulates dead pipelines. The same property that rescues a missed run is what creates the ghosts. The fix is a staleness rule: if the last run is several intervals in the past, stop predicting and surface it for review instead.

0008 · Languages: Python, SQL, C++

From lesson 0008.

  1. “Tell me about your C++.” The most predictable question on the whole skills section, because it is the one item with no project behind it. Being unprepared for a predictable question is the expensive part.
    Model answer — say yours first

    It is the weakest of the three and I would rather tell you than have you find it. It comes from academic and on-device work rather than production services, and it is at the level of reading and modifying rather than designing. The concept I am genuinely confident about is RAII, partly because Python's context managers are the same idea. If this role is C++ heavy, that is worth establishing now.

  2. “What happens if you do CPU work inside an async handler?” The standard test for whether someone has operated an async service or only written one. The wrong answer is that only that request is affected.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Every other request in the process waits. The loop is one thread and a coroutine holds it until it awaits, so two hundred milliseconds of computation is two hundred milliseconds where nothing else progresses, including requests that were nearly done. The tell in production is latency rising on endpoints that have nothing to do with the slow one. The fix is a thread for a blocking call, or a process for real CPU work.

  3. “How would you enforce that only one version of a record can be live?” A design question with an application-level answer and a database-level answer. Only one of them survives concurrency.
    Model answer — say yours first

    A partial unique index, in the database. A unique index over only the rows where the status is live means the second insert fails rather than succeeding. The application-level version is a check then an insert, and two concurrent requests can both pass the check. That is exactly how the rubric model does it, and there is a second partial index for drafts.

  4. “Give me a SQL bug you have actually shipped.” Fishing for whether your SQL is real. A NULL answer is more convincing than a performance one, because performance bugs are caught in review and NULL bugs are not.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The NULL family, because they read as correct. A NOT IN against a subquery that contains a single NULL returns no rows at all, and nothing errors. The query looks right, the result is empty, and empty reads as “no matches” rather than as a bug. It is the same failure shape as a research step that soft-fails and returns a confident empty report.

  5. “Rate your Python out of ten.” A question with no good numeric answer. Replacing the scale with a boundary is both more useful and more senior.
    Model answer — say yours first

    I would rather give you a boundary than a number, because the scale is not shared. I am comfortable with async services, the concurrency model, the data layer and testing. I am weaker on C extensions and interpreter internals, and I have never needed to profile at that level. That tells you where I would be productive immediately and where I would need time.

0009 · LLM and Agents

From lesson 0009.

  1. “What is an agent, to you?” The opening question of most LLM loops. A vague answer sets the level for everything after it, and vagueness here is very common.
    Model answer — say yours first

    A model in a loop with tools. It is given typed functions, it proposes a call, my code executes it and returns the result, and it decides again. Without the loop and the tools it is a prompt. The part I would emphasise is that the model never executes anything itself. It emits a request and my process decides whether to honour it, which is where validation, logging and refusal all live.

  2. “Why is that multi-agent rather than three function calls?” The standard challenge, and the one where conceding beats defending. The restriction is the impressive part.
    Model answer — say yours first

    In the vendor platform it genuinely is three function calls with one composer, on purpose. Each stage has its own prompt, schema and model tier, which is what makes them worth separating. What it deliberately is not is autonomous: agents never call each other. That restriction is what gives one readable control flow, one place for a deadline, and a stage you can test alone. Agents-calling-agents was rejected, not unavailable.

  3. “How do you stop an agent doing something dangerous?” Testing whether you think about containment or about prompting. Only one of those answers holds up.
    Model answer — say yours first

    By controlling what tools exist, because that bounds what is reachable at all. Then by executing every call in my own code, where I can validate, log or refuse. Then by structured output, so a dangerous instruction has no shape to arrive in. Prompt wording is the weakest layer and I would not lead with it. In the debugger, the strongest control on generated SQL is an allowlist permitting only SELECT and EXPLAIN.

  4. “Which of these models have you actually used in production?” A direct audit of the second half of the line. Two of the four need qualifying and getting there first is worth a lot.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Gemini extensively, across every CloudSufi system, including a bake-off between its tiers on a fixed evidence payload. Claude in the pipeline debugger's crews. GPT I would qualify: I built an OpenAI-compatible gateway, so I know the API contract deeply, but the model behind it was Gemini. Llama has no project behind it and I would take it off the line.

  5. “Where did you decide not to use an agent?” A senior question, and a gift. It is the one place where restraint is the whole answer.
    Model answer — say yours first

    In the lineage agent, for the field mappings. Those come out of structured tables, so a parser is correct where a model would be plausible, and the mappings are extracted rather than generated. Also in the schematization pipeline's validation step, which is a subprocess rather than a judge model, because an oracle exists. The rule I use is that a model should only be asked questions with no deterministic answer.

0010 · Model Optimization

From lesson 0010.

  1. “How much memory does a 7B model need at 4-bit?” A direct arithmetic check. It takes five seconds and it instantly separates people who have deployed from people who have read.
    Model answer — say yours first

    About 3.5 gigabytes for the weights, since four-bit is half a byte per parameter. Then the KV cache on top, which is two for keys and values, times layers, times key-value heads, times head dimension, times bytes per element, per token. On a long conversation that can rival the weights. The trap is using attention heads rather than key-value heads, which overstates it by four or more under grouped-query attention.

  2. “Why does quantization speed things up?” Testing whether you understand the bottleneck. The wrong answer is that the arithmetic is faster, and it is very common.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Bandwidth. Generating a token means reading essentially every weight, and modern accelerators can do far more arithmetic than they can be fed. Halving the bytes per weight roughly halves the data moved per token, so throughput follows. On a device the effect is stronger still, because there is one user and no batching to amortise the weight read across sequences.

  3. “What did quantization cost you in accuracy?” Looking for whether you measured it and on what. A general benchmark answer is the weak one here.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The honest answer is that the damage is uneven, so the number depends entirely on what you measure. Fluency survives low bit widths well, which is dangerous because the model still sounds right. What degrades is precise discrimination, and choosing between fifty similar functions is exactly that. So the evaluation had to be the function-selection task rather than perplexity, which barely moves.

  4. “Tell me about the distillation you did.” The trap question on this line, because a plausible-sounding wrong answer is sitting right there on the same résumé.
    Model answer — say yours first

    I have not shipped model distillation, and I would rather say that than reach for something adjacent. I understand it: a student trained against the teacher's softened output distribution rather than hard labels, where the value is in what the teacher thought was nearly right. The pipeline on my réésumé that distilled 150 gigabytes was corpus curation, which shares the word and not the operation.

  5. “Pruning or quantization, if you had to pick one?” A judgment question. The right answer considers the training budget, which most candidates skip entirely.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Quantization, almost always first. Sixteen-bit to four-bit is exactly a factor of four, it needs no training run, and it is reversible if the accuracy is unacceptable. Pruning removes blocks and then needs a healing fine-tune, because the remaining blocks were trained expecting the ones you deleted. So the deletion is trivial and recovering the accuracy is not, which makes it the second thing to try rather than the first.

0011 · Infrastructure

From lesson 0011.

  1. “Why Kubernetes? It sounds like this would run on Cloud Run.” A challenge disguised as a question, and a gift, because the predecessor did run on Cloud Run and moved for a nameable reason.
    Model answer — say yours first

    It did, and the proof of concept stayed there. What moved it was session lifetime. The product holds long-lived streaming connections during an interview and keeps session state per interview on the server, so a deploy or a scale-down can cut somebody off mid-sentence. The release also bundles a migration job that must finish before new pods serve. Our other service, the schematization pipeline, is stateless and is still on Cloud Run for exactly that reason.

  2. “How would you scale this to ten times the traffic?” Most candidates answer with replicas. The first three bottlenecks here are all external, and more pods make one of them worse.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Not by adding pods first. The first limit is outbound research: every interview fans out to third-party search and fetch APIs with their own rate limits, and nothing caps how many interviews are in flight, so more pods make that worse. Second is the connection pool, especially anywhere a session is held across a model call. Third is the model provider's quota. Admission control at the pipeline level comes before any horizontal scaling.

  3. “What is missing from your architecture?” Sometimes asked directly, more often implied. Having the list ready converts an audit into a conversation.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Four things. No messaging layer, so post-interview scoring is an in-process background task and it is lost if the pod dies. No cache, although the blind research pass is identical across interviews with the same vendor. One region and one Postgres with no replicas, so no read scaling and no failover. And no identity model beyond a proxy and a service key, on a platform that is multi-tenant by nature. The queue is the one I would add first, because it closes a correctness gap rather than a performance one.

  4. “How is tenant isolation enforced?” The sharpest of the four gaps, because the product is inherently multi-tenant and the enforcement is conventional rather than structural.
    Model answer — say yours first

    In application code, by scoping every query to a workspace, with permission and tenancy tests covering it. What does not exist is enforcement below that, like row-level security in Postgres. So a query written without the filter is a data leak with only a test in the way. I would argue for making the boundary structural rather than conventional, because that is the difference between catching most mistakes and catching all of them.

  5. “What would a deploy look like during a live interview?” A specific operational question that a candidate who only deployed once will not have thought about.
    Model answer — say yours first

    It needs session draining rather than a rolling update. Stop admitting new interviews to a pod, let the ones in progress finish, then cycle it. A turn is short and a session is long, so a standard rollout terminates somebody mid-interview. The migrate job already handles the schema ordering. What the release does not have is a drain step, and version pinning per session is the pattern I would reach for next.

0012 · RAG and Evaluation

From lesson 0012.

  1. “How do you evaluate an LLM system?” The central question of a senior AI loop. Most candidates answer with methodology. You can answer with three experiments and what each one changed.
    Model answer — say yours first

    I start by asking whether an oracle exists. Three of my systems have one and use it: a subprocess running the real processor, a query planner explaining generated SQL, and a parser rather than a model on structured tables. Where none exists, three things I actually ran. A bake-off replaying one fixed evidence payload through the real code path with only the model swapped, on label-free metrics. An A/B on transcript window size at 10, 20 and 40 entries with eight runs an arm. And an audit of my own ground truth, which found only 28 of 49 reference files were valid.

  2. “How do you know your judge model is right?” The standard follow-up to any LLM-as-a-judge claim. The strong answer includes direction of error, which most people never measure.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Three angles, none complete. Agreement with a human on a sample. Self-consistency across repeated runs, which for us is about one claim in five flipping on identical evidence. And the direction the errors go, which the bake-off measured: the pro model was more conservative, and the cheaper one erred towards calling unverifiable things partially supported. That last direction is the dangerous one, and it is why the tier choice was not just about cost.

  3. “Tell me about your hybrid search implementation.” A direct audit of an item with nothing behind it. Conceding and redirecting to where it would help is the only good outcome.
    Model answer — say yours first

    I have not built one, and I would rather say that than stretch. My systems use web search in one place and vector retrieval in another, which is two mechanisms rather than a fused retriever. Where it would genuinely help is the debugger's incident screener: error logs contain exact tokens like codes and table names, and those are precisely what a pure vector search loses. A keyword arm fused with the vector arm would improve that routing decision.

  4. “Your retriever always returns five results. Is that a problem?” Tests whether you know how top-k behaves and whether you have thought about it in your own system.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Yes, and more than usual here, because those five feed a routing decision rather than an answer. Top-k always returns k, so a genuinely novel failure gets the five least dissimilar incidents and a confident category built on them. There is no similarity floor. What I would add is a distance threshold below which it routes to the expensive investigate branch by default, so an unfamiliar input fails towards spending money rather than towards silence.

  5. “What is the weakest part of your evaluation work?” An invitation to be honest. There is a specific, defensible answer that also shows you apply your own rule consistently.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The 368-scenario harness scoring generated code by embedding similarity. For the prose fields that is defensible, since an investigation has no exact match. For the corrected-code field it breaks my own rule, because the production path already validates SQL with EXPLAIN and Python with a parse. The oracle exists and the harness does not use it. That is the first thing I would change, and it is reusing code rather than writing any.

0013 · On-Device RAG over a Quantized MobileLLM

From lesson 0013.

  1. “RAG on a television? Retrieval over what?” The first question every time, because the word RAG sets an expectation of documents. The answer reframes the whole bullet.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Over the device's own function catalogue. It has more than fifty functions, each with a description and typed parameters, and all of that together is far more than a small on-device model can hold alongside the user's sentence. So the request is embedded, matched against the function descriptions, and only the nearest few go into the prompt. The corpus is the capabilities rather than any content.

  2. “What happens when it picks the wrong function?” The question that decides the conversation. The strong answer distinguishes two different failures before answering.
    Model answer — say yours first

    First I would separate two cases, because they need different fixes. Either the right function never reached the shortlist, which is a retrieval recall failure and no model quality recovers it. Or the shortlist was correct and the model chose badly, which is selection and it is exactly where quantization hurts. Then the response depends on reversibility: a wrong brightness change is trivially undone, and anything destructive should have required a confirmation regardless of confidence.

  3. “Why not just fine-tune a classifier over fifty intents?” A fair challenge with a genuinely strong alternative. Dismissing it is the weak move.
    Model answer — say yours first

    It is a strong baseline and it would be smaller and faster. The argument against it is change rather than accuracy. Every new function means retraining, and a television's catalogue grows with every firmware release. With retrieval, adding a function is writing a description and embedding it. It also generalises better to phrasings nobody anticipated, which a classifier trained on a fixed set handles poorly.

  4. “How do you stop it doing something destructive by mistake?” Looking for whether safety is a threshold or a property. The threshold answer is the common one and it is weaker.
    Model answer — say yours first

    By making reversibility a property of each function rather than a confidence cutoff. Anything destructive gets a confirmation step even when the model is confident, because a confident wrong answer is precisely the case that hurts. Anything trivially reversible acts immediately with a visible undo. A single global confidence threshold treats deleting a recording and changing brightness identically, which is the wrong design.

  5. “Someone asks for something the TV cannot do. What happens?” Tests whether you know top-k always returns k. Most candidates assume a relevance filter that is not there.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Without a similarity floor, retrieval returns the nearest few anyway and the model confidently performs the closest available action. That is worse than failing, because the person did not get what they asked for and something else changed. The fix is a distance threshold below which the honest answer is that the television cannot do it. Nothing in a top-k pipeline offers “none of these” unless you build it.

0014 · Agentic Journaling and Semantic Routing

From lesson 0014.

  1. “Semantic routing between what?” The first question, because the phrase is abstract. An answer about topics turns this into a classifier and loses the design.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Between modes rather than topics. Is the person asking what happened, asking what they should do, or asking for something planned. Those need different retrieval and produce different output shapes: a recollection cites the entry it came from, a plan is a structured itinerary. If one branch produced all three the schema would have to be loose enough to hold all of them, which means loose enough to be wrong in all of them.

  2. “How is this different from RAG over any document set?” The question that separates a generic answer from a designed one. The recency argument is specific and immediately convincing.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Time is part of relevance rather than metadata. Somebody asks for a suggestion and the most semantically similar entries are every restaurant they ever wrote about, including one from four years ago in a city they have left. In a document corpus that is a good match. Here it is wrong. So the score combines similarity with recency and with frequency, because something mentioned once is a data point and something mentioned monthly is a preference.

  3. “What makes it agentic?” A standard challenge. Conceding two of three branches is what makes the third claim land.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Only the planning branch, and I would rather say that than defend all of it. A recollection is one retrieval and one generation. A trip plan reads preferences, generates candidate options, then builds a schedule, and scheduling regularly invalidates a candidate and sends it back. That feedback is what makes the path depend on what is found, which is my test for whether something is genuinely an agent.

  4. “How would you evaluate personalisation?” Hard, and most candidates propose a labelled set. The strong answer separates what is checkable offline from what needs users.
    Model answer — say yours first

    There is no ground truth, because the correct answer is a fact about one person. What is checkable offline is grounding: did the retrieved entries actually support the suggestion made. That is a faithfulness measurement and it catches the worst failures. Quality needs real users, and the signal I would want is disagreement with the grounding rather than with the output, because that tells you which part was wrong.

  5. “What is the risk of a system like this?” Looking for product judgment rather than a technical answer. The word to reach for is intrusion.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Surfacing something from a period the person would rather not revisit, or recommending from a misreading of who they are. That failure is not inaccuracy, it is intrusion, and people stop using things that misunderstand them. No purely semantic system knows which period is painful, so the only honest mitigation is user control over which entries feed suggestions. That is a product feature rather than a model improvement.

0015 · Memory for a Dementia Companion

From lesson 0015.

  1. “Why MemGPT rather than just retrieval?” The opening question every time. An answer about bigger context loses the room immediately.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Because the model writes as well as reads. In retrieval the corpus is fixed and somebody else built it, so nothing the model produced goes back in. Here the model calls a function to save a fact, replace one that changed, or search older conversations, and the store grows out of the conversation. That is the feature, and it is also the risk, because a retrieval mistake is wrong once and a memory mistake is wrong from tomorrow onwards.

  2. “What stops it from remembering something wrong?” The follow-up that decides the conversation. The write-policy answer is specific and it lands.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Splitting memory by write policy rather than by lifetime. Stable facts about the person are read constantly and written rarely, and changing one needs a second mention in a different session or a caregiver confirming it. What happened is append-only and timestamped, so it can hold a contradiction without resolving it. Nothing in the system lets a single sentence overwrite a fact, because a single sentence cannot tell a person who moved house from a person who is confused.

  3. “How do you make sure the eight o'clock reminder actually happens?” Looking for whether you know which parts deserve a model at all.
    Model answer — say yours first

    By taking it out of the model entirely. Whether it is eight o'clock has one right answer, so it belongs in a table with a timer, not in memory that can be summarised away. The model phrases the reminder in the person's own terms and records what they said back. Acknowledgement goes into the same table rather than staying in the conversation, because a duplicate dose is a real harm and the record of it needs to be somewhere a scheduler can read.

  4. “They tell you something that contradicts what you stored. Now what?” The question that separates a product answer from a database answer. Do not resolve it.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Hold both. The stored fact stays, the contradiction is appended with a timestamp, and a caregiver sees it. I would not overwrite, because one confused sentence would then be the truth forever. And I would not correct them in the moment, because whether to orient somebody to reality or go along with them is a clinical judgment with genuine disagreement behind it. That is not a decision a prototype should be making by itself.

  5. “What does editing memory cost you at inference time?” A systems question inside an LLM question. Very few candidates connect the two.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The prefix cache. The always-present block of facts sits near the front of the prompt, so changing it invalidates every cached key and value after it and the whole conversation's prefix is recomputed. It is the most expensive edit in the system precisely because it is the earliest one. The practical consequence is to batch memory edits at turn boundaries rather than writing mid-turn, and to keep that block small.

0016 · A Pretraining Data Pipeline at 150&#8239;GB

From lesson 0016.

  1. “Why MinHash and not a hash?” The opening question. An answer about speed gets it backwards, because exact hashing is faster.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Because an exact hash only finds exact copies, and the problem on a wiki is near-copies. The same release note in four spaces, a page template with one field filled in differently, a document with a paragraph added. Every one of those hashes differently and they are all the same text. MinHash estimates how much two documents overlap as sets rather than testing whether they are equal, which is the property the problem actually needs.

  2. “Walk me through the dedup stage.” Where the union-find point earns its keep. Most answers stop at candidate pairs.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Cut each document into overlapping runs of words so it becomes a set. Hash that set many times, keep the minimum each time, and you have a signature whose positions agree as often as the sets overlap. Cut the signatures into bands and match whole bands to get candidate pairs cheaply. Then the part people skip: those pairs go into union-find, because a pair is a fact and a deletion is a decision. Group first, keep one per group under a stated rule, and the output stops depending on file order.

  3. “150 gigabytes went in. What came out?” Asked every time, and the absence of the number is what the interviewer notices.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Per stage rather than as a total, because the shape of the drop is what tells you anything. Quality filtering takes the largest bite, dedup takes the next, and repetition removal is usually small. If a pipeline keeps ninety per cent it barely ran, and if it keeps two per cent it probably deleted something it should not have. I would also show a sample of what each stage discarded, because reading the discarded pile is where the expensive mistakes turn up.

  4. “How did you know the filters were the right ones?” The question that separates borrowing fineWeb from understanding it.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The only real test is an ablation. Build the corpus with and without a filter, train a small model on each, and compare on a downstream eval. That is what fineWeb actually contributed, and taking the filter list without the ablation is taking the answer and leaving the reasoning. It matters more here than on a web crawl, because several C4 rules are wrong on an engineering wiki. Dropping pages with curly braces removes code blocks, which are the best content in there.

  5. “What stops a secret ending up in the training data?” Volunteer this one rather than waiting for it. It is the gap the word clean hides.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Nothing in these three stages, and I would raise it before being asked. A credential in a code block is unique, well-formed and not repetitive, so every filter I have keeps it. The same goes for pages about individual employees. Clean here means deduplicated and reasonably well-formed, and it does not mean safe to train on. Closing that gap needs a fourth stage doing secret scanning and entity detection, and it is the first thing I would add.

0017 · Teaching It: Pruning, KG-RAG, Multimodal

From lesson 0017.

  1. “Teach me layer pruning.” The most likely reply to this bullet. Two minutes, one mechanism, one cost.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Delete whole transformer blocks from a trained model, then fine-tune briefly to recover. It works because the middle blocks change the hidden state very little, and you can measure that directly by comparing the state going into a run of blocks with the state coming out. Small angle means those blocks are doing little, so cut a contiguous run from the deeper part and keep the last one or two. Then heal with a low-rank fine-tune, which is cheap and is part of the method rather than polish. The speedup is free because the blocks never execute, and the cost is that multi-step reasoning falls over long before factual recall does.

  2. “Why not just prune weights?” A good follow-up, and the answer is a hardware one that most candidates miss.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Zeroing individual weights leaves a matrix the same size with the same number of operations. You only get a speedup if the hardware can skip the zeros, and outside of a few structured sparsity patterns it cannot. Layer pruning has no such dependency. The blocks are gone, so memory, prefill, per-token latency and the key-value cache all drop by the same fraction on any hardware. That is the reason it is the first structured pruning to reach for.

  3. “When is a knowledge graph actually worth it?” Checking whether you can argue against a technique you taught.
    Model answer — say yours first

    When the questions cross documents. Chunk retrieval answers what a corpus says about one thing and is bad at how two things connect, because that answer is in no single passage. The graph gives those questions somewhere to be answered from. But most questions are single-hop, and the cost lands at index time as a model pass over the whole corpus that has to be redone when the corpus changes. If nobody is asking multi-hop questions, that is a large bill for nothing.

  4. “How does an image get into a language model?” Straightforward, and the token cost is the half that shows engineering rather than reading.
    Model answer — say yours first

    A vision encoder turns the image into vectors and a small projector maps them into the language model's token space. After that the model cannot tell them from words. Training is staged: freeze both big parts and train only the projector on image-caption pairs, then instruction-tune. The consequence people miss is that an image costs tokens, often hundreds, and more at higher resolution. They fill the context and they all go through prefill, which is why resamplers exist. They emit a fixed token count whatever the image size.

  5. “How do you know the sessions were useful?” The question the bullet is actually about. Honesty here is worth more than a metric.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Not from attendance, and not from a feedback form. The signal I trust is somebody coming back weeks later having tried it, or arguing with me about it, because both mean the idea survived the walk back to their desk. I would describe the outcome as awareness rather than adoption. If I could point at a thing that shipped because of a session I would say so, and I would rather give you the lower claim than one that unravels on the next question.

0018 · Voice-Only Commerce on ONDC

From lesson 0018.

  1. “What is ONDC, and why does it matter here?” Many interviewers outside India will not know it. A clear one-sentence answer buys a lot of credit.
    Model answer — say yours first

    An open network for commerce in India. Instead of integrating with one marketplace, a buyer application sends a search to a gateway and the gateway broadcasts it to many seller platforms. Their answers come back separately as callbacks over the next few seconds. That matters here because there is no single response to wait for, and a voice channel can only say one thing at a time, so the system has to decide when it knows enough to speak.

  2. “How is this different from a voice wrapper over a normal shop?” The question that separates the real project from the generic description of it.
    Model answer — say yours first

    A normal shop gives you one response with a list in it. Here the results arrive over seconds from platforms that never told you they were going to reply. On a screen that is invisible, because you render three rows and add four more. In voice the first sentence is a commitment. So the design is to acknowledge immediately, which buys network time, then set a deadline on the request rather than on any seller, and rank over whatever arrived.

  3. “How does somebody give you a delivery address?” Asked almost immediately after checkout is mentioned. The strong answer refuses the premise.
    Model answer — say yours first

    They do not dictate one. A spoken address through a recogniser, in a kitchen or on a street, is a delivery failure waiting to happen. Saved addresses live in the profile and the system reads one back for a yes. Choosing between three saved addresses is a good voice interaction. Entering a new one is not, and that is a case where the right answer is that this flow does not belong in voice at all.

  4. “What stops a dropped connection from ordering twice?” A systems question. Very few candidates reach for idempotency unprompted.
    Model answer — say yours first

    An idempotency key generated per order attempt and sent with every retry, so the network can recognise a repeat rather than create a second order. It matters more here than in most products because this is a mobile flow in India, so a dropped connection is the normal case and not an edge case. A timeout tells you nothing about whether the order was created, which is exactly why the client cannot decide on its own to try again.

  5. “What breaks first in the real world?” Looking for whether you have seen this in use rather than in a demo.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Speech recognition, and long before anything architectural. Brand names, product names and numbers are exactly where recognisers fail, and an order is made of those three things. Quantities are the worst, because small numbers sound alike and get code-mixed between languages. That is why the quantity is read back before confirm every single time, and why I would treat number extraction as its own step rather than trusting the transcript.

0019 · 2D to 3D: NeRF, Instant-NGP, Splatting

From lesson 0019.

  1. “Three weeks to a hundred minutes. How?” The first question and the one that goes wrong if you answer it as an algorithmic speedup.
    Model answer — say yours first

    By replacing a person with a fitting procedure. Three weeks was somebody modelling the object by hand in a 3D package. A hundred minutes is recovering camera poses from photographs, fitting a radiance field, and preparing it for delivery. Nothing got three hundred times faster. The bracketed figure in the bullet says it more honestly, because ninety-eight per cent less manual effort is a labour claim and labour is what actually changed.

  2. “What is a NeRF, in your own words?” A definitional question. Saying it without reaching for the word mesh is what shows you used one.
    Model answer — say yours first

    It fits a function rather than reconstructing a shape. Give it a point in space and a direction you are looking from, and it returns a colour and how solid things are there. To render a pixel you walk along its ray, ask that function at many points, and accumulate colour weighted by density until the ray is blocked. Training minimises the difference between rendered pixels and the real photographs. And it is a fit per scene, so every object is its own training run.

  3. “Why is splatting real-time and a NeRF is not?” Where people conflate the two speed problems. Keep them separate.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Because it stops marching rays. A radiance field needs dozens of samples per pixel, each one a network evaluation. Splatting represents the scene as a few million fuzzy blobs with a position, a shape, an opacity and a colour, and rendering means projecting them onto the screen and blending. Graphics hardware is built for exactly that. The cost it pays instead is a depth sort every frame, per view, because blending semi-transparent things is order-dependent.

  4. “What actually fails in production?” Separates having run this from having read about it. The answer is two stages upstream.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Camera pose estimation, nearly every time. It matches features between photographs, so it needs texture, and a plain wall or a reflective surface or photos taken too far apart all break it. The worst part is that it reports success and produces poses anyway. The symptom appears two stages later as a soft or ghosted reconstruction, which looks like a model problem. That is why the real deliverable was a capture protocol rather than a parameter setting.

  5. “Thirty per cent better quality on what?” The one number with no unit. Correct it yourself before this gets asked.
    Model answer — say yours first

    That is the figure I would restate. If it came from peak signal-to-noise ratio then a percentage is the wrong unit, because that number is decibels on a log scale and a percentage of it does not mean anything stable. The defensible version names the metric, gives both values, and says how many scenes it was measured over. If it came from a perceptual metric instead, that is worth saying, because those track what people actually see rather more closely.

0020 · Time-Distributed LSTM over Hand Pose

From lesson 0020.

  1. “Why not just use the video?” The opening question. The size argument is immediate and it lands.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Because the target was a low-end phone. A frame at modest resolution is over a hundred thousand numbers, and twenty-one joints on each of two hands with three coordinates each is a hundred and twenty-six. That collapse is what lets the recognition model be small. You also inherit a pose estimator that is already tuned for phones, so the expensive perception work is done by a component that was going to exist anyway. And nothing about background, clothing or skin tone survives into the keypoints, which is a property worth having.

  2. “What does that approach lose?” Volunteer this before it is asked. It is the answer that shows domain understanding rather than model understanding.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Everything that is not a hand, and in sign languages that is not a detail. Eyebrows, head tilt and mouth shape carry grammar. A raised brow can turn a statement into a question and a head shake can negate a clause. Those are grammatical markers rather than tone, and a hand-keypoint pipeline cannot represent them. So the honest scope is recognising signs from hand motion rather than interpreting sign language, and I would say that before the title does it for me.

  3. “Why an LSTM and not a transformer?” Often asked to see whether you defend the choice or apologise for it.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Because the target processes frames as they arrive on a weak device. A recurrent model carries a fixed-size state and takes one frame at a time, so memory does not grow with clip length and it streams naturally. Attention needs the window held and its cost grows with the square of the sequence. That is an engineering fit rather than a date. If I were doing it now I would probably use attention over a short window, but the input representation is the part I would keep.

  4. “What accuracy, and how did you split the data?” The second half of that question is the real one, and many candidates have not thought about it.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The split matters more than the number. Sign datasets are small and recorded by a handful of people, so a random split puts the same signers in training and test, and the model can score well by recognising people rather than signs. The right split puts each person entirely on one side. Accuracy drops under it, sometimes a lot, and that is the number that means anything about a new user. I would also show the confusion pairs, because signs differing by a small handshape are the interesting failures and an average hides them.

  5. “How do you know it works on a phone?” A measurable claim. Have the number or say you have the argument rather than the number.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The right answer is a parameter count and a per-frame latency on a named device, and if I do not have those I would present it as an architectural argument instead. The argument is real. At thirty frames per second you have about thirty-three milliseconds per frame, the pose estimator takes most of it, and a model over a hundred numbers per frame fits in what is left. The binding constraint is the estimator rather than the classifier, which is worth saying because it points at the right place to optimise.

0021 · 3D-RNN over Resting-State fMRI

From lesson 0021.

  1. “What is functional connectivity, exactly?” A definitional opener. The word correlation rather than connection is what earns credit.
    Model answer — say yours first

    You divide the brain into regions with an atlas, average the signal inside each region over the scan, and correlate every region's time course with every other's. That matrix is the connectome. It is important that this is a correlation and not a connection, because nothing here observes a pathway. Two regions can move together because they are communicating, or because both follow a third region, or because both follow breathing. And the signal is indirect and slow, since blood oxygenation peaks several seconds after the neural event.

  2. “Three hundred scans. Is that enough?” The question this whole chapter is built around. Answer it as a protocol question.
    Model answer — say yours first

    It is normal for the field and tiny for the method, and both halves of that matter. One scan is millions of numbers, so the model has far more parameters than examples and almost anything fits the training set. That means the validation protocol decides the result rather than the architecture. The things I would want to state are the split, the motion handling, and what the model was compared against, and none of those is about the network.

  3. “How did you handle head motion?” If the interviewer knows this field, this is their first real question. Knowing it by name is the credential.
    Model answer — say yours first

    It is the signature confound here, so it deserves a specific answer. Moving in the scanner raises apparent short-range connectivity and lowers long-range connectivity, and children with ADHD move more, so motion carries information about the label directly. The standard mitigations are regressing motion parameters out, discarding volumes where the head moved too much, and matching the groups on motion. None of them is complete, and discarding volumes removes the most data from exactly the children who move most.

  4. “What did you compare against?” Bringing up the no-imaging baseline unprompted is the strongest move available in this chapter.
    Model answer — say yours first

    The comparison that matters is a model with no imaging in it. In the public ADHD competition an entry using only personal characteristics, age and sex and handedness and IQ, scored at the top, ahead of models built on the scans. So the honest benchmark is that baseline on the same held-out split, and the difference between the two is the actual claim being made about brains. Reporting only against other imaging models does not establish that the imaging contributed anything.

  5. “Could this be used clinically?” A judgement question. The confident no is the right answer and hedging is the wrong one.
    Model answer — say yours first

    No. No imaging measure is a validated diagnostic for ADHD, the effect sizes in this literature are small and overlapping, and three hundred subjects cannot support a clinical claim. There is also a ceiling that more data would not remove, which is the label itself. An ADHD diagnosis is a clinical judgement made against behavioural criteria and clinicians disagree, so the target is a noisy measurement of a category rather than a biological fact. This is a research result about whether a signal exists.

0022 · The Career Narrative

From lesson 0022.

  1. “Walk me through your résumé.” The opener in most loops. Set the frame here or somebody else sets it for you.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Lead with the thread rather than the timeline. LLM systems since the start of 2023, agents and retrieval and the evaluation around them, with two peer-reviewed papers across the same years. Then the roles in order, one sentence each, at an even pace. Research prototypes at Samsung, a shipped agentic debugger with layered validation at Innovaccer, and a platform owned end to end at CloudSufi one level up. Name the seven months yourself, in one sentence, and finish on what you are looking for now.

  2. “Seven months at Innovaccer. What happened?” The guaranteed question. Fifteen seconds, then stop and let the pause sit.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Three parts and no more. The fact, said plainly with no adjectives. A reason about the work rather than about people, which has to be one this job does not reproduce. Then what the move bought, which on this résumé is a higher level and a platform to own. Then stop. The silence afterwards feels long to you and normal to them, and anything said into it sounds like justification rather than information.

  3. “How do I know you will not leave in six months?” The question behind all the others, asked directly. Do not treat it as hostile.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Answer the thing being estimated rather than the thing being said. What makes a stay likely is something that takes years and exists here, so name it specifically. Then be honest about the pattern rather than disputing it, because disputing the premise is worse than reframing it. Each move bought more ownership, and the next thing I want is long enough to own an outcome rather than a task, which is not a promise about intent but a statement about what I am selecting for.

  4. “What are you looking for?” The same question wearing a friendlier hat. Consistency between this and the direct version is what gets checked.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Whatever you name here has to be the same thing your leaving reason implied, and it has to be something this company visibly has. Not growth and not impact, because those exist everywhere and therefore explain nothing about staying here. Something structural about scope, ownership or the kind of problem, and ideally something that takes more than a year to get, because an answer achievable in twelve months schedules your next departure out loud.

  5. “What are you working on outside work?” A softer question with a hard use. A concrete answer beats an adjective every time.
    Model answer — say yours first

    Name something that exists rather than something you intend. A curriculum you are actually working through has a shape and a syllabus and can be described in a sentence, which is more than any claim about being curious can do. It also answers the depth concern indirectly, because sustained study of one area is exactly what somebody worried about breadth is listening for.
