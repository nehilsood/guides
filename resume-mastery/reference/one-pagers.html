0001 · Owning a Vendor-Evaluation Platform

The full chapter is here.

The line being defended
“Owned an AI vendor-evaluation platform end-to-end (Python/FastAPI, async SQLAlchemy, PostgreSQL, GKE) — live avatar interviews scored against a weighted rubric, with a two-pass Scout/Evaluator pipeline (Gemini, Tavily) grounding every vendor claim in a cited source.”
CloudSufi · bullet 1 of 4
Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

A spoken answer to “tell me about the vendor-evaluation platform you owned.” This is the opening of most loops where this bullet appears, and the failure mode is a tour. Read it aloud, then say it again with the page covered.

  1. Situation

    Companies choosing a software vendor have to interview them. Those interviews were slow, inconsistent between interviewers, and took the vendor’s claims on trust.

    Checking those claims properly meant an afternoon of searching that nobody had.

  2. Mechanism

    So the platform runs the interview through an avatar, and then does the part people skip. Afterwards, one orchestrator sequences three agents.

    A Scout researches the vendor twice. Once blind, so they cannot steer what gets looked up. Once targeted at what they actually claimed.

    An Evaluator scores every question against that question’s own written anchor. A Coordinator recommends the follow-up.

    The rule I would defend hardest is that agents never call each other. Exactly one file composes them.

    That is the only reason there is one place to put a deadline, and one place to read the control flow.

  3. What I did

    Two decisions I would make again. First, the model proposes and the code decides. It picks a band and a number.

    My code then clamps it into the band it chose, drops unasked questions, and computes every total. A score a vendor argues with has to be arithmetic you can show them.

    Second, grounding is a schema property, not a prompt instruction. A claim cannot be emitted without a sources array, because the output format has no shape for one.

  4. What it cost

    Two things, and I would rather say them than be asked. The Scout soft-fails, because research failing must never cost an interview its scorecard.

    The price is that a total search outage is indistinguishable from a vendor with no public footprint. The report comes back confident and empty.

    And the claims report is not reproducible. About one claim in five gets a different verdict on identical evidence. Pinning temperature to zero did not fix it.

    Almost every flip is on the no-data-versus-partial line. So it is a prompt-definition problem, not a sampling one. That one is still open.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“We used a multi-agent pipeline to evaluate vendors.” Every candidate says a version of this, and it invites the one question it cannot survive: “what makes it multi-agent rather than three function calls?” The honest and much stronger answer is that it is three function calls with one composer, on purpose — and that agents-calling-agents was rejected because it destroys the single place a deadline can live. Lead with the constraint, not the label.

The other trap is “every claim is grounded in a cited source.” Said flatly, it claims hallucination is impossible, and the follow-up — “what stops it citing a URL that wasn't in the evidence?” — has no good improvised answer. The version that holds names the mechanism and its limit in one breath: the schema forbids an uncited claim, and citation fidelity is measured separately as a fabrication rate.

Walk them through it · one vendor, end to end

The say it out loud block is ninety seconds. This is the ten minutes after that, when they say “walk me through it.” One vendor, followed from the first minute of the call to the number on the scorecard. The vendor is invented. Everything the system does to them is not.

The picture. A job interview where the panel also runs a background check. One person asks the questions. A second looks the candidate up, both before the interview and after it. A third grades the answers against a written marking scheme. Nobody grades while the candidate is still talking.

The case. Northwind Analytics is being evaluated for a security-heavy purchase. Security is thirty per cent of this rubric. During the call they say they are SOC 2 certified. Follow that one sentence all the way to the score it produces.

  1. The call

    What happens. An on-screen avatar runs the interview. It asks a fixed list of questions, and that list comes from the rubric rather than from the interviewer. Nothing is scored while the vendor is speaking.

    Concretely. Northwind gets the security topic's four questions. On the third one they say they are SOC 2 Type II certified.

    If they ask why. Live scoring sounds better and is worse. A grade handed out in minute one cannot use what gets said in minute forty.

  2. The handoff

    What happens. The call ends and the transcript is saved. Only then does the scoring pipeline start, as a background task.

    Concretely. The web request that finalised the interview has already returned its response. The pipeline is still running after it.

    If they ask why. That is the reason the pipeline opens its own database session. The request's session is closed the moment the response is sent, and the work outlives the response.

  3. Blind pass

    What happens. The Scout researches Northwind before it has read a word of the transcript. It finds the company website, runs industry-neutral searches, looks for the person who attended, and checks whether we have dealt with them before.

    Concretely. One of those searches is Northwind Analytics controversies. It survived a cull that deleted three other queries, because it is the one that finds what nobody volunteered.

    If they ask why. If the only research is aimed at what the vendor said, the vendor picks the entire evidence base. What they never mention is never checked. The blind pass is the half that cannot be steered.

  4. Targeted pass

    What happens. Now the Scout reads the transcript. It pulls out the factual claims and fires one search per claim.

    Concretely. “We are SOC 2 Type II certified” becomes one search. The claims are capped at ten, and all ten searches go out at once inside a single gather.

    If they ask why. Run one after another, those searches would blow the deadline on their own. The concurrency here is load-bearing rather than a tidy-up.

  5. In parallel

    What happens. Three separate model calls then run over the same gathered sources. A written summary, a structured claims report, and a check on the representative who attended.

    Concretely. None of the three needs another one's output, so all three run concurrently and each carries the same retry ladder.

  6. The record

    What happens. The claims report is not free text. Every claim comes back as a fixed record. A status, the claim itself, a note, and an array of sources with name, URL and quoted text.

    Concretely. Northwind's SOC 2 claim returns as PARTIAL. The evidence is an audit page dated 2019 and nothing after it. The other three statuses are verified, contradicted and no data.

    If they ask why. The model is never asked to cite its sources. The output format has no shape for a claim without them. That is the whole difference between grounding as advice and grounding as a rule.

    The limit, said first. A schema constrains shape and never string values. Nothing stops a URL appearing in that array that was not in the evidence. The failure has a name here and a measured rate, and it is called a bad cite.

  7. The scoring

    What happens. The Evaluator reads the transcript and the Scout's verdicts together. It scores each question against that question's own written anchor.

    Concretely. The security question has three anchors written out as prose, for low, medium and high. Northwind's answer plus a partial verdict matches the medium anchor, which describes a vendor claiming a certification it cannot currently evidence.

    If they ask why. The model makes exactly one judgment. Which of these three paragraphs does this answer look like. That is a reading task, and reading is the thing these models are actually good at.

  8. The arithmetic

    What happens. The model proposes a band and a number. My code decides what the number is.

    Concretely. The code clamps the number into the band the model itself picked. It drops questions that were never asked. It excludes an unanswered question rather than scoring it zero. Then it computes every total in Python.

    If they ask why. A vendor can lose a contract over this page. A score somebody argues with has to be arithmetic you can put in front of them.

  9. The weights

    What happens. Question scores average into their topic. Topics are then weighted into the total.

    Concretely. Security is thirty per cent. That number lives on the topic row inside one specific rubric version. The buyer set it, we never invent one, and the model never sees it.

    If they ask why. An earlier design kept a single weight row shared across every domain. Change security's weighting for a healthcare buyer and you changed it for the retail one too.

  10. Soft-fail

    What happens. The Scout sits inside a deadline and a catch-all. If search is slow or down, the scorecard is still produced.

    Concretely. The price is that a total search outage looks exactly like a vendor with no public footprint. The report comes back confident and empty.

    If they ask why. Losing an interview's scorecard because a third-party search API had a bad afternoon is the worse failure. Saying the cost out loud is what makes it a decision rather than a discovery.

  11. The argument

    What happens. A Coordinator turns all of it into a recommended follow-up, and the buyer reads the scorecard.

    Concretely. Northwind can be shown that its security score came from one question, one written anchor, and one audit page dated 2019.

    If they ask why. A disagreement is now about one question's anchor prose. That is a conversation two people can actually have, instead of an argument about the system as a whole.

A spoken answer to “you cut turn latency by nearly half — what did you actually do?” The version below volunteers both résumé corrections early, on purpose: said first they are candour, said last they are damage control.

  1. Situation

    The avatar platform calls us once per utterance and expects an OpenAI-shaped reply. The first version waited for the whole answer before sending anything.

    So the avatar stood silent for the entire generation. In a conversation, the pause before someone starts talking is the thing people actually feel.

  2. Mechanism

    So I moved it to streaming. Two corrections I would rather make myself than have pulled out of me.

    It is server-sent events over Gemini's OpenAI-compatible REST endpoint, not the bidirectional Live API. My résumé overstates that. And the before-and-after pair is my own measurement, not a benchmark in the repo.

    The mechanism is nicer than it sounds. The Host asks for a JSON object with the reply field declared first.

    Under a strict schema its characters come out first, and an incremental extractor forwards them as they land. It never advances past a fragment it cannot fully decode.

  3. What I did

    Alongside that, I split one retry policy into three failure classes at the model call. Model gone, our payload wrong, or spurious.

    That came out of an incident where model aliases plus a substring test meant every single turn was misdiagnosed as an outage, and silently retried.

    236 warnings, a hundred percent of turns, and no alert, for four days.

    Where it stands now, measured over 86 host turns: p50 two and a half seconds, p90 4.5, p95 4.7, nothing above 5.7.

  4. What it cost

    Two things. I made part of this path deliberately slower. The settle beat went from half a second to two, so a vendor who is only pausing mid-thought does not get interrupted.

    It is the second-largest bar in the whole breakdown. It is inside every number I just quoted, and I could improve my median tomorrow by deleting it.

    And streaming gives up the ability to take words back. The buffered path could discard a bad reply. Once the avatar is speaking, it is speaking.

    Separately, “zero session drops” is true of sessions. In the same period four of six interviews failed to score, which is a different layer and was fixed separately.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“We moved to streaming Gemini Live and cut latency 47%.” This is the résumé line said out loud, and it fails on one question — “so you're holding a bidirectional session to the model?” — because the honest answer is no, it is SSE over the OpenAI-compatible REST endpoint. Correcting your own wording unprompted reads as precision. Being corrected reads as either carelessness or something worse, and the interviewer cannot tell which, so they assume the worse one.

The second trap is quieter: “we got the median down to 2.2 seconds.” Any median quoted against a hard budget summons the tail question instantly, and here the tail is fine — so leading with p95 costs you nothing and buys the room. The version that lands: “p50 2.5, p95 4.7, nothing above 5.7, and a deliberate two-second wait is inside all of those.” Volunteering the tail and the self-inflicted delay in one sentence is the whole difference between a number you reported and a system you ran.

The picture. A simultaneous interpreter at a conference. They start speaking before the sentence they are translating has finished, because a room that waits for whole paragraphs is a room in silence. The price is that a word already out of their mouth cannot be taken back. That trade is this whole chapter.

The case. A vendor has just finished answering a question about their deployment model. Follow that one turn. The avatar is a third-party product we do not own, and it speaks exactly one dialect.

  1. The utterance

    What happens. The avatar platform decides the person has stopped speaking. It sends us their words and asks for a reply.

    Concretely. That request arrives in OpenAI's chat-completions format. The avatar only knows how to talk to that shape, so the gateway wears it.

    If they ask why. We do not control the avatar and cannot teach it a new protocol. Looking like OpenAI is the cost of using somebody else's face.

  2. The settle beat

    What happens. Before anything is sent to a model, we wait. Two full seconds.

    Concretely. That wait used to be half a second. I raised it to two on purpose, which made my own headline number worse.

    If they ask why. Half a second reads a breath as a finished answer, and the avatar talks over somebody mid-thought. The beat is now the second-largest bar in the latency breakdown. I could improve my median tomorrow by deleting it.

  3. The gateway

    What happens. The request is authenticated, and then the interview logic runs. Which question is next, what has already been asked, what state this session is in.

    Concretely. None of that is visible from outside. The caller sees an OpenAI endpoint and nothing else.

    If they ask why. After that auth check, this service never returns an error. An internal failure becomes a successful response carrying a bland line. An avatar standing silently in front of a vendor is worse than an avatar saying something dull.

  4. The model call

    What happens. The gateway calls Gemini and asks for the answer as a stream rather than as a finished block.

    Concretely. This is server-sent events over Gemini's OpenAI-compatible REST endpoint. It is not the bidirectional Live API, and my résumé overstates that. I would rather correct it myself than have it pulled out of me.

    If they ask why. Server-sent events are a one-way stream over ordinary HTTP. Only the server needs to push here, so a full socket buys nothing.

  5. Reply first

    What happens. The model is asked for a JSON object, and the spoken reply is declared as the first field in it.

    Concretely. Under a strict schema the fields come out in the declared order. So the characters we want to speak arrive before anything else does.

    If they ask why. Streaming only helps if the useful part arrives first. Put the reply field third and you have a stream that still makes everyone wait.

  6. The extractor

    What happens. Characters arrive a few at a time. An incremental extractor pulls the reply out of a JSON document that is not finished yet.

    Concretely. It never advances past a fragment it cannot fully decode. A half-arrived escape sequence waits rather than being guessed at.

    If they ask why. Speaking a character you got wrong is unrecoverable. The avatar has already said it.

  7. The avatar talks

    What happens. The first safe characters go back up the stream and the avatar starts speaking. The rest of the answer is still being generated behind it.

    Concretely. Measured over 86 host turns, the median turn now lands at two and a half seconds. p90 is 4.5, p95 is 4.7, and nothing went above 5.7.

    If they ask why. Streaming made nothing faster. The model takes as long as it always did. What changed is when the avatar is allowed to open its mouth.

  8. Barge-in

    What happens. Say the vendor starts talking again while that reply is still generating. The in-flight turn is abandoned rather than raced.

    Concretely. A superseded turn is cancelled and writes nothing to state. It does not get to finish and quietly win.

    If they ask why. Two turns both writing is how a session ends up with a reply to a question nobody asked.

  9. When it fails

    What happens. Three different things can go wrong on that model call, and all three arrive looking identical. The model is gone, our payload is wrong, or it is spurious.

    Concretely. Telling two of them apart with a substring test is what cost me four days. A pinned alias resolved to a model that refused a parameter we send.

    If they ask why. Every turn was diagnosed as an outage and silently retried on a fallback. A hundred per cent of turns, 236 warnings, no alert. Nobody alerts on warnings, and that is the part I would change first.

  10. The cost

    What happens. The buffered version could look at a finished reply and throw it away. The streaming version cannot.

    Concretely. Once the avatar is speaking, it is speaking. Any guardrail has to run before the first character leaves, not after the last one.

    If they ask why. And the résumé line says zero session drops, which is true of sessions. In the same window four interviews out of six failed to score. That is a different layer and it was fixed separately.

A spoken answer to “walk me through the schematization pipeline.” The trap here is the word “agents.” Lead with the split between the writer and the judge, and the ten sub-agents become a detail instead of the headline.

  1. Situation

    Public statistics arrive as spreadsheets, and every publisher formats them differently. Before anything can be compared, an engineer has to say what each column means in a shared vocabulary.

    That was hand work, one column at a time, against documentation.

  2. Mechanism

    Five stages. Sample the data, pick the vocabulary, write the mapping, check it, score it. The middle two sit inside a loop.

    The part I would defend hardest is that the checker is a program, not a model. We run the mapping through the real processor as a subprocess and ask whether data rows came out.

  3. What I did

    The loop feeds that program's actual error text back into the next prompt. The model is correcting a named failure, not guessing again.

    And it stops three ways, not one. Acceptable, stagnant, or out of attempts. Stopping when the score stops moving is what keeps one hopeless dataset from costing as much as forty good ones.

  4. What it cost

    Two things I would say before being asked. The three-hours-to-thirty-minutes figure is a field estimate, not something I instrumented.

    And the accuracy is uneven. Census datasets average 39.3% and crime datasets 2.6%. I ran the analysis, and the driver is vocabulary coverage, not anything about the files.

    I also found our answer key was broken. Only 28 of 49 hand-written maps validate. I reported that rather than quoting the flattering number.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“We used a multi-agent system with self-correcting retry loops.” It is true and it is the weakest version of this story. It invites “so what decides that a correction is an improvement?” and the honest answer is the interesting one you skipped: a subprocess. Lead with the oracle, not the agent count.

The other trap is “100% success rate.” It is in the README and it means the pipeline completed, on four datasets. Said without that qualifier next to a PV accuracy of 26.8%, it reads as a contradiction and the interviewer will find it.

The say it out loud block is ninety seconds. This is the ten minutes after that, when they say “walk me through it.” One spreadsheet, followed from the moment it lands to the draft an engineer opens. The dataset is a stand-in. The numbers and the failures are measured.

The picture. Translating a form into a language whose dictionary is fixed and published. You cannot coin a word. And there is one way to know your translation works. You hand it to the machine that reads that language and see whether anything comes out.

The case. A state statistics office publishes a crime table. Eleven columns, names like TOT_IPC_2019, and no documentation. Before anyone can compare it with another state, each column has to be said in a shared vocabulary.

  1. The spreadsheet

    What happens. The file arrives with no explanation. An engineer would open it, read the column names, and start looking things up in documentation.

    Concretely. That hand work runs about three hours for one dataset. It is one column at a time, against a vocabulary nobody has memorised.

    If they ask why. I should flag that the three-hour figure is a field estimate rather than something I instrumented. It is the kind of number an interviewer is right to push on.

  2. Sample it

    What happens. Stage one reads a sample of the actual rows rather than just the header.

    Concretely. A column called TOT_IPC_2019 means very little. The values under it mean a lot more.

  3. Pick the vocabulary

    What happens. Stage two chooses which part of the shared vocabulary this dataset belongs to, out of seven categories.

    Concretely. That vocabulary is Data Commons, a public knowledge graph with a fixed set of properties. You can only map a column to a property that already exists in it.

    If they ask why. Get this stage wrong and every stage after it is wasted. The mapping will be internally consistent and pointed at the wrong half of the graph.

  4. Write the map

    What happens. Stage three writes the actual file, which says this column means this property with these values.

    Concretely. That file is the PVMAP, a property-value map. It is the artefact the whole pipeline exists to produce.

  5. Run it

    What happens. Stage four does not read the mapping and form an opinion about it. It runs the mapping.

    Concretely. stat_var_processor.py is launched as a subprocess against the real data. Its exit code is the verdict, and the question is whether data rows came out.

    If they ask why. This is the part I would defend hardest. A model asked whether its own output looks right will say yes. A program that runs it either produces rows or does not.

  6. The feedback

    What happens. If the run failed, the real error text goes back into the next prompt.

    Concretely. The model is not asked to try again. It is handed the named failure and asked to fix that.

    If they ask why. Stages three and four sit inside a loop for this reason. Writing a map and checking it is the part you do repeatedly, so it is the part that gets a loop around it.

  7. Three exits

    What happens. The loop stops three ways. The answer is acceptable, the score has stopped improving, or the attempts have run out.

    Concretely. Ten sub-agents run in order inside that loop, and three of them can raise the stop signal, for three different reasons.

    If they ask why. Stopping when the score stops moving is what keeps one hopeless dataset from costing as much as forty good ones. Succeeding is the least interesting of the three exits.

  8. The handover

    What happens. Stage five scores the result, and the engineer opens a draft rather than a blank file.

    Concretely. The review pass runs about thirty minutes against the three hours it replaced. They refine rather than author, and the evidence for each mapping is attached.

    If they ask why. Knowing which exit fired is what tells them whether they are reviewing a solved dataset or an abandoned one.

  9. Where it fails

    What happens. Accuracy is very uneven between domains, and I would rather say so than be asked.

    Concretely. Census datasets average 39.3 per cent. Crime datasets average 2.6. That is the same pipeline on both.

    If they ask why. The team assumed wide or messy tables were the cause, so the proposed fix was prompt work. I pulled all 49 benchmark datasets and tested seven structural features. Every structural hypothesis failed.

  10. The answer key

    What happens. The real driver turned out to be vocabulary coverage in the target schema, which no amount of prompting moves.

    Concretely. While checking that, I ran the hand-written answer key through the same processor. Only 28 of 49 produced valid output.

    If they ask why. We were partly measuring against broken answers. I reported that in the same memo as our results, which made them harder to present and made the roadmap correct.

A spoken answer to “tell me about the lineage agent.” Lead with the fork. The number comes last, with its decomposition attached, so it never sits alone.

  1. Situation

    A client was migrating off a legacy SAP warehouse onto BigQuery, and nobody could say how the old one was wired.

    What existed was a pile of Word documents, with the real answer buried in mapping tables inside them.

  2. Mechanism

    The design is one fork. Code where there is a right answer, the model where there is a judgment.

    The mapping tables get parsed deterministically, so the 516 field mappings are extracted rather than generated. Object identity and cross-references go to the model, because those genuinely need an opinion.

  3. What I did

    Two things I would defend. The model's answers pass guardrails that reject non-SAP names, self-loops, and generic words like TABLE offered as an object name.

    And the model sometimes called merge before extraction. That produced a graph with zero field mappings and no error.

    I fixed it with a before-tool callback rather than a better prompt. A prompt lowers a failure rate. A precondition removes it.

  4. What it cost

    The headline needs a caveat and I would rather give it than have it pulled. The 99.2% is a weighted composite. Node and edge are a quarter each, fields are 0.30, structure is 0.20.

    Inside the field term, most of the weight is count accuracy and a distribution cosine.

    So it is not 99.2% of columns checked one by one. The résumé wording implies more than the metric does.

    The other limit is the sample. Five documents, hand-written answers. Enough to catch a regression, not enough to claim a general accuracy figure.

There is no system to trace here. The thing to follow is the interviewer's eye going down the dates, and the fifteen seconds that decide what the rest of the conversation is about. Two lines below are yours to fill, and they should stay yours.

The picture. A landlord reading a rental history with three addresses in three years. They are not judging the moves and they are not owed an apology. They are estimating one thing, which is whether they will be advertising the place again in eight months.

The case. Four employers since January 2023. Twelve months, eighteen months, seven months, and a current role under a year old. The dates are on the page and anybody can add them up.

  1. The scan

    What happens. They read down the right-hand column before they read anything else.

    Concretely. The seven-month role in the middle is what stops them. So is a current role that has not reached a year.

    If they ask why. A senior hire takes months to become worth what it costs. A short tenure is a loss that somebody has to explain internally, which is why this gets asked at all.

  2. The real question

    What happens. What they ask is why you left. What they want to know is different.

    Concretely. Will you still be here in two years, and is there something wrong that I should know about.

    If they ask why. Every part of a good answer exists to lower that estimate. Nothing in it is an apology, and an apology raises the estimate rather than lowering it.

  3. Go first

    What happens. Raise it yourself, in the first few minutes, before anybody has to go looking.

    Concretely. That converts a discovered concern into a handled one, and those land completely differently.

  4. The frame

    What happens. Lead with the thread rather than with the timeline.

    Concretely. Building LLM systems since the start of 2023, across four companies and two peer-reviewed papers. Agents, retrieval, and the evaluation and guardrails around them.

    If they ask why. Set that frame before somebody builds a different one. What follows is then usually a question about the work rather than about the dates.

  5. The arc

    What happens. The moves are not the story. What changed across them is.

    Concretely. Samsung was research, prototypes and papers. Innovaccer was shipping, an agentic debugger tested against a 368-scenario suite. CloudSufi is owning a platform end to end.

    If they ask why. Prototypes, then a shipped system, then something owned. Each move bought more of the outcome rather than more of the task, and that is a trajectory rather than a pattern of leaving.

  6. Three sentences

    What happens. When the direct question comes, the answer has three parts and takes about fifteen seconds.

    Concretely. A fact, a reason, and what you are optimising for now. In that order.

    If they ask why. Then be quiet. Let them ask a follow-up if they want one, and they usually do not.

  7. The fact

    What happens. The first sentence names it without softening it.

    Concretely. The seven months at Innovaccer is the thing to say yourself rather than wait for.

  8. The reason

    What happens. The second sentence is yours to write, and it has to pass one hard test.

    Concretely. [Your one sentence goes here.]

    If they ask why. Say it out loud, then ask whether it would also be true of this job. If it would, you have just described the next departure rather than explaining the last one.

  9. The forward half

    What happens. The third sentence is the one most people skip, and it is the one that answers the real question.

    Concretely. What I am after now is [the specific thing this role has]. That is the part that takes more than a year, and it is why this conversation.

    If they ask why. It has to be named as something this role actually has. A generic answer is heard as no answer, and it cannot be reused between companies.

  10. Then stop

    What happens. Three things will undo all of it, and they are all easy to do by accident.

    Concretely. Overexplaining, because length is read as discomfort. Any criticism of a former employer, because it is heard as a preview of how you will describe this one.

    If they ask why. And inconsistency. Interviewers compare notes, and two different reasons across two rounds is a much bigger problem than one short role. Say the same sentence every time.

Now cover it and redraw

Sixty seconds. Four boxes, one frame, three parts, one test.

  • The months in each role, said without flinching.
  • Prototypes, shipped system, owned platform. The frame.
  • Fact, reason, forward — then stop.
  • The reproduction test, because it is the only rule that matters.
Time yourself saying the three parts. If it runs past twenty seconds, the reason is carrying more than one clause and it should not be.
