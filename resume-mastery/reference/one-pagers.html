0001 · Owning a Vendor-Evaluation Platform

The full chapter is here.

The line being defended
“Owned an AI vendor-evaluation platform end-to-end (Python/FastAPI, async SQLAlchemy, PostgreSQL, GKE) — live avatar interviews scored against a weighted rubric, with a two-pass Scout/Evaluator pipeline (Gemini, Tavily) grounding every vendor claim in a cited source.”
CloudSufi · bullet 1 of 4
Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

A spoken answer to “tell me about the vendor-evaluation platform you owned.” This is the opening of most loops where this bullet appears, and the failure mode is a tour. Read it aloud, then say it again with the page covered.

  1. Situation

    Companies choosing a software vendor have to interview them. Those interviews were slow, inconsistent between interviewers, and took the vendor’s claims on trust.

    Checking those claims properly meant an afternoon of searching that nobody had.

  2. Mechanism

    So the platform runs the interview through an avatar, and then does the part people skip. Afterwards, one orchestrator sequences three agents.

    A Scout researches the vendor twice. Once blind, so they cannot steer what gets looked up. Once targeted at what they actually claimed.

    An Evaluator scores every question against that question’s own written anchor. A Coordinator recommends the follow-up.

    The rule I would defend hardest is that agents never call each other. Exactly one file composes them.

    That is the only reason there is one place to put a deadline, and one place to read the control flow.

  3. What I did

    Two decisions I would make again. First, the model proposes and the code decides. It picks a band and a number.

    My code then clamps it into the band it chose, drops unasked questions, and computes every total. A score a vendor argues with has to be arithmetic you can show them.

    Second, grounding is a schema property, not a prompt instruction. A claim cannot be emitted without a sources array, because the output format has no shape for one.

  4. What it cost

    Two things, and I would rather say them than be asked. The Scout soft-fails, because research failing must never cost an interview its scorecard.

    The price is that a total search outage is indistinguishable from a vendor with no public footprint. The report comes back confident and empty.

    And the claims report is not reproducible. About one claim in five gets a different verdict on identical evidence. Pinning temperature to zero did not fix it.

    Almost every flip is on the no-data-versus-partial line. So it is a prompt-definition problem, not a sampling one. That one is still open.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“We used a multi-agent pipeline to evaluate vendors.” Every candidate says a version of this, and it invites the one question it cannot survive: “what makes it multi-agent rather than three function calls?” The honest and much stronger answer is that it is three function calls with one composer, on purpose — and that agents-calling-agents was rejected because it destroys the single place a deadline can live. Lead with the constraint, not the label.

The other trap is “every claim is grounded in a cited source.” Said flatly, it claims hallucination is impossible, and the follow-up — “what stops it citing a URL that wasn't in the evidence?” — has no good improvised answer. The version that holds names the mechanism and its limit in one breath: the schema forbids an uncited claim, and citation fidelity is measured separately as a fabrication rate.

Explain it · one picture

The picture. A job interview where the panel also runs a background check. One person asks the questions. A second looks the candidate up, both before the interview and after it. A third grades the answers against a written marking scheme. Nobody grades while the candidate is still talking.

0002 · The Gateway and the Move to Streaming

The full chapter is here.

The line being defended
“Built its custom OpenAI-compatible LLM gateway, then moved the inference path from REST to streaming Gemini Live, cutting end-to-end turn latency 47% at p50 (4.1 s to 2.2 s) and 52% at p90 (5.1 s to 2.4 s), with zero mid-interview session drops.”
CloudSufi · bullet 2 of 4
Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

A spoken answer to “you cut turn latency by nearly half — what did you actually do?” The version below volunteers both résumé corrections early, on purpose: said first they are candour, said last they are damage control.

  1. Situation

    The avatar platform calls us once per utterance and expects an OpenAI-shaped reply. The first version waited for the whole answer before sending anything.

    So the avatar stood silent for the entire generation. In a conversation, the pause before someone starts talking is the thing people actually feel.

  2. Mechanism

    So I moved it to streaming. Two corrections I would rather make myself than have pulled out of me.

    It is server-sent events over Gemini's OpenAI-compatible REST endpoint, not the bidirectional Live API. My résumé overstates that. And the before-and-after pair is my own measurement, not a benchmark in the repo.

    The mechanism is nicer than it sounds. The Host asks for a JSON object with the reply field declared first.

    Under a strict schema its characters come out first, and an incremental extractor forwards them as they land. It never advances past a fragment it cannot fully decode.

  3. What I did

    Alongside that, I split one retry policy into three failure classes at the model call. Model gone, our payload wrong, or spurious.

    That came out of an incident where model aliases plus a substring test meant every single turn was misdiagnosed as an outage, and silently retried.

    236 warnings, a hundred percent of turns, and no alert, for four days.

    Where it stands now, measured over 86 host turns: p50 two and a half seconds, p90 4.5, p95 4.7, nothing above 5.7.

  4. What it cost

    Two things. I made part of this path deliberately slower. The settle beat went from half a second to two, so a vendor who is only pausing mid-thought does not get interrupted.

    It is the second-largest bar in the whole breakdown. It is inside every number I just quoted, and I could improve my median tomorrow by deleting it.

    And streaming gives up the ability to take words back. The buffered path could discard a bad reply. Once the avatar is speaking, it is speaking.

    Separately, “zero session drops” is true of sessions. In the same period four of six interviews failed to score, which is a different layer and was fixed separately.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“We moved to streaming Gemini Live and cut latency 47%.” This is the résumé line said out loud, and it fails on one question — “so you're holding a bidirectional session to the model?” — because the honest answer is no, it is SSE over the OpenAI-compatible REST endpoint. Correcting your own wording unprompted reads as precision. Being corrected reads as either carelessness or something worse, and the interviewer cannot tell which, so they assume the worse one.

The second trap is quieter: “we got the median down to 2.2 seconds.” Any median quoted against a hard budget summons the tail question instantly, and here the tail is fine — so leading with p95 costs you nothing and buys the room. The version that lands: “p50 2.5, p95 4.7, nothing above 5.7, and a deliberate two-second wait is inside all of those.” Volunteering the tail and the self-inflicted delay in one sentence is the whole difference between a number you reported and a system you ran.

Explain it · one picture

The picture. A simultaneous interpreter at a conference. They start speaking before the sentence they are translating has finished, because a room that waits for whole paragraphs is a room in silence. The price is that a word already out of their mouth cannot be taken back. That trade is this whole chapter.

Now cover it and redraw

Sixty seconds. The test is whether the settle beat comes out first — if you draw the Gemini call before the wait, you have drawn somebody else's gateway.

  • The settle beat drawn wide, with the cancel branch hanging off it.
  • “reply” declared first — without this mark, streaming is unexplained magic.
  • The long return arrow to the avatar, crossing under everything, labelled “starts speaking.”
  • One real number. p50 2504, p95 4703, and the sentence that the beat is inside both.
Say “wait, call, extract, speak” as you draw. Four words, left to right, and the first one is the one nobody expects.

CloudSufi · bullet 3 of 4
Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

A spoken answer to “walk me through the schematization pipeline.” The trap here is the word “agents.” Lead with the split between the writer and the judge, and the ten sub-agents become a detail instead of the headline.

  1. Situation

    Public statistics arrive as spreadsheets, and every publisher formats them differently. Before anything can be compared, an engineer has to say what each column means in a shared vocabulary.

    That was hand work, one column at a time, against documentation.

  2. Mechanism

    Five stages. Sample the data, pick the vocabulary, write the mapping, check it, score it. The middle two sit inside a loop.

    The part I would defend hardest is that the checker is a program, not a model. We run the mapping through the real processor as a subprocess and ask whether data rows came out.

  3. What I did

    The loop feeds that program's actual error text back into the next prompt. The model is correcting a named failure, not guessing again.

    And it stops three ways, not one. Acceptable, stagnant, or out of attempts. Stopping when the score stops moving is what keeps one hopeless dataset from costing as much as forty good ones.

  4. What it cost

    Two things I would say before being asked. The three-hours-to-thirty-minutes figure is a field estimate, not something I instrumented.

    And the accuracy is uneven. Census datasets average 39.3% and crime datasets 2.6%. I ran the analysis, and the driver is vocabulary coverage, not anything about the files.

    I also found our answer key was broken. Only 28 of 49 hand-written maps validate. I reported that rather than quoting the flattering number.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“We used a multi-agent system with self-correcting retry loops.” It is true and it is the weakest version of this story. It invites “so what decides that a correction is an improvement?” and the honest answer is the interesting one you skipped: a subprocess. Lead with the oracle, not the agent count.

The other trap is “100% success rate.” It is in the README and it means the pipeline completed, on four datasets. Said without that qualifier next to a PV accuracy of 26.8%, it reads as a contradiction and the interviewer will find it.

Explain it · one picture

The picture. Translating a form into a language whose dictionary is fixed and published. You cannot coin a word. And there is one way to know your translation works. You hand it to the machine that reads that language and see whether anything comes out.

  • The five stages left to right. If you only get this, you can still answer the opening question.
  • The loop frame around generation and validation. It is a cycle, not two steps.
  • Validation is a subprocess. The one mark to keep if you lose the others.
  • The three exits along the bottom, and 28 of 49 on the right.

CloudSufi · bullet 4 of 4
Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

A spoken answer to “tell me about the lineage agent.” Lead with the fork. The number comes last, with its decomposition attached, so it never sits alone.

  1. Situation

    A client was migrating off a legacy SAP warehouse onto BigQuery, and nobody could say how the old one was wired.

    What existed was a pile of Word documents, with the real answer buried in mapping tables inside them.

  2. Mechanism

    The design is one fork. Code where there is a right answer, the model where there is a judgment.

    The mapping tables get parsed deterministically, so the 516 field mappings are extracted rather than generated. Object identity and cross-references go to the model, because those genuinely need an opinion.

  3. What I did

    Two things I would defend. The model's answers pass guardrails that reject non-SAP names, self-loops, and generic words like TABLE offered as an object name.

    And the model sometimes called merge before extraction. That produced a graph with zero field mappings and no error.

    I fixed it with a before-tool callback rather than a better prompt. A prompt lowers a failure rate. A precondition removes it.

  4. What it cost

    The headline needs a caveat and I would rather give it than have it pulled. The 99.2% is a weighted composite. Node and edge are a quarter each, fields are 0.30, structure is 0.20.

    Inside the field term, most of the weight is count accuracy and a distribution cosine.

    So it is not 99.2% of columns checked one by one. The résumé wording implies more than the metric does.

    The other limit is the sample. Five documents, hand-written answers. Enough to catch a regression, not enough to claim a general accuracy figure.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“It extracts column-level mappings at 99.2% accuracy.” It is the résumé line said out loud, and it invites “so 99.2% of the 516 mappings were correct?” The answer is no, and having to say no to your own sentence is a bad position. Give the four weights in the same breath as the number.

The other trap is “we used guardrails to prevent hallucination.” The guardrails do not prevent hallucination. They reject a specific set of malformed outputs. What prevents hallucination in the mappings is that a model never wrote them, and that is a much stronger sentence.

Explain it · one picture

The picture. Two people reading a departed engineer's handover notes. One copies the tables out exactly, because a table is already an answer. The other reads the prose around them and works out what the writer meant. You would never let the second person rewrite the tables.

  • The fork. Parsed on one side, judged on the other. Everything else is detail.
  • Zero hallucination risk on the left branch. The one mark to keep.
  • The callback arrow, because it is the story an interviewer remembers.
  • The four weights on the right, so the 99.2% never stands alone.

Innovaccer · bullet 1 of 3
Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

A spoken answer to “tell me about the pipeline debugger.” Lead with the triage, because it is the judgment call. The autonomy lands better once the interviewer has heard how little of the work reaches it.

  1. Situation

    A data platform runs thousands of scheduled jobs and some fail every night. Someone on call reads each log and decides whether it matters.

    Most of it is repetitive, and most failures are not interesting.

  2. Mechanism

    The system is a state machine, not a conversation. Named steps, explicit routers, one readable control path.

    In front of everything is a cheap screener. It matches the log against past incidents and returns one of three categories: retry it, ignore it, or investigate it.

    That triage is the whole idea. Only the third branch costs anything, and it is the rare one.

  3. What I did

    On the investigate branch it can propose a fix and retrigger the pipeline unattended. So the part I would want you to push on is what stands in the way.

    Four things. The screener has to escalate. The solution's action field has to say apply. A router has to agree. And a feature flag with a hard retry threshold has to allow it.

  4. What it cost

    Two corrections I would rather make myself. My résumé says the specialists run in parallel. On the default path they do not.

    One agent holds them as five tools and reasons through them in sequence. There is a parallel branch, and it is behind a flag that is off.

    And the validation layer that proves a fix before applying it defaults to off. That is the first thing I would change.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“Specialist agents debug SQL, Python, file and infrastructure faults in parallel.” It is the résumé read aloud, and the default configuration does not do it. One question about the dispatcher gets there. Say “five tools on one reasoning agent by default, with a parallel branch behind a flag” instead, and you sound like someone who has read their own config.

The other trap is “it fixes pipelines automatically.” Said flat, it makes an interviewer nervous and they start looking for what you forgot. Said as “it can retrigger unattended, and here are the four gates,” the same fact reads as careful engineering.

Explain it · one picture

The picture. A hospital triage nurse. Almost nobody walking through the door needs a surgeon. The nurse does not treat anyone. The whole job is deciding who gets treated and how urgently, and that decision costs a minute rather than an afternoon.

Now cover it and redraw

  • The three-way split at the screener. Two arrows sideways, one down.
  • “the only expensive branch” on the downward arrow. The one mark to keep.
  • Sequential, not parallel inside the lead manager box.
  • The retrigger box and the flag guarding it.
  • The word false next to pipeline validations, so you never forget it.

0006 · Layered Validation on an Autonomous Path

The full chapter is here.

The line being defended
“Guarded its autonomous path with layered validation — live Snowflake schemas in agent context, table-name checks closing the injection path in LLM-written SQL, and EXPLAIN plus syntax, import, and runtime checks proving each generated fix before it touches production — regression-tested on a 368-scenario offline evaluation suite.”
Innovaccer · bullet 2 of 3
Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

A spoken answer to “how do you validate what the model writes?” The winning move is precision about what each check proves, followed by the sentence nobody expects: none of them proves it is right.

  1. Situation

    The debugger can write a fix and apply it to a production pipeline with nobody watching. So something has to check the fix first.

    No single check proves a fix is correct. So you build several cheap ones, and each rules out a different way of being wrong.

  2. Mechanism

    Three layers. First, the real table definitions go into context before anything is written, so it is not guessing at column names.

    Second, generated SQL is sent to Snowflake as an EXPLAIN. That proves it parses and binds against the live schema, and it never executes.

    Third, Python goes through parso with error recovery on, so we get every syntax error in one pass instead of stopping at the first.

  3. What I did

    The parso choice is the one I would defend. Each round trip to the model costs a generation, so returning four errors at once is one correction cycle rather than four.

    And I would state the limit plainly: EXPLAIN tells you the sentence is grammatical and every word is real. It does not tell you the sentence is true.

  4. What it cost

    Two corrections to my own bullet. It says table-name checks close the injection path. The real barrier is an allowlist permitting only SELECT and EXPLAIN.

    And “regression-tested” is generous for 368 recorded cases scored by cosine similarity, with no threshold published.

    The gap that bothers me most is in the harness. It scores generated code by how similar it reads, when the production path already has a validator that could run it.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“Every generated fix is validated before it touches production.” The flag guarding that layer defaults to off, so the sentence is true of the design and possibly not of the deployment. “There is a validation layer, it sits behind a flag, and here is the default” costs you nothing and cannot be contradicted.

The other trap is “we prevent SQL injection with table-name validation.” An interviewer who cares about security will ask how, and the answer is a keyword denylist plus a statement allowlist. Lead with the allowlist. It is the strong control and it is the one that is actually there.

Explain it · one picture

The picture. Proofreading a letter written in a language you half speak. A spellchecker proves every word is a real word. A grammar checker proves the sentences parse. Neither of them can tell you the letter says something true. That gap is the whole chapter.

Now cover it and redraw

Sixty seconds. Two branches, one gate, and one dashed line that undoes all of it.

  • The split into SQL and Python, with a different check on each.
  • Proves it binds, proves nothing about meaning. The one mark to keep.
  • error_recovery=True on the Python side, and why.
  • The dashed bypass and the word false. Never draw this diagram without it.
Say “grammatical, not true” out loud as you draw the EXPLAIN box. Two words and the layer's limit is explained.

0007 · Execution Forecasting and Dependency Resolution

The full chapter is here.

The line being defended
“Built the execution-forecasting layer of a daily workflow planner (EWMA, Prophet) — inferring each pipeline's run cadence from execution history to predict its next run date, driving an auto-generated daily execution plan with parent–child dependency resolution in place of manually tracked schedules.”
Innovaccer · bullet 3 of 3
Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

A spoken answer to “tell me about the forecasting layer.” This is a small system, so depth is the only thing that impresses. Get to the weekday failure fast, because owning it is the whole performance.

  1. Situation

    A platform runs thousands of pipelines, and a lot of them have no trustworthy schedule. Somebody kept a spreadsheet of what was supposed to run today.

    The idea was to stop reading intent and start reading evidence.

  2. Mechanism

    Take the distinct days a pipeline ran, difference them into gaps, and take an exponentially weighted mean of those gaps. Add it to the last run.

    Alpha is a half, so the newest gap carries half the weight, the one before a quarter. It adapts to a real change in cadence within a few runs.

    Then two details that matter. The test is greater than or equal, so overdue work stays on the plan.

    And if a pipeline's parent is already scheduled, the child is dropped. The parent will trigger it anyway.

  3. What I did

    The decision I would defend is using history rather than the declared schedule. It is less precise than reading a cron expression. It is also right more often, because configuration drifts and history does not.

  4. What it cost

    The honest limitation, and I would rather raise it. An interval has no calendar in it.

    A weekday job gives gaps of one, one, one, one, three. The weighted mean is about 1.4, so after a Friday run it predicts Saturday, a day that job has never run.

    That is exactly what Prophet would fix, and I should be clear: Prophet is a strategy branch in the design with no implementation behind it. EWMA is what runs.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“We used EWMA and Prophet to forecast execution.” It is the bracket in the résumé read aloud, and it names one library that was never written. The enum branch calls _analyze_with_prophet and that method does not exist. Say “EWMA implemented, Prophet designed for and not built, and here is the failure that motivated it” — which is a better engineering story than two working algorithms would have been.

The other trap is calling this “time series forecasting.” It is a weighted average of date differences. Using the grander phrase invites questions about stationarity and seasonality that the implementation cannot answer, and the plain description is entirely respectable.

Explain it · one picture

The picture. Working out when the bin lorry comes by writing down when it actually came, rather than by reading the council's timetable. The timetable says what somebody intended. The notebook says what happened. When the two disagree, the notebook is right.

Now cover it and redraw

Sixty seconds. A straight pipeline across the top, one filter, and one box that admits the weakness.

  • The four boxes across the top. History, intervals, EWMA, predicted date.
  • Greater than or equal, and why. Overdue stays overdue.
  • The parent filter before the plan.
  • The weekday box. The one mark to keep, because it is the whole interview.
Say “one, one, one, one, three” out loud as you draw the bottom box. Five numbers and the limitation explains itself.

0009 · LLM and Agents

The full chapter is here.

The line being defended
“LLM & Agents: Google ADK, MCP, function calling, multi-agent orchestration, semantic routing, MemGPT; Gemini, GPT, Claude, Llama”
Technical Skills · line 2 of 4
Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

For “tell me about your agent experience” — the most likely opening question of the entire loop. Lead with the definition, then the restriction, because the restriction is what makes you sound like you have run these in production.

  1. Situation

    I have built agent systems in three frameworks across two companies. Google ADK, CrewAI and DSPy.

    What they have in common is the only definition I think is useful. An agent is a model in a loop with tools, and the model proposes calls that my code executes.

  2. Mechanism

    The design decision I would defend hardest is a restriction rather than a capability. In the vendor platform, agents never call each other.

0021 · 3D-RNN over Resting-State fMRI

The full chapter is here.

The line being defended
“Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder Prediction Using Resting-State Networks — 3D-RNN framework predicting ADHD from resting-state fMRI functional connectivity across 300+ scans.”
ICICV 2022, Springer · Lecture Notes in Networks and Systems · second of three authors
Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

For “tell me about the fMRI paper.” Say the sample size early and volunteer the confounds. Restraint is what makes this one land.

  1. Situation

    Children lie in a scanner doing nothing, and it records which brain regions rise and fall together over the session.

    The question is whether those patterns separate children with an ADHD diagnosis from those without.

  2. Mechanism

    An atlas divides the brain into regions, and you correlate every region's signal with every other's. That matrix is what the model reads.

    Three-dimensional convolutions read each volume and a recurrent layer reads the sequence. The same shape as the hand-pose paper, with a different encoder.

  3. What I did

    And the honest framing comes first. Millions of numbers per subject and three hundred subjects, so almost anything fits.

    Which means the validation protocol decides the result, not the architecture. That is the part an expert in this area asks about immediately.

  4. What it cost

    Two confounds carry the label directly. Children with ADHD move more, and motion changes measured connectivity. Site does the same thing.

    And in the public competition, an entry using only age, sex, handedness and IQ scored at the top with no imaging at all. Any imaging model has to beat that.

The sentence that sounds fluent and gets you caught

“We predicted ADHD from resting-state fMRI with a 3D-RNN across three hundred scans.” Every word of that is in the bullet and it invites the three questions this literature always gets. How was it split, what about head motion, and did it beat a model with no imaging in it. Answering those before they arrive turns a suspicious claim into a credible one.

The other trap is the word predicting near the word ADHD. It sounds like a diagnostic capability, and no imaging measure is a validated diagnostic for ADHD. Say classifying, and say plainly that this is a research result rather than a clinical one.

Explain it · one picture

The picture. Working out who in an office is on the same team by watching who goes for coffee at the same time. Nobody hands you the org chart. You infer the structure from what rises and falls together, over and over, with nothing being asked of anybody.

Now cover it and redraw

Sixty seconds. One chain, one sample-size band, two confounds, one baseline.

  • Correlation, not a wire. The definition that keeps you honest.
  • Millions of numbers, three hundred people. The framing sentence.
  • Two confounds, and why both carry the label.
  • The no-imaging baseline, because it is the answer nobody expects you to know.
Say “the protocol decides, not the architecture” as you draw the red band. It is the sentence that says you know this field.
