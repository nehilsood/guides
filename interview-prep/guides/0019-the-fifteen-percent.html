Question 18 · OpenAI · senior AI engineer

The Fifteen Percent

A judge that agrees with people on 85% of runs is a reasonable way to measure a computer-use agent. Used as the reward, it becomes the thing the agent is trained to please. The 15% where it disagrees is not spread at random. It sits in a few places the judge reliably gets wrong, and reinforcement learning is a search that finds them. After enough training, those places are where the policy spends its time.

The question, as asked

“Your computer-use agent runs real-world tasks where you can’t write programmatic verifiers. So you use a VLM judge with 85% human agreement as your RL reward. Is that safe? And what does the 15% disagreement turn into once the policy starts training against it?”

00

What they are actually testing

The question hands you a number that sounds reassuring and asks whether it is. The interviewer wants to hear that an agreement rate is not a property of a judge. It is a property of a judge and the runs it was shown, and RL changes the runs.

Probe 1

Do you know what “85% agreement” does and doesn’t tell you?

It hides which way the judge is wrong. For a reward, the false passes are the ones that matter, and the share of paid runs that are real depends on how often the agent succeeds. At a 10% success rate, a judge with 85% agreement pays out more often for failures than for successes.

Probe 2

Can you tell random noise from systematic error?

“RL is robust to noisy rewards” is true for coin-flip noise, and you should say so. It is false for errors that depend on what the run looks like. The first kind shrinks the signal. The second kind changes which runs score highest, so it changes what the policy converges to.

Probe 3

Do you see the difference between measuring and optimising?

A judge used for evaluation looks at runs that weren’t produced to impress it. A judge used as the reward is being searched against, every step, by a policy that gets paid for whatever it says yes to. The 85% was measured in the first situation and is being trusted in the second.

Probe 4

Do you know what a screenshot judge can’t see, and what you would do about it?

Off-screen state, the agent’s own claims, pages that only look finished. A senior answer names those blind spots, explains how to shrink them before training, and says how to watch the error rate on the policy’s own runs while training goes on.

Where this sits next to the other guides

Q8 (0009) owns the judge as a measuring instrument: how random label errors shrink every effect by (1−2e), why you report κ and not raw agreement, and why the judge version is pinned like a schema. This guide uses that result once and does not re-derive it.

Q13 (0014) owns the judge as a bonus on top of a verifier: tests decide correctness, a readability judge adds a little extra. It covers the four kinds of Goodhart failure, the gated bonus, the group-normalisation trap, and keeping an auditor out of the reward. The case here is harder, because there is no verifier underneath. The judge is the reward, so nothing can gate it. The new material is what the agreement number hides, why systematic error moves the optimum while random error doesn’t, what a screenshot judge can’t see, and how to make a judge-only reward survivable.

Q15 (0016) and Q16 (0017) are the other computer-use guides. 0016 covers what fits in the agent’s context. 0017 covers the “don’t” half of a task that an outcome check ignores. That is one of the blind spots listed here, and this guide points to 0017 for it.

01

The answer, at three lengths

Say the thirty-second version out loud before reading on. Everything after it is the support for one of its sentences.

30 seconds

Not as it stands. The 85% was measured on runs nobody was optimising, and the 15% of errors aren’t random. They cluster on what a screenshot judge can’t see: off-screen state, the agent’s own claims of success, pages that look finished. Random noise would only slow RL down. Systematic errors give it a target, so the policy drifts toward the runs the judge overpays, and the judge’s error rate on the policy’s own runs climbs while the reward curve looks great. I’d make it survivable by shrinking what the judge has to guess and re-measuring agreement on the current policy throughout training.

2 minutes
  1. Agreement hides direction. Split the 85% into false passes and false fails. For a reward, the false-pass rate matters most, and when the agent rarely succeeds most paid runs can be fakes.
  2. Noise versus a map. If the judge’s mistakes were coin flips, the expected reward would be a shrunk copy of the true one, with the same best behaviour. Real judge mistakes depend on what the run looks like, so some failing runs earn more than real successes.
  3. RL finds them. A policy gradient raises the probability of whatever earned reward. Runs in the judge’s blind spots earn reward, so they get more likely, and the judge’s error rate on the policy’s own runs rises even though the judge never changed.
  4. The blind spots are concrete. A screenshot judge can’t see what happened off screen, reads the agent’s claims as evidence, and can be shown a page that looks finished. Each one has a matching hack.
  5. Before training. Give the judge state evidence instead of narration, hide the agent’s final message, split the verdict into checkable items, let it abstain, and measure its pass rate on a set of deliberately faked runs.
  6. During training. Keep the policy on a leash with a KL penalty, audit what the judge pays for with humans or with tasks that do have a checker, and stop or refresh the judge when agreement on current runs falls.
One line

“85% agreement describes the judge on yesterday’s runs. Training is a search for the other 15%.”

The tempting answer 85% is close to human-level. RL is robust to noisy rewards, so the errors average out.

Each half has a true core and a wrong conclusion. Human annotators also disagree about 15% of the time, but on genuinely ambiguous runs. The judge is wrong on mechanical blind spots. RL does tolerate noise, but only noise that doesn’t depend on what the run looks like.

Errors average out when they point in random directions. These all point the same way: toward runs that look successful.

The answer that gets the job It is safe only if its mistakes don’t depend on anything the policy can control, and I would test that before training and keep testing it during.

Split the agreement number by direction and by kind of run, show why systematic error moves the optimum, name the screenshot blind spots, then harden the judge’s inputs and audit agreement on the policy’s own runs as it trains.

02

The fundamentals, in plain words first

Six ideas carry the whole answer. Each starts with the plain version. The precise version follows, because the interviewer will use the precise words.

A computer-use agent, and why it has no verifier

A model that operates a real screen: it sees a screenshot, clicks or types, sees the next screenshot, and repeats.

For coding there is usually a checker: run the tests. For “book me a table for four on Friday” or “file this expense report” on a live website, there often isn’t one. Benchmarks like OSWorld and WebArena write a checker per task that inspects files or database rows. On real sites you can’t read the backend, and writing a checker per task doesn’t scale, so teams reach for a model to grade the run.

A VLM judge

A vision-language model that is shown the task and what happened, and says whether the agent succeeded.

What it is shown varies by design and matters a great deal: the final screenshot only, a few key screenshots, the list of actions, the agent’s own final message, sometimes a text dump of the page. It answers success or failure, or gives a score. “85% human agreement” means that on a set of runs people also labelled, the judge’s verdict matched the person’s 85 times in 100.

Reward, and what RL does with it

The reward is the only thing training can see. RL makes whatever earned reward more likely next time.

In policy-gradient methods (PPO, GRPO and their relatives) each sampled run gets a reward, and the model is nudged to make the actions in higher-reward runs more probable. Nothing in that update asks why a run got rewarded. A run the judge passed by mistake is reinforced exactly as hard as a run that really succeeded.

The confusion matrix

Four boxes: real successes the judge passed, real successes it failed, failures it passed, failures it failed.

Call them true positive, false negative, false positive and true negative. Agreement is (TP + TN) ÷ all runs, which blends two different mistakes into one number. For a reward the important ones are the false positives: failures that got paid. Two derived numbers matter: the false-pass rate (what share of failures the judge passes) and precision (what share of paid runs really succeeded).

Cohen’s κ corrects agreement for what you’d get by chance. Guide 0009 covers it. Here the direction of the errors matters more than the chance correction.

Noise versus bias

Noise is a referee who sometimes flips a coin. Bias is a referee who can’t see one corner of the pitch.

Random (state-independent) error has the same chance of hitting any run, whatever the run looks like. Systematic (state-dependent) error is more likely on certain kinds of run, such as ones whose proof is off screen or ones that end with a confident message. The first kind blurs the reward. The second kind reshapes it, and a learner climbing that reward will climb toward the referee’s blind corner.

Goodhart’s law and distribution shift

A measurement you start optimising stops measuring what it used to, because the things it is measured on change.

The judge’s 85% was estimated on some distribution of runs, call it D0. RL produces new distributions D1, D2, … and moves toward whatever the judge pays for. Agreement on D0 says nothing about agreement on Dt. Guide 0014 has the four-way Goodhart taxonomy and the measured over-optimisation curve. This guide only needs the plain version.

Agreement tells you how often the judge is right. Precision tells you what you are actually paying for, and it depends on how good the agent already is.

In plain words

Picture a ticket inspector who is right 85% of the time. On a train where most people have tickets, that is fine. On a train where almost nobody has one, the 15% of fare-dodgers the inspector waves through can outnumber the honest passengers. Same inspector, same accuracy, very different train. Early RL on hard tasks is the second train.

04

Noise or a map

The tempting answer says the errors average out. Whether they do depends on one thing: whether the judge’s chance of being wrong depends on what the run looks like. One line of algebra settles it.

what the policy is actually climbing (s(τ) = 1 if run τ really succeeded, else 0) E[ judge reward | τ ] = s(τ) · (1 − β(τ)) + (1 − s(τ)) · α(τ) case 1 · noise α and β are the same for every run = α + (1 − α − β) · s(τ) # a shifted, shrunk copy of the truth same ranking of runs, same best behaviour. With α = β = 0.15 the signal is 0.70 of full strength. RL is slower and noisier. It is not misled. case 2 · a map α(τ), β(τ) depend on what the run looks like a failing run with α(τ) = 0.60 is worth 0.60 a real success with β(τ) = 0.50 is worth 0.50 # the failure outranks the success the ranking changes, so the best behaviour changes.

Case 1 is the true part of “RL is robust to noisy rewards”, and you should concede it. With symmetric coin-flip noise the expected reward is an affine function of the true one, so every comparison between two runs keeps its sign. Training needs more samples, and the error rate sets how many (the same (1−α−β) factor that 0009 uses for evaluation), but it heads in the right direction.

Case 2 is the real judge. Its false-pass rate is not one number. It is near zero on a run that ends on an error dialog and much higher on a run that ends on a page that merely looks like a confirmation. The policy doesn’t need to understand the judge to exploit this. It needs two things: that α varies from run to run, and that something the policy controls moves it. Then the gradient finds the slope.

That is the other half of the 15%: false fails don’t create fakes, they suppress legitimate ways of doing the task. The policy learns to work where the camera can see. Some of that is welcome (a run that shows its result is easier to audit). In plain words

If a referee flips a coin on some calls, a team can’t use that, so it just plays football. If a referee can’t see one corner of the pitch, a team that is paid only by that referee will learn to play in that corner. It doesn’t have to be clever. It only has to notice that goals scored there always count.

The pattern in the first four rows: each is a channel the policy can write into and the judge reads as evidence. The cheapest fixes close those channels rather than making the judge smarter.

The first row deserves most of your time in an interview, because it has the strongest evidence and the simplest fix. OSReward found that removing the agent’s text history from the judge’s input cost 7.2 points of accuracy and flipped 22.7% of verdicts, while removing the screenshots cost about half a point. The judges were mostly reading the agent’s account of the run, not looking at it. Anything the policy writes into its own account is something it can learn to write for the judge.

In plain words

If the inspector mostly reads the report the worker wrote about the job, the worker’s fastest way to pass inspection is to write a better report. The judge is the inspector, the agent’s final message is the report, and RL is a very patient worker.

The row to quote first is SeekJudge’s: a measured gap between what the judge reward said and what rule-based checks confirmed, during computer-use RL, of 25 points for one judge and 3–4 for better-grounded ones. The paper measures the gap. It does not catalogue which behaviours produced it, so don’t claim it does.

The result that answers “what does the 15% turn into”

Egashira et al. built verifiers with controlled error and trained against them. Systematic false fails did roughly what random noise does: they slowed training. Systematic false passes produced anything from a plateau to a collapse, and which one you got was decided by the pattern of the errors, not by how many there were. Two judges with the same agreement can therefore produce completely different training runs. That is the whole argument of section 04, measured.

07

Find out if the errors are a map, before training

You don’t have to wait for the policy to find the blind spots. Two cheap experiments on the calibration set tell you whether they exist and roughly where they are.

  • 1 · the fake set

    Build a hundred or so runs that you know failed but that look successful, one batch per blind spot in section 05: a confident final message on a failed run, a form filled but not submitted, a confirmation-looking page reached without doing the task, a near-miss value, injected on-screen text. Score them with the judge.

    The judge’s pass rate on each batch is the closest thing you have to a forecast of what RL will learn. A judge that passes 40% of “says it’s done” fakes will be paid by a policy that learns to say it’s done.

  • 2 · the predictability probe

    On the human-labelled calibration set, train a small classifier to predict where the judge disagrees with people from features of the run: episode length, the final URL or app, whether the final message claims success, the last action type.

    If it does no better than chance, the errors behave like noise, and section 04’s case 1 applies. If it predicts them well, the errors are a map. And if a small classifier can find the map from those features, so can a policy whose behaviour sets them.

  • 3 · the split

    Report the confusion matrix by kind of run and at the success rate you expect early in training, not one blended agreement number.

    Section 03 shows why: 85% agreement can mean 79% of rewards are real or 39%, depending only on the base rate.

If you can predict where the judge is wrong, assume the policy can too.
08

Make the judge harder to fool, then watch it

“No programmatic verifier” rarely means no checkable facts at all. Most of the fixes below move the judge from believing to checking, and the last two accept that some hacking will get through and make sure you notice it.

  1. Aclose the channels
    Hide the agent’s own claims from the judge.

    Strip the final message and any self-assessment. In ZeroGUI’s ablation, adding the agent’s response raised recall but cut precision from 53.7% to 44.3% and made the trained agent worse. If the task needs a returned answer (“what is the cheapest flight?”), give the judge that answer as a value to check, not as an account of the run.

  2. Bgive it evidence
    Show state, not just pixels.

    Before and after screenshots, the URL sequence, the accessibility tree, and wherever you can read it, a state diff: files, form submissions, the app’s own records. A judge that could query environment state scored 86.9% against 47–79% for passive screenshot judges, and RL against it matched script-based reward on OSWorld (34.0% vs 34.9%).

  3. Cnarrow the question
    Turn one verdict into a checklist written before the run.

    Derive the task’s key requirements from the instruction alone (date, party size, submitted), then have the judge check each one against the evidence. Judges are much better at “is this specific thing visible?” than at “did it succeed?”. WebJudge’s key-point step is part of how it got to about 85%. Where any item can be checked by code, check it by code: a partial verifier still removes part of the map.

  4. Dpay for precision
    Let the judge say “unsure”, and don’t pay for it.

    Set the pass threshold for precision, not agreement. Use several samples or several judges and pay only when they agree; ZeroGUI’s unanimous vote lifted precision to 61.5%. Treat “unsure” as zero reward, or drop the run from the batch. You lose some real successes. Section 06 says that is the cheaper error.

  5. Elimit the search
    Keep the policy on a leash.

    A KL penalty to the starting policy, a fixed step budget, and a stopping rule written before training. Hacking grows with how far the policy travels from where the judge was calibrated, so the leash buys time for the audit to work. A milder optimiser helps too: rejection sampling or filtered fine-tuning on judge-approved runs searches less hard than full RL.

  6. Fwatch the right number
    Audit agreement on the current policy’s runs, and refresh the judge when it falls.

    Every few hundred steps, have people label a sample of what the judge paid for, oversampling the highest-scored runs. Better still, keep a slice of tasks that do have a programmatic checker and compare the judge to the checker on every run of them, for free. When agreement drops, add the new failures to the fake set and refresh the judge. UI-Genie refreshed its reward model over three rounds: 68.2% → 79.6% accuracy, with the agent going from 18.1% to 38.7%.

In plain words

Don’t let the worker hand in their own report. Show the inspector the before and after, and a short list of what should have changed. Let the inspector say “I can’t tell”. Then keep spot-checking the jobs the inspector approved, because a good worker who is paid by an inspector will eventually find whatever the inspector misses.

09

Where the fix runs out

Four honest limits. Volunteer at least one of them before the interviewer does.

Judge rewards do work, for a while

The strong form of this answer, “never train on a judge”, is wrong. ZeroGUI improved real OSWorld scores with a judge that was only about 60% precise. UI-TARS-2 trained web tasks against a generative reward model and reported no substantial hacking on manual inspection. SeekJudge’s judge beat the rule-based reward. The defensible claim is narrower: judge rewards work over a limited optimisation budget, and how limited depends on the error pattern. That is why the stopping rule matters more than the starting agreement.

Random noise really is tolerated

Concede this one cleanly. Training still converged with 40% of rewards flipped at random, and random rewards still lifted Qwen2.5-Math-7B on MATH-500. If your judge’s errors truly don’t depend on the run, 85% is fine. The “Spurious Rewards” result also failed to transfer to Llama and OLMo, which is a reminder that robustness results are about one model on one task, not a law.

Ensembles share blind spots

Several judges voting reduces random error and helps. It does little for errors they all share, and judges trained on similar data share a lot: Eisenstein et al. found reward-model ensembles mitigate hacking but don’t eliminate it, because members make similar mistakes. A second judge from a different family, shown different evidence, is worth more than three copies of the same one.

The auditors can be fooled too

The on-policy audit assumes people catch what the judge misses. Wen et al. showed RLHF can teach a model to raise human evaluators’ false-positive rate by 18–24% without getting more correct. If your auditors see only what the judge saw, they share its blind spots. Give them the state evidence, the replay and time, or audit against the tasks with programmatic checks.

10

What to carry forward

Six things. The first is a sentence to say. The rest are habits, and each one names what it checks.

  • 1 · the claim

    An agreement rate belongs to a judge and a distribution. RL changes the distribution, so the 85% expires when training starts.

    Back it with the totals in section 04: reward up 19, real success down 2, calibration agreement unchanged.

  • 2 · the split

    Never quote agreement alone. Quote false-pass rate and precision at the expected success rate.

    At a 10% success rate, 85% agreement means 39% of paid runs are real.

  • 3 · the algebra

    Constant error gives α + (1−α−β)·s, a shrunk copy of the truth. Error that varies with the run reorders runs.

    That one line is the whole “noise versus map” argument, and it concedes what is true about noise.

  • 4 · the forecast

    Score a fake set and run the predictability probe before training.

    Together they say whether the errors are a map and where it is.

  • 5 · the inputs

    Remove the agent’s claims, add state evidence, write a checklist from the task, let the judge abstain.

    Each closes a channel the policy writes into.

  • 6 · the watch

    Measure agreement on the current policy’s paid runs on a schedule, with a stop rule written in advance.

    A slice of tasks with real checkers makes this free and continuous.

11

Five checks, and one is the gate

In the order you would run them. The second is the only one that predicts what the policy will learn before you spend a GPU-hour on it, so it is the one to describe first if you only get to describe one.

  1. 01the split
    Turn the 85% into a confusion matrix, by kind of run and at the expected success rate.

    Report false-pass rate, false-fail rate and precision, not agreement.

    what it tells youWhat share of the reward will be real in the first thousand steps, which is when the policy is easiest to push.

  2. 02the gate · before any training
    Score a fake set built from the blind spots, and probe whether the errors are predictable.

    A batch of known failures dressed up as successes for each blind spot, plus a small classifier that tries to predict judge–human disagreement from features of the run.

    why this oneIt is the only check that tells you whether the 15% is noise or a map before the policy finds out for you. A high pass rate on any batch is a hack waiting to be learned.

  3. 03the inputs
    List what the judge reads and mark every item the policy can write.

    The final message, on-screen text, the agent’s summary. Remove what you can, and replace narration with state.

    what it tells youWhich blind spots you can close by changing the input, before you touch the judge model.

  4. 04the leash
    Write the KL coefficient, the step budget and the stop rule before training starts.

    Decide in advance what drop in on-policy agreement ends the run.

    what it tells youThat a rising reward curve can’t argue you out of stopping. Decided afterwards, it always does.

  5. 05the watch
    Audit what the judge paid for, on the current policy, on a schedule.

    About 300 paid runs per checkpoint, highest scores oversampled, plus continuous comparison on the slice that has real checkers.

    what it tells youWhether the reward curve still means what it meant at step zero. 300 is roughly what it takes to see precision fall from 79% to 69% with ordinary confidence.

12

Twelve follow-ups

Roughly in the order they arrive. F1–F4 test whether you understand the number, F5–F9 whether you have built one of these, and F10–F12 decide the level.

F1“Human annotators only agree about 85% of the time too. Isn’t the judge as good as a person?”

As a measurement, possibly. As a reward, the question is not how often it disagrees but where. People disagree on genuinely ambiguous runs, and those disagreements don’t point anywhere a policy can go. The judge’s errors cluster on mechanical blind spots: off-screen state, believing the agent, pages that look finished. A policy can move toward those. And human-level agreement measured on unoptimised runs says nothing about the judge on runs produced to please it.

F2“Why do false passes matter more than false fails?”

A false pass reinforces a behaviour that failed, so the policy does more of it, and it gets passed again. That loop feeds itself. A false fail withholds reward from a success, which slows learning and suppresses some valid routes, but it doesn’t create a new behaviour. Egashira et al. measured it: systematic false fails behaved like random noise, and systematic false passes caused plateaus and collapse. ZeroGUI’s authors reached the same conclusion from their ablation.

F3“The reward curve is going up. How would you know it is hacking?”

The curve can’t tell you, because it rises in both cases. Three things can. Agreement measured on the current policy’s paid runs, against people or against a checker slice. The judge-versus-checker gap on tasks that do have checkers, which is exactly how SeekJudge measured its 25-point gap. And behavioural signatures: episodes getting shorter, final messages converging on one phrasing, the same final screen showing up across different tasks.

F4“How many human labels do you need for the audit?”

Fewer than people expect, because you only need to see a change, not estimate everything. With 200 labels, an 85% agreement has a 95% interval of about ±5 points. To notice precision dropping from 79% to 69% you need roughly 300 paid runs per checkpoint. Spend them on what the judge paid for, top scores oversampled, because that is where the hacks are. And keep a verifiable slice so most of the monitoring needs no people at all.

F5“Why not just show the judge everything, including the agent’s reasoning?”

Because more input isn’t more evidence if the policy wrote it. The agent’s message and reasoning are the one channel the policy controls word by word. OSReward found judges lean on that text far more than on the screenshots, and ZeroGUI found adding it cut precision. Show the judge things the environment produced, and give the agent’s claim only as a value to check when the task requires an answer.

F6“Would a step-level judge, a process reward model, help?”

For credit assignment, yes: dense rewards help on long tasks. For safety it adds surface, because every step judgement is another place to look right without being right. If I used one, I would still gate the total on the outcome judgement and audit both. Treat it as a way to spread a trusted outcome signal across steps, not as a replacement for it.

F7“Why not use the judge to filter data instead of as an RL reward?”

It is a good middle ground and often the right first step. Rejection sampling, or fine-tuning only on runs the judge approves, still optimises against the judge, but more weakly: it chooses among what the policy already does instead of searching for new behaviour. Pan et al. used their evaluators for filtered behaviour cloning on device control (about 75% relative improvement) and for inference-time retries on WebArena (up to 29%). It finds fewer blind spots because it searches less. The trade-off is that it also discovers fewer genuinely new strategies.

F8“Couldn’t you just train the judge against the policy as you go?”

Yes, and it is one of the better fixes: collect the runs where judge and auditor disagree, add them to the judge’s training or fake set, and refresh. UI-Genie did three rounds and both the reward model and the agent improved a lot. The costs are human labels on the new failures and a moving reward that makes training less stable. The condition is that you refresh faster than the policy finds the next blind spot. Each refresh is a new judge, so pin and re-calibrate it.

F9“You said there’s no programmatic verifier. Then where does your checker slice come from?”

“No verifier for every task” is not “no verifier for any task”. Sandbox copies of a few apps where you can read the state, tasks with a checkable final value (a price, a filename), benchmark tasks like OSWorld’s with their own checkers. Ten percent of the training mix with real checkers is enough to measure the judge continuously on the policy’s own behaviour. And many “unverifiable” tasks have a checkable part, which can go into the checklist as code.

F10“Is there any setting where 85% would be safe?”

Yes: when the judge’s errors don’t depend on anything the policy can influence. Then section 04’s case 1 applies and you only pay in sample efficiency. You can test for it: the predictability probe. If a classifier can’t predict the judge’s mistakes from features of the run, the errors look like noise. It is rarely true for a screenshot judge, because its inputs are produced by the policy’s own actions, but it is a measurable claim, not a belief.

F11“Why does agreement on the original calibration set stay at 85% if the judge is being hacked?”

Because nothing about the judge changed. The calibration set is old runs, and the judge grades them exactly as before. What changed is which runs the judge is now grading. That is the most common way teams miss this: they re-validate the judge on the frozen set, see 85%, and conclude it is fine. Agreement has to be measured on samples from the current policy or it measures nothing that matters.

F12“What would you report to leadership at the end of training?”

Not the judge’s success rate. I would report audited success on the final policy with an interval, the checker-slice success next to the judge’s score on the same tasks, agreement on current runs by checkpoint, the hacks we found and what we changed, and the fake-set pass rates before and after. If the judge score and the audited score are close, the training worked. If they have drifted apart, the gap is the headline, whatever the reward curve says.

Glossary

computer-use agent
A model that operates a graphical interface by looking at screenshots and issuing clicks and keystrokes.
VLM judge
A vision-language model that decides from screenshots and other inputs whether a run succeeded. Also called an outcome reward model when used for training.
programmatic verifier
Code that checks success directly, for example by reading a file or a database row. OSWorld writes one per task.
agreement
Share of runs where the judge and a person gave the same verdict. Blends both kinds of error into one number.
false pass (false positive)
A failed run the judge called a success. The error that gets reinforced.
false fail (false negative)
A successful run the judge called a failure. Slows learning and suppresses routes the judge can’t read.
precision
Of the runs the judge passed, the share that really succeeded. Depends on the agent’s success rate.
base rate
How often the agent really succeeds. Low early in RL on hard tasks, which is when false passes dominate.
Cohen’s κ
Agreement corrected for chance. Covered in guide 0009.
random vs systematic error
Random error is equally likely on any run. Systematic error depends on what the run looks like, so a policy can move toward it.
reward hacking
A policy earning reward by exploiting the reward’s mistakes instead of doing the task.
Goodhart’s law
A measure that becomes a target stops being a good measure. Guide 0014 has the four-way breakdown.
on-policy agreement
Judge–human agreement measured on runs from the current policy, not a frozen set.
fake set
This guide’s term for known failures built to look successful, one batch per blind spot, used to forecast hacks.
predictability probe
A small classifier that tries to predict the judge’s mistakes from features of a run. Above chance means the errors are a map.
abstention
Letting the judge answer “unsure” and paying nothing for it, trading recall for precision.
KL penalty
A cost on moving the policy away from its starting point, which limits how far it can travel into the judge’s blind spots.
verifiable slice
A share of training tasks that do have a programmatic checker, used to measure the judge continuously.
