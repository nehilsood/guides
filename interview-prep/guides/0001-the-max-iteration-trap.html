The Max-Iteration Trap

One question, four hidden defects, and the fundamentals you need to name them out loud.

The question

“Your self-correction loop improves output quality on every pass, but p99 latency just tripled in prod. Your teammate says 'just cap the max iterations.' Why is that a band-aid, and what's actually broken?”

The one-sentence answer

A cap doesn't remove the tail — it clips it, and it clips it on exactly the requests that needed the extra compute, so you trade a visible latency problem for an invisible quality problem. What's actually broken is that the loop has no per-request stopping rule, its quality claim is measured as an average instead of per request, and it's an uncontrolled retry loop inside a shared serving engine with no latency budget.

00

What the interviewer is actually testing

This is not a trivia question. It is a judgement question wearing systems clothing. Four things are being probed at once, and a good answer touches all four without being asked.

Probe 1

Symptom vs. cause

Can you tell the difference between a knob that makes a number go down and a fix that makes the system correct? Everyone can find the knob.

Probe 2

Do you think in distributions?

“Improves quality on every pass” is an average. “p99 tripled” is a tail. If you argue about the two as if they're the same kind of number, you've failed the question.

Probe 3

Do you know the serving stack?

Batching, KV cache, prefix reuse, queueing. A refinement loop is not just an algorithm — it is extra load on a shared engine, and that's where p99 usually comes from.

Probe 4

Can you disagree well?

Your teammate isn't wrong that you need a bound. Do you dunk on them, or do you ship their cap today as a guardrail and fix the mechanism properly? Seniority lives here.

01

The answer, at three lengths

Lead with the 30-second version. Interviewers interrupt; make sure the first thirty seconds contain the thesis, not the setup.

Say this — 30 seconds

“The cap is a real safety bound and I'd ship it today as a stopgap, but it's not a fix, because it doesn't change the shape of the latency distribution — it truncates it. And it truncates uniformly, on requests that are not uniform: the ones sitting at the cap are the hard ones that actually needed pass four. So I've converted a latency regression into a quality regression on my worst slice, and unlike latency, that regression isn't in my dashboard.

What's actually broken is three things. One, the loop has no stopping rule — it has no way to tell 'this is done' from 'this is not done', so it burns the whole budget on every request instead of exiting early on the easy ones. Two, 'quality improves every pass' is almost certainly a benchmark mean; per request, refinement both fixes and breaks answers, so I don't actually know what the extra passes are buying. Three, at the systems level this loop is an unbounded internal retry loop feeding the same batching engine, with no latency budget and no admission control — which is exactly why p99 tripled while p50 barely moved.”

Say this — 90 seconds (add these beats)
  1. Split the tail before theorising. “First I'd decompose p99: iteration count × per-iteration latency × queue wait. Those are three different bugs with three different fixes, and the numbers tell me which one I have.”
  2. Name the variance mechanism. “The reason p50 is flat and p99 tripled is that iteration count is now a random variable. Total latency variance picks up a Var(N)·E[X]² term. The tail of the total is dominated by the tail of the iteration count, not by the cost of one pass.”
  3. Name the queueing mechanism. “Extra passes raise effective arrival rate, so utilisation rises, and queue wait scales like 1/(1−ρ). My loop is also making other people's requests slow — including requests that never enter the loop.”
  4. Fix the stop signal, not the ceiling. “The mechanism I want is an external verifier — tests, a compiler, a schema check, a retrieval check, a small reward model — so the loop stops on ground truth. A model grading its own work is the thing that's missing, and it's the thing you can't get by tuning a constant.”
  5. Make the budget adaptive, then cap as a backstop. “Difficulty-conditioned budget from a cheap router, a wall-clock deadline rather than an iteration count, and then a hard cap plus circuit breaker as the last line of defence.”
  6. Restate the objective. “And I'd push back on the framing: the goal was never 'maximise quality'. It's 'maximise quality subject to a p99 SLO' — goodput, not accuracy. If we'd written the SLO down first, the loop would have had a budget from day one.”
Don't say
  • “We'll set max_iterations = 3 and ship it.” — that's the trap, verbatim.
  • “Just add a bigger GPU / more replicas.” — capacity hides an algorithmic bug and moves the cliff, it doesn't remove it.
  • “Cache the results.” — nothing here says the traffic is repeated.
  • “p99 doesn't matter, most users are fine.” — 1% of requests is 1 in 100; an agent making 20 model calls hits p99 about 18% of the time.
  • Anything that ends without naming a measurement you'd take. “What's broken” needs evidence, not a story.
02

Fundamentals: percentiles, and why p99 is the one that bites

Tail amplification: why 1% is never 1% of your users

If a single call has a 1% chance of exceeding your p99, and a user session makes k calls, the chance that session hits at least one slow call is 1 − 0.99k:

Interview-grade line

“In an agentic product, p99 per call is roughly p50 per session. That's why the tail is the SLO — not the mean.”

03

Fundamentals: where LLM latency actually comes from

You cannot reason about a latency regression in an LLM system without decomposing it. Two phases, two bottlenecks, two different metrics.

In plain words

Most requests are fine after one pass, so the typical request (the median, p50) barely got slower. What changed is that the number of passes now varies, and a few hard requests go round the loop many times. Those few make up the slowest 1% (p99), which is why it tripled. A cap trims them but never explains why they didn’t stop.

04

Why p50 stayed flat and p99 tripled

This is the single most impressive thing you can explain in this answer, because it is the observation the interviewer gave you and most candidates skate past it.

The mechanism, stated properly

Total latency is a compound random variable: a random number of passes N, each costing a random X. For the mean, the two just multiply. For the variance, a second term appears — and it's the one that owns your tail.

E[Total] = E[N] · E[X] Var[Total] = E[N] · Var[X] + Var[N] · E[X]² \_____________/ \______________/ cost jitter ITERATION-COUNT jitter (was always (this term is NEW, and it there) scales with E[X] SQUARED)
The line to deliver

“Adding a loop didn't make each pass slower — it made the number of passes uncertain. The Var(N)·E[X]² term is new, it scales with the square of the per-pass cost, and it lands almost entirely in the tail. That's why p50 moved 17% and p99 moved 3×. Capping N caps that variance by brute force, but the reason N has a fat tail is that nothing in the system knows when to stop.”

In plain words

Every extra pass is another request to the same shared model server, so the server gets busier. Waiting in a queue does not grow evenly: near full capacity, a little more load makes every wait much longer. So the loop slows down requests that never loop at all. The fix is a lower-priority queue for refinement passes, so they can’t crowd out first passes.

05

The loop is also slowing down requests that never enter it

This is the part that separates “I understand algorithms” from “I have run something in production.”

Every refinement pass is another request into the same inference engine. If 30% of requests take 3 extra passes, your effective arrival rate λ isn't up 30% — it's up ~90%. Utilisation ρ = λ/μ climbs, and queue wait does not climb linearly. It climbs like 1/(1−ρ).

06

Serving-stack specifics worth naming

Prefix caching — check this before you touch the algorithm

Say this — it lands very well

“Before I redesign anything, I'd check the prefix cache hit rate on passes 2..N. A correction loop is the ideal prefix-cache workload, and if we're missing, we're paying full prefill N times on a context that's monotonically growing. That's a config bug that looks exactly like an algorithm bug.”

In plain words

A model that checks its own work, with nothing outside to check against, sometimes “fixes” answers that were already right. An average over many requests can still rise while some answers get worse, and nobody sees those. So compare each request’s answer before and after the extra passes, and let an outside check, such as unit tests, decide what is right.

07

The quality claim is probably also wrong

“Improves quality on every pass” is the premise you should politely refuse to accept at face value. This is the highest-value pushback available in the whole question.

Intrinsic vs. extrinsic self-correction

TypeFeedback sourceWhat the literature finds
IntrinsicThe model critiques its own output from a prompt aloneFrequently degrades quality. Without ground truth, models can't reliably judge whether their previous answer was correct — so they “fix” correct answers.
ExtrinsicUnit tests, compiler, schema validator, tool output, retrieval, a trained verifier / reward modelThis is where the real gains are. The loop stops on evidence rather than on vibes.

The error-dynamics model — the crispest way to say it

Treat each iteration as a two-state Markov transition on correctness:

EIR = P(correct → incorrect) "error introduction rate" ECR = P(incorrect → correct) "error correction rate" Refinement stops paying when: ECR / EIR = Acc / (1 − Acc)

Read what that threshold demands. A model already at 91% accuracy needs ECR/EIR > 8.6 just to break even — it must be more than eight times better at fixing errors than at creating them, because the pool of correct answers it can damage is ten times larger than the pool of errors it can repair. Measured ECR/EIR for weaker models sits near 1.0. For those models, iteration monotonically degrades accuracy, and the empirical dividing line is brutally sharp: near-zero EIR (≲0.5%) separates loops that help from loops that hurt.

The pushback, phrased safely

“I'd want to see the quality number as a paired, per-request comparison, not an aggregate. Refinement both fixes and breaks answers, and a mean can rise while a subset of requests regresses. If pass 3 fixes 4% and breaks 3%, the dashboard says +1% and the users who regressed are invisible. Which means the cap isn't just cutting gains — it's cutting an unknown mix of gains and unmeasured regressions. We don't currently know which.”

Overthinking: more passes actively hurt past a difficulty-dependent point

  • Negative flips are real and common. In studied reasoning traces, ~67.5% of correct→incorrect transitions involve the model explicitly reconsidering and rejecting an answer that was right.
08

So: what is actually broken

Four named defects. Name them as defects, not as opinions — each one is a missing component, not a badly tuned parameter.

Defect 1 · Control

There is no stopping rule

The loop cannot distinguish “done” from “not done”, so it has no way to exit early on the ~90% of requests that were already correct after pass 1. It runs to whatever ceiling exists. A cap replaces one wrong global constant with a smaller wrong global constant; what's missing is a per-request decision.

Defect 2 · Evaluation

Quality is measured as an average

Aggregate improvement is compatible with a meaningful slice getting worse. Without paired per-request evals you don't know your EIR, you don't know whether the loop is above or below the break-even threshold, and you can't price the cap's quality cost.

Defect 3 · Architecture

Compute is spent in depth, not width

Sequential refinement is the one shape that converts GPU time into wall-clock latency 1:1, and it chains errors. Same budget spent as parallel sampling + aggregation is usually both faster and better.

Defect 4 · Systems

Latency is not a first-class constraint

No SLO, no per-request latency budget, no deadline propagation, no admission control, no priority separation between first passes and refinement passes, no circuit breaker. The loop can consume unbounded shared capacity, which is why the blast radius includes requests that never entered it.

In plain words

The real fix gives the loop a way to know it is done: an outside check, such as running the tests, that stops it once the answer passes. Easy requests then get one pass and hard ones get more, all inside a time limit. The cap stays only as a last-resort safety limit.

09

The fix, in the order you'd actually do it

Present this as a sequence with the cheap, reversible things first. The cap survives — it just moves to the end, and it changes role from mechanism to guardrail.

  1. Ship the cap today, as a stopgap, and say so. “I'd take my teammate's change immediately — bleeding p99 is worse than a small quality risk for a day. I'd also add a wall-clock deadline alongside it, because iterations are a proxy and time is the thing we're actually defending.” Then instrument, and keep going.
  2. Decompose the tail before redesigning. Log per-request: iteration count N, queue wait per pass, prefill tokens per pass, prefix-cache hit rate, decode tokens, and the stop reason. Plot the distribution of N. Compare p99 of E2E conditioned on N. This tells you which of three bugs you have: fat N tail, growing per-pass cost, or queueing contention.
  3. Fix the cheap infrastructure wins. Prefix/KV cache reuse across passes; don't resend a monotonically growing transcript when a diff will do; separate priority class or queue for refinement passes so first-pass TTFT is protected; scale on concurrent-request count, not GPU%.
  4. Put in a real stop signal — external, cheap, asymmetric. Run the unit tests. Run the compiler. Validate against the schema. Execute the tool. Check the citation actually exists. Score with a small reward/verifier model. Stop when the check passes. Cheap deterministic checks are the best deal in the whole design: near-zero latency, ground-truth signal. Where no deterministic check exists, use oscillation and confidence-trajectory detectors and a fixed-point test (if pass k+1 differs from pass k below a threshold, you've converged — stop).
  5. Make the budget adaptive, not uniform. A cheap difficulty router assigns the budget: easy requests get 1 pass, hard requests get 5. Optimal budget varies ~7.5× across difficulty, so uniform allocation is wrong for nearly every request. Set each bucket's budget where marginal accuracy per millisecond falls below your threshold — an explicit efficiency frontier, not a guess.
  6. Convert depth to width where you can. Where you have a verifier, best-of-N + verify is one wall-clock round instead of N. Where you have no verifier, self-consistency over independent samples beats sequential refinement at matched compute. Reserve sequential passes for cases where the critique genuinely carries information the first pass couldn't have.
  7. Make it anytime and deadline-driven. Always hold a returnable best-so-far. Compute the budget as remaining_deadline / expected_pass_cost and propagate the deadline down the call chain. Under load shedding, degrade to one pass rather than dropping the request — graceful degradation, not a cliff.
  8. Write the SLO down and optimise the right objective. The target is not “maximum quality”; it's maximum quality subject to a p99 latency SLO — i.e. goodput. Add the error budget and burn-rate alerts, a guardrail metric for quality regression on the hard slice, a canary, and a circuit breaker that trips the loop off entirely when the tail budget is burning. Now the cap is a correct backstop, because something smarter is doing the real work.
Closing line

“So: the cap is a fuse, and I do want a fuse. But right now the fuse is the design. What I want is a loop that stops because it has evidence it's done, that gets a budget proportional to how hard the request is, and that can't spend other requests' latency budget to buy its own quality. Once those three exist, the cap almost never fires — and that's how you know it's a guardrail rather than a policy.”

11

Follow-ups they will ask

Twelve probes, ordered roughly by how likely they are. Each answer is written to be said aloud in under a minute.

F1

“Your verifier costs latency too. Haven't you just moved the problem?”

No, because the economics are asymmetric — and I'd show the arithmetic. A refine pass is a full generation: prefill plus hundreds of decode tokens. A verify step can be a unit-test run (milliseconds, no GPU), a schema validation (microseconds), or a small reward model doing a single forward pass with no long decode. Call refine 800 ms and verify 40 ms. Today I pay 4 × 800 = 3200 ms unconditionally. With a verifier I pay 40 ms × passes + 800 ms × passes_actually_needed, and passes-needed is 1 for most traffic. Expected cost drops even though I added a component.

The second half of the answer is that I only need the verifier to be right about stopping, not about grading. That's a much easier calibration problem, and I can tune the threshold asymmetrically: a false “keep going” costs one pass of latency; a false “stop” costs quality. I'd pick the operating point from that cost ratio, not from accuracy.

F2

“There's no verifier for this task. Open-ended prose. Now what?”

Then I stop running a sequential loop, because sequential refinement without ground truth is the configuration the literature says degrades quality. Three moves, in order:

  • Find a proxy verifier. Fully open-ended is rarer than it looks — structural validity, schema conformance, factual claims checkable against retrieval, constraint satisfaction (“did it stay under 200 words / cite three sources / avoid the banned list”), or a tool that executes something. Partial ground truth is still ground truth.
  • Switch depth for width. Independent samples plus aggregation, or plus a pairwise preference model. One wall-clock round, no error chaining.
  • Escalate instead of iterating. A cascade: cheap model first, and on low confidence route once to a stronger model rather than looping the weak one. One escalation is bounded latency; a loop isn't.

And if none of that is available, I'd say so plainly: then the honest design is a fixed small budget, and we should stop calling it self-correction and start calling it a fixed-cost second draft.

F3

“p50 didn't move but p99 tripled. What does that fact alone tell you?”

That the regression is in the tail of the iteration-count distribution or in load-induced queueing — not in the base cost of a pass. If per-pass cost had risen, p50 would have risen too. So most requests are exiting after one pass (good — that means an early-exit signal partly exists, or the loop isn't always entered), and a minority are running to exhaustion.

The two candidates are distinguishable: replay identical traffic at low fixed load. If the tail survives, it's the algorithm — a hard slice genuinely runs long. If it collapses, it's contention, and my loop is stealing capacity from unrelated requests. I'd check the correlation between p99 and concurrent-request count before I'd check anything about the model.

F4

“Fine — how do you set the budget, if not a constant?”

Three inputs, none of them a hand-picked integer:

  • Difficulty. A cheap router or a difficulty estimate from the prompt assigns a budget per bucket. Optimal allocation varies ~7.5× across difficulty levels, so bucketing is where most of the win is.
  • Deadline. Budget = remaining_deadline / expected_pass_cost, recomputed each pass and propagated from the caller. Time is what the SLO is written in, so time is what the loop should be governed by. An iteration count is a proxy that goes stale the moment prompt lengths change.
  • Marginal value. Per bucket, stop where marginal accuracy per millisecond falls below a threshold you choose from the SLO. Published cost-balanced operating points land around 50% compute reduction for ~6% accuracy loss, or ~97% of peak accuracy at 60% of compute — pick the point deliberately, on an efficiency frontier, and treat it as a product decision rather than a tuning artefact.
F5

“What if the loop never converges — it just oscillates between two answers?”

That's a real and common mode, and it's detectable rather than merely survivable. Fixed-point detection: if the diff between pass k and k+1 is below a threshold, you've converged — stop, don't spend the rest of the budget. Oscillation detection: track the answer across passes; a flip-flop pattern is the strongest single predictor of a correct→incorrect transition (r≈0.78). On detecting oscillation, don't pick the latest — return the earliest stable answer or aggregate across passes, because “latest” is a bad default when the process isn't contracting.

Framed properly, this is a control-stability question: the loop is a feedback system and it converges only if the correction rate dominates the error-introduction rate. If it's oscillating, the loop is not contracting, and no iteration budget will make it converge. That's the case where you stop iterating entirely rather than tuning the ceiling.

F6

“Isn't quality more important than latency here?”

Past a threshold, latency is quality — and past the client's timeout, latency is an error. If our timeout is 5 s and p99 is 7.2 s, we don't have a slow product, we have a >1% failure rate, plus retry load that makes the tail worse. So it isn't a trade between two goods; it's a constraint.

The right framing is: set the SLO from actual user need, then maximise quality subject to it. And the SLO is workload-specific, not universal — voice agents need ~150 ms p99 TTFT because TTS adds 100–200 ms downstream; inline code completion needs sub-100 ms to feel native; a batch/async job can happily take 3 s. If this loop is serving an async review pipeline, tripling p99 might be entirely fine and I'd say so. The bug is that nobody wrote the number down, so we can't tell.

F7

“Why not just add capacity? GPUs are cheaper than engineering time.”

Sometimes that's the right call, and I'd cost it rather than dismiss it. But three caveats. First, tail SLOs are bought with headroom, and headroom is superlinear: tightening p99 TTFT from 500 ms to 200 ms at the same RPS costs roughly 35% more infrastructure. Second, capacity fixes the queueing component and does nothing for the algorithmic component — if the tail persists on a low-load replay, no amount of hardware helps. Third, the loop's cost multiplier hits $/request as hard as it hits latency, so scaling up pays the multiplier at a higher unit count. Adding capacity to an uncontrolled amplification loop is how you get a bill that scales with a bug.

F8

“Walk me through how you'd ship the real fix safely.”

  • Write the SLO first — p99 E2E target, error budget, burn-rate alerts (a 14.4× burn over an hour pages).
  • Offline first. Replay production traffic; compute paired per-request quality deltas, not aggregates; report the regression rate on the hard slice as a named guardrail metric.
  • Shadow. Run the verifier in shadow mode to measure its stop-decision precision/recall against known outcomes before it controls anything.
  • Canary on a small traffic slice, watching both the tail and the guardrail. Latency wins that cost quality must be visible in the same review.
  • Kill switch and circuit breaker. Loop off entirely under tail-budget burn; degrade to one pass rather than dropping requests.
  • Leave the cap in, as a fuse, and alert when it fires — because a firing cap now means the smarter mechanism failed, which is information.
F9

“How does this interact with the inference engine specifically?”

Four concrete interactions. Prefix cache: refinement passes share a long prefix, so hit rate on passes 2..N should be near 1; if it isn't, we're re-prefilling a growing context N times and that alone can explain a 3× tail. Batch slots: loop requests are long-lived; concurrent-request count predicts tail TTFT better than GPU utilisation, so autoscale on concurrency, around 24–28 rather than waiting for 80% GPU. KV memory: long-lived requests raise cache pressure, and pressure causes preemption and re-prefill — a cliff that appears only in the tail. Scheduling: continuous batching removes queue-level head-of-line blocking but not the token-iteration-level version, and FCFS treats a 5th refinement pass as equal in priority to someone's first request. That's the policy I'd change first: refinement passes go in a lower-priority class so they can never crowd out first passes. That's the same “prioritise originals over retries” remedy used against retry storms.

F10

“You keep saying the loop is like a retry storm. Make that precise.”

A metastable failure needs a trigger and a sustaining positive feedback loop. Trigger: a load spike or a capacity loss. Sustaining loop: higher latency → more requests in flight → refinement passes queue behind first passes → less effective capacity for first passes → higher latency. The defining symptom is that the system stays degraded after the trigger goes away, because the amplified work is now self-sustaining. The remedies transfer directly: a global budget on in-flight refinement work (not per-request — total, so the amplification factor is bounded system-wide), a circuit breaker that drops to one pass, and separate queues so originals beat retries. I'd add: an uncapped loop is worse than a retry storm in one way, because it's internal — no client-side backoff will ever throttle it.

F11

“Suppose the extra passes genuinely do help, every time, measured properly. Is the cap fine then?”

Then the cap becomes an explicit, priced quality/latency decision rather than a band-aid — and that's a legitimate place to land, provided three things are true. One, the budget is adaptive, so the cap isn't spending easy requests' compute on nothing; a uniform cap wastes budget on easy items and starves hard ones even when refinement always helps. Two, the cap is expressed as a deadline and the algorithm is anytime, so you always return the best answer available at the deadline instead of a truncated one. Three, you've decided what happens to the requests that hit the ceiling — silently returning a worse answer is different from telling the caller “partial”, and for some products that distinction matters a lot.

So: same conclusion, different reason. Even in the best case the fix isn't “cap iterations”, it's “budget time, adaptively, and degrade gracefully.”

F12

“Your teammate is in the room and is annoyed. What do you actually say to them?”

“Let's ship your cap this afternoon — the tail is bleeding and I'd rather have a fuse than an argument. I want to add a wall-clock deadline next to it, because prompt lengths drift and an iteration count won't hold. Then can we spend a day on instrumentation before we pick the number? If the tail turns out to be queueing, the cap is treating the wrong system, and I'd hate for us to tune a constant for a month over something that's a scheduler config.”

Why this matters: the question includes a teammate on purpose. The failure mode candidates fall into is treating “band-aid” as an invitation to be contemptuous. Take the stopgap, name the follow-up, and keep the disagreement about the system rather than about the person.

Glossary

Continuous batching
Scheduling at token-iteration granularity so new requests join a running batch immediately, instead of waiting for the whole batch to finish.
HOL blocking
