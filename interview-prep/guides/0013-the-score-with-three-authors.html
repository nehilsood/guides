Question 12 · Meta · senior AI engineer

The Score With Three Authors

A model everybody agrees is stronger scores lower. Before that is a fact about the model, notice what else moved between the two runs: somebody rebuilt the image, somebody bumped the harness, and the only thing written on the result was the model’s name. A benchmark score is a joint measurement of a model, a harness and an environment — reported as a property of one of them. Everything else in this question follows from that one mislabelling.

The question, as asked

“We moved our coding eval from reference-similarity scoring to execution-based tests. This quarter, a model everyone agrees is stronger scores lower than last quarter’s model on the same Docker benchmark. What do you investigate before you call it a regression?”

What they are actually testing

Read the verb. They did not ask what went wrong with the model; they asked what you investigate — and then put the word “regression” at the end of the sentence as something you are allowed to withhold. The question is whether you treat a benchmark as a result or as a piece of equipment.

P1

Do you know that a comparison has two arms, and that you only controlled one of them?

Last quarter’s number came out of last quarter’s container, last quarter’s harness and last quarter’s task list. This quarter’s came out of today’s. Three things changed and one of them has a name on it. Any candidate can say “I’d look at the failures”; the senior move is to notice that there is no valid comparison yet to have failures of.

P2

Do you understand what execution-based scoring actually fixed, and what it quietly kept?

It fixed the thing BLEU got wrong: an implementation no longer has to look like the reference. But the reference did not disappear. It became a test suite — executable, invisible, and now a dependency-carrying piece of software that somebody has to maintain. You traded a metric you could read for a metric you have to run.

P3

Can you tell a measurement failure from a measurement?

A dashboard reports one number for “failed”. Underneath it are a container that could not start, a test suite that ran out of wall clock, a diff that never applied, and a wrong answer. Only the last of those is about the model’s coding, and in a bad quarter it is the minority. Sorting them is the first move, and it costs nothing but attention.

P4

Do you know what a control is, and have you ever run one?

The benchmark ships with the correct answers. Running those correct answers through today’s container is a positive control, it takes one command, and almost nobody does it on a schedule. If the reference solution fails, you are not holding a measurement of a model. You are holding a measurement of a broken machine, and you were about to attribute it to a person.

Where this sits next to Q6, Q8 and Q11

Four questions in this series are about evaluation and they fail in four different places. Q6 asked what a benchmark is structurally blind to — it scores what varied and never sees the constraints that did not. Q8 asked whether a number has a counterfactual behind it. Q11 asked whether the test data was already inside the training data. Q12 asks whether the two numbers you are subtracting came out of the same instrument.

Q11 and Q12 are the closest pair, and they are worth holding together because they share one tool and no mechanism. Q11’s cheapest move was to re-split the data and re-run the old model; Q12’s cheapest move is to re-run the old model in the new environment. Different defect, same instrument: the baseline you already have, run again under the conditions you are worried about. If you only remember one habit from either guide, remember that one.

01

The answer, at three lengths

Lead with the control, not the taxonomy. The taxonomy is the better-known half of this answer and it is the second thing you say, because sorting failures only helps once you know the machine still works.

Ten seconds

“Whether it is a regression at all. An execution benchmark is software with dependencies, and it decays. So first I’d run the gold patches through today’s container — if the reference solutions fail, the benchmark broke, not the model. Then I’d sort every failure by cause, because only assertion failures on validated tests say anything about the model.”

A minute

“The comparison is between two quarters, and the model is not the only thing that differs between them. The image was probably rebuilt, the harness probably moved, and anything in that image that was pinned loosely has drifted. So before I look at a single model output I’d do two cheap things. Run the known-good solutions through today’s container — that is a positive control and it tells me whether the instrument still reads true. And re-run last quarter’s model on today’s harness, which costs one inference run of a checkpoint I already have and turns a cross-time comparison into a like-for-like one.”

“Then the taxonomy. Every failure goes into exactly one of four buckets: infrastructure error, timeout, patch did not apply, assertion failed. The first two are about the machine. The third is about the model’s edit format, which is a real capability but not the one we are claiming to measure — published patch-apply rates differ by seventeen points between agents on the same tasks. Only the fourth is a statement about whether the code was right.”

“And then I’d be honest about the size of the move. Two points on a few hundred tasks at pass@1 is inside the noise band for most suites, and the published spread between replicate runs of the same system is one and a half to two points. If the drop survives all of that, then it is a regression, and I’d go read the failures.”

Three minutes

Everything above, then the part that separates a careful engineer from a thorough one — the three controls and what each is blind to:

The gold patch is a positive control, and it cannot see over-specification. Running the reference solution catches environment drift, because drift breaks everything equally. But a test that is over-fitted to the reference implementation — it checks a background colour nobody asked for, or an exact error string — will always pass the reference, by construction. The gold patch is the thing the test was written against. It is structurally incapable of detecting that the test is too narrow.

The negative control is the one nobody runs. Apply an empty patch, or the code as it stood before the fix, and the failing tests must still fail. If they pass, that task is not testing anything and every score it ever contributed was free. This is how you catch the opposite error: tests so weak that incorrect code gets through, which means last quarter’s number may have been inflated and you are comparing against something that was never real.

The baseline re-run makes the comparison valid without needing to know what broke. This is the one I would insist on. Grouping, pinning and auditing all require you to enumerate what might have changed; re-running the old checkpoint requires you to enumerate nothing. Environment drift moves both models down together and preserves the gap. A real regression attacks the gap specifically. One run tells them apart.

What none of the three can do is read. Over-specification is only visible to a human looking at a sample of the rejected patches and asking whether they are wrong or merely different. That is the expensive check, it does not automate, and it is exactly the one that OpenAI eventually ran on SWE-bench Verified — finding that 59.4% of the hard failures they audited came from flawed tests rather than from the models.

Then say what you will and will not announce. “The model regressed” is not supportable yet. “The score fell 2.3 points; 31 of the 100 failures were infrastructure, the gold patches pass at 94% on today’s image, and the old checkpoint scores lower on it too” is — and it is a sentence that ends an argument instead of starting one.

Do not say I’d look at the failed outputs and work out what the new model got wrong.

It assumes the answer key is still correct, and it starts with the most expensive activity available. Reading failures is slow, skilled work, and if a third of them are container errors you will spend a day discovering that a day was the wrong unit.

The second-worst answer is the opposite reflex: “re-run it, it’s probably noise.” Often true, and it covers exactly one of the five things that could be wrong. It is also unfalsifiable as stated — if you do not say how many runs and what interval would change your mind, you have not proposed a measurement, you have proposed a delay.

Say instead An execution benchmark is software with dependencies, and it decays. I validate it with gold patches first, then sort failures by cause. Only assertion failures on validated tests count as a regression.

Names the category error, names the control, names the gate. Add one clause and it goes further: “… and I’d re-run last quarter’s model on today’s harness, because that is the only version of this comparison where the model is the only thing that changed.”

02

What execution-based scoring fixed, and what it quietly kept

The question opens by telling you the team migrated from reference-similarity to execution. That clause is not scene-setting. It is the premise the rest of the answer argues with, so it is worth being able to say precisely what the migration bought and what it charged.

Reference-similarity scoring compares the generated code to one known-good answer as text. BLEU, borrowed from machine translation, counts overlapping n-grams; CodeBLEU adds syntax-tree and data-flow terms. The defect is obvious once stated: two correct programs can share almost no tokens. Rename the variables, swap a comprehension for a loop, return early instead of nesting, and a perfect answer scores badly. Evtikhiev and colleagues measured how badly the metrics track human judgment and concluded that on the datasets they studied, no metric could reliably reproduce a human ranking when two models were within five BLEU points of each other — which is most of the differences anybody argues about.

Execution-based evaluation — pass@k, as introduced with HumanEval — replaces the comparison with a question: does it run, and does it pass the tests? That is a genuine advance and it is the right default. But it is worth being exact about what changed, because the exactness is the whole answer to this question. What changed is which set of programs gets accepted.

C = the set of programs that are actually correct what you want to measure R = the set of programs close to the reference TEXT what BLEU accepted T = the set of programs that PASS THE TEST SUITE what execution accepts BLEU's failure R is far smaller than C huge false-negative rate and it is not even a subset a wrong program can score well Execution's failure T is a much better stand-in for C. It is still not C. T minus C = weak tests wrong code passes score too high C minus T = over-specified tests right code fails score too low And one failure mode BLEU did not have at all: T is not a fixed set. It is whatever the suite accepted on the machine, on the day.

Those last two lines are the pivot of this entire question. BLEU was a pure function of two strings: give it the same pair of strings in 2019 and in 2026 and it returns the same number, on any machine, forever. A test suite is not a function of two strings. It is a program with a Python version, a dependency resolution, a filesystem, a network, a clock and a CPU budget, and every one of those is a thing that can be different today.

The sentence to carry

Reference-similarity scoring was wrong in a way you could read. Execution-based scoring is wrong in a way you have to run — and it is wrong in two directions at once, one of which inflates your score and one of which deflates it.

You did not remove the reference when you moved to execution. You compiled it. The hidden test suite is the reference, rewritten in a language you cannot skim, and it has acquired a build.

This also explains why the newsletter’s wrong answer — “look at the failed outputs and work out what the new model got wrong” — is wrong rather than merely slow. It is the right procedure for the previous metric. Under BLEU, a low score is unambiguously a statement about the generated text, because nothing else is in the computation. Under execution, a low score is a statement about a joint outcome, and reading the outputs is step four of four.

Read the grid as a design of experiment and the problem is immediate: the two numbers you have differ in both the model and the environment, so any difference between them is attributable to either, in any proportion, including proportions that cancel. A five-point environmental penalty masking a three-point model improvement produces exactly the two-point drop on the dashboard, and nothing in the data distinguishes that story from the obvious one.

Filling the shaded cell costs one evaluation run of a checkpoint you already have. It needs no retraining, no new data, no annotation and no meetings. And it is strictly more informative than reading failures, because it answers the question that was asked — is this a regression — rather than the question you wish had been asked, which is why.

Why the top-right cell is usually gone, and why that is the real lesson

A Docker tag is a mutable pointer. python:3.11-slim in June and python:3.11-slim in September are two different images with the same name, and when your CI rebuilds and pushes evalimg:latest, the previous one is unreferenced and will be collected. The digest — sha256:… — is the immutable identity, and it is the thing nobody records.

So: your Dockerfile is in version control. The thing it builds is not. A Dockerfile is a recipe whose ingredients are fetched at build time from the open internet, which means it is a script for producing an environment, not a record of one. The analogy that matters here is not source code; it is a lockfile. Teams that have internalised package-lock.json for applications routinely have nothing equivalent for the machine their benchmark runs on.

Recording the image digest alongside every reported score is roughly four lines of CI and it converts an unrepeatable experiment into a repeatable one. It is the cheapest structural fix available in this entire question and it is almost never in place before somebody has been burned.

04

How a container rots, with numbers

“Someone rebuilt the image and an unpinned dependency moved” is the right story. It is also the story everybody tells and nobody has measured, which is a shame, because it has been measured and the figure is memorable.

Ghaleb and colleagues took 8,132 open-source projects that built successfully, left the Dockerfiles completely untouched, and rebuilt them repeatedly over a period of nine months. Same recipe, same command, no code changes. 798 of them — 9.81% — produced at least one build that behaved differently. Roughly one project in ten, from doing nothing at all, across about the span between two quarterly evaluations.

The causes are not evenly spread, and the shape is worth memorising because it tells you where to look first:

Two readings. First, three-fifths of it is dependency resolution — so the single highest-yield question about any benchmark image is “what does it install, and how tightly?” Second, a fifth of it is pure transience, which is exactly why a rebuilt image gets a free pass: it fails, somebody retries, it works, and the incident closes without anybody asking what changed in the layers.

The part that is genuinely counter-intuitive

An image that is never rebuilt does not drift. The drift arrives with the rebuild — and rebuilds are not triggered by anyone wanting to change the benchmark. They are triggered by a base-image CVE, a registry migration, an Apple Silicon laptop needing an arm64 variant, a CI cache expiring, or somebody tidying a Dockerfile on a quiet Friday.

Which means the benchmark tends to break when somebody does their job properly, and it breaks quietly, between quarters, with no commit that mentions the evaluation. That is why this failure mode survives teams that are otherwise disciplined, and why the defence has to be a control you run every time rather than a rule about who may touch what.

And a frozen image is not a frozen experiment. The things that still move with every byte of the image identical: an expiring CA bundle (the cause of 5.24% of flaky builds above), any test that reaches a package index or an API, any test that depends on the current date — a certificate fixture that expires, a timezone whose rules changed, a leap day — and the host’s CPU count, which decides how many test workers run and therefore whether a slow suite finishes inside its timeout.

05

Three controls, and the one thing none of them can see

A laboratory does not accept an instrument reading without putting a known sample through it first. Evaluation harnesses are the only measuring equipment in the building that are routinely trusted without that step, and the reason is simply that nobody framed them as equipment.

You have three controls available. Two of them ship inside the benchmark already, one of them is a checkpoint on disk, and together they cost an afternoon. What matters is being able to say which defect each one catches — because they do not overlap, and the one everybody names is the one with the most interesting blind spot.

  • Positive control

    Run the gold patches through today’s container. They must all pass.

    This is the check the newsletter leads with and it deserves the position. It catches environment drift cleanly, because drift is indiscriminate: a dependency that moved under you breaks the reference solution exactly as readily as it breaks a model’s. If the known-good answer fails, you are not holding a measurement of a model.

    Blind spot, and it is structural: a gold-patch control cannot detect an over-specified test. If the hidden test checks an implementation detail nobody asked for — a background colour, an exact exception message, a particular keyword argument — the gold patch passes it by definition, because the gold patch is the implementation the test was written against. The positive control is the thing the fault is defined relative to, so it can never see the fault.

  • Negative control

    Apply an empty patch, or the code exactly as it stood before the fix. The target tests must still fail.

    Almost nobody runs this one, and it catches the error pointing the other way: tests so weak that incorrect code satisfies them. If the suite passes on code that does nothing, that task has been awarding free points to every model that ever touched it — including last quarter’s, which means the number you are comparing against was partly fictional.

    Blind spot: a negative control proves a test can fail. It says nothing about whether it fails for the right reason, and nothing about over-specification either.

  • Baseline re-run

    Score last quarter’s checkpoint on today’s harness and today’s image.

    The one I would argue hardest for, because of a property the other two do not have: it requires no enumeration. Pinning, auditing and gold patches all demand that you first think of what might have changed. Re-running the baseline demands nothing — whatever moved, moved for both arms. Environment decay pushes both models down together and preserves the gap between them. A genuine regression attacks the gap specifically. One run separates those two worlds, and it is a run of a model you already have.

    Blind spot: it validates the comparison, not either number. A benchmark whose tests are systematically too narrow will depress both arms equally, and the re-run will cheerfully confirm that the comparison is fair while both numbers remain wrong.

  • No control exists

    Over-specification. Somebody has to read the patches.

    Take a sample of the assertion failures — twenty is enough to learn something, and it is an afternoon — and for each one ask a single question: is this patch wrong, or is it merely different? There is no automated version of this check, because deciding that an alternative implementation is also correct is precisely the judgment the test was supposed to encode and failed to.

    This is the expensive check, and it is the one that found the biggest published result in this area.

The result that makes this concrete

SWE-bench Verified is the most heavily human-audited coding benchmark in existence. OpenAI built it in 2024 by having 93 professional Python developers annotate 1,699 instances of the original SWE-bench, discarding everything where the issue was underspecified or the tests rejected valid solutions, and keeping 500 that survived.

In February 2026 the same organisation announced it would stop treating that benchmark as a measure of frontier capability. They had audited 138 of the hardest tasks — 27.6% of the set — and found that 59.4% of them had flawed test cases that reject functionally correct submissions, with 35.5% of the audited set failing specifically because the tests were too narrow. Meanwhile the reported state of the art had moved from 74.9% to 80.9% in six months, and models were reproducing gold patches in ways that implied they had seen them.

Read that pair carefully, because the obvious reading is the wrong one. It is not that auditing does not work. It is that an audit is a measurement with a date on it, and the properties it certified were not permanent.

The controls are already in the dataset. They just have a timestamp on them.

Here is the detail that turns this from advice into a diagnosis. SWE-bench-style benchmarks define each task with two sets of tests: FAIL_TO_PASS, which must fail before the fix and pass after it, and PASS_TO_PASS, which must pass in both states. Those two sets are the positive and the negative control. They were computed by actually running the tests, twice, against the gold patch and against the unfixed code.

They were computed once, on the day the dataset was built, against the environment as it existed that day. Nothing re-establishes them afterwards. So the honest statement of the problem is not that these benchmarks lack validation — they were validated carefully, by people who understood exactly this risk. It is that the validation is a cached result, and nobody set an expiry on the cache.

Which gives you a very concrete thing to say in the room, and it is better than any amount of general caution: “the dataset already contains the control. Re-running it on today’s image is the first thing I’d do, and if it is not green I would not look at the model at all.”

06

One word for four different events

A resolve rate is a single number, and underneath it sits a word — failed — doing four jobs at once. Splitting it is the cheapest analytical step in the whole investigation: no re-runs, no compute, no new data. The information is already in the logs and nobody looked.

Start from what the metric is really computing. A task counts as resolved only if every stage of a pipeline succeeds, so the headline is a product:

resolve rate = P(patch applies) × P(container and setup succeed) × P(suite finishes inside the wall clock) × P(assertions pass | all of the above) ↑ the only factor the claim is about

Four factors multiply into one score, and a drop anywhere looks identical on the dashboard. Worse, three of those factors are properties of the harness and the machine, so the headline number has a well-defined sensitivity to things nobody is claiming to have improved.

The patch-apply bucket is worth more attention than it gets

It is the bucket most likely to be the real cause of the scenario in the question, because it moves when the harness moves and it has nothing to do with reasoning. Published patch-apply rates across coding agents on the same task set range from about 79% to 96% — a seventeen-point spread that is pure format compliance. And the mechanism is well understood: asking a model to emit unified-diff text directly is fragile, because line numbers, hunk counts, context lines and trailing newlines all have to be exactly right. Switching the harness so that the model edits files and the runner exports the diff drops apply failures below 1.5%.

So: a model post-trained on a different edit convention can write entirely correct fixes that never land. Your benchmark will report that as a coding regression, your deck will report it as a capability gap, and the fix is four lines in the scaffold. Always report patch-apply rate next to resolve rate. If it moved, nothing below it is interpretable.

The honest objection, before the interviewer makes it

“Infra errors are part of reality. If the agent can’t work in our environment, that’s a real failure.” Sometimes fair — if the agent itself caused the container to die, that is about the agent. The distinction worth drawing is between failures the agent caused and failures it merely suffered, and the test for it is the gold patch again: run the reference solution on the same task. If the container dies for the reference too, the agent did not cause it.

And note the asymmetry in how you should be wrong here. Excluding a failure that was genuinely the model’s makes your score too kind, which is a bias you can describe. Including a failure that was the cluster’s makes your score a function of an unrelated system, which is a bias you cannot describe at all. When in doubt, exclude and report the exclusion count — the count is what keeps it honest.

07

Your test suite is a classifier, and nobody reports its error rate

The hidden tests decide, for each submitted patch, whether it is correct. That is a binary classifier. We would never ship a classifier without knowing its precision and recall, and yet this one is deployed on every model in the company and its error rates are unpublished, unmonitored and — this is the part that matters — not constant over time.

the patch is the patch is actually correct actually wrong +------------------+------------------ suite says PASS | fine | FALSE POSITIVE | | weak test | | your score is too high +------------------+------------------ suite says FAIL | FALSE NEGATIVE | fine | over-specified | | your score is too low +------------------+------------------

Both errors are measured, both are large, and they have been measured on the single most scrutinised benchmark in this field.

False positives: the number you are comparing against

The SWE-Bench+ study re-examined patches that the benchmark had marked as successful. 31.08% of the passing patches were judged suspicious because the tests were too weak to establish correctness, and a further portion passed because the fix was written out in the issue text (which is leakage — a different defect, and the subject of Q11 in this series; the point here is only that it inflates the same number). With the problematic instances filtered out, one widely-reported agent configuration fell from 12.47% to 3.97%.

UTBoost attacked the same problem by generating additional tests and re-running the leaderboard’s own submissions. It found 345 patches that had been recorded as passing and were wrong, traced to insufficient tests on 5.2% of SWE-bench Verified instances and 7.7% of Lite. The consequence is the bit worth remembering: 24.4% of Verified leaderboard entries and 40.9% of Lite entries were affected, producing 11 and 18 ranking changes respectively. Not a philosophical concern. Positions on a public table, swapped.

For this question, the implication is direct and slightly uncomfortable: last quarter’s 41.2% may never have been 41.2%. If a fraction of those passes came from tasks whose tests accept wrong code, the comparison was already against an inflated figure before anything drifted.

False negatives: the error that grows as your models improve

This is the half the newsletter gestures at — the “add a Learn More button, top right” task whose hidden test also pins the background colour — and it deserves a sharper statement than “underspecified”, because it has a property that none of the other failure modes in this question have.

The sixth cause, which is not on the usual list

A benchmark’s false-negative rate rises with the capability of the thing you point at it — with no drift, no rebuild and no change to the benchmark at all.

The mechanism is simple once stated. An over-specified test only produces a wrong answer when the submitted patch is correct but different. While models are weak, they fail for ordinary reasons and the test’s narrowness never gets exercised. As models get stronger, the failures that remain concentrate precisely on the tasks where the model produced a valid alternative and the test insisted on the gold one.

That is exactly the distribution OpenAI reported: they audited the hard, still-unsolved tasks, and 59.4% of them turned out to have flawed tests. Those tasks did not become flawed. They became visibly flawed, because a model finally got good enough to be wrong in the benchmark’s blind spot.

So a stronger model really can score lower on an unchanged benchmark in an unchanged container. It is rarer than environment drift and it is much harder to detect, which is why the answer to this question ends in reading patches and not in a script.

Both errors push the same way, and it is the wrong way for you

Worth reasoning through once, because it reframes benchmark saturation as partly an artefact rather than entirely a ceiling. The argument below is a derivation, not a measurement — say so if you use it.

  • Weak tests compress the gap. A free pass helps whichever model would otherwise have failed. The stronger model was going to pass anyway, so the weaker one collects most of the benefit.
  • Over-specified tests also compress the gap. They punish correct-but-different solutions, and a stronger model is more likely to produce a solution that is genuinely its own rather than a reconstruction of the canonical one.

Both defects therefore act to hide improvement. An ageing benchmark does not simply become noisy; it becomes systematically harder to improve on for reasons that have nothing to do with the difficulty of the tasks. Which gives a cleaner reading of what happened to SWE-bench Verified: the state of the art moved 74.9% to 80.9% and then compressed into a narrow band at the top, and the remaining 19.1 points were substantially unreachable rather than merely hard.

There is a mature way to measure this and benchmark builders rarely use it

“Are these tests any good?” is not a new question. Software engineering has had a principled answer for decades: mutation testing. Introduce small, deliberate faults into the code — flip a comparison, drop a statement, change a constant — and measure what fraction the suite catches. That fraction, the mutation score, is a direct estimate of the suite’s false-positive rate, which is the quantity nobody publishes for a benchmark.

It is not free — you run the suite once per mutant — but it is a background job on a fixed task set, and it is exactly the sort of thing a benchmark should carry in its metadata. A benchmark that published a mutation score per task would let you weight tasks by how much their verdict is worth. None of them do. If you are asked in an interview how you would make an execution benchmark trustworthy over multiple years, that is a strong and specific thing to propose.

08

How much of two points is nothing at all

Noise is the last item on the newsletter’s list and the first one you can settle with arithmetic rather than opinion. Doing that arithmetic out loud is one of the cheapest ways to sound like somebody who has run evaluations rather than read about them.

Take the scenario at face value: a few hundred tasks, pass@1, 41.2% down to 38.9%. With 500 tasks and a pass rate near 0.4, the standard error on either number alone is

SE = sqrt( p(1−p) / n ) = sqrt( 0.41 × 0.59 / 500 ) = 0.0220 i.e. 2.2 points so a single reported score carries roughly a ±4.3 point 95% interval and the reported difference is 2.3 points.

Two scores measured independently give a difference with standard error sqrt(2) × 2.2 = 3.1 points, so a 2.3-point gap is not within a mile of significant. Pairing helps, and it is what you should do — the two models saw the same tasks, so the right test is on the tasks that disagreed. If 60 tasks flipped from fail to pass and 48 from pass to fail, the paired standard error is sqrt(60+48) / 500 = 2.1 points. Better, but not transformative, and the conclusion is the same: the headline difference is inside the noise band of the experiment that produced it.

Two refinements worth knowing about, because they are where the published practice is.

  • Standard errors on evals should usually be clustered. Benchmark tasks are not independent draws — several tasks come from the same repository, the same issue tracker, the same template. Treating them as independent understates the variance, sometimes substantially. Miller’s Adding Error Bars to Evals is the short, practical reference: clustered standard errors, variance reduction by pairing, and how to size an eval so it can detect the difference you care about before you run it.
  • Power is the question to ask first. “How many tasks would I need for a 2-point difference to be detectable?” has an answer, and for pass rates near 0.4 with a paired design it is in the low thousands. If your suite is 500 tasks, you have pre-committed to not being able to resolve 2 points. That is a fine choice — but then 2 points must never be reported as a result, in either direction.

Where the randomness actually comes from, including at temperature zero

A common and wrong belief is that temperature 0 removes sampling variance and therefore makes the run deterministic. It removes the sampler’s randomness. It does not remove the arithmetic’s. Thinking Machines ran 1,000 completions of one prompt at temperature 0 on Qwen3-235B and got 80 distinct completions; the first 102 tokens were identical in every one and the divergence began at token 103.

The cause is that the kernels are not batch-invariant: a matrix multiplication accumulates floating-point values in a different order depending on how many requests were batched together, so the same input row produces a slightly different logit depending on who else happened to be in the batch. Fixing the three operations that matter — RMSNorm, matmul and attention — gave bitwise-identical output across all 1,000 runs, at a throughput cost of about 61.5% in the reference implementation, later brought down to roughly 34%. For an agent that takes dozens of sequential model calls, a one-token divergence early in a trajectory is amplified into an entirely different sequence of edits.

And the suites themselves are flaky independently of any of that. At Google, 1.5% of all test runs are flaky and around 16% of 4.2 million tests have failed at least once without a code change. That is the baseline rate for ordinary engineering test suites run by a company with unusually good infrastructure; a benchmark assembled from a few hundred third-party repositories will not do better.

Putting it together, the published spread between replicate runs of the same system on SWE-bench-style evaluations lands around 1.6 to 2.0 points, with cross-run standard deviations near 0.8 to 1.0 — which brackets the entire difference in the question.

The reporting trap on the other side

If run-to-run spread is two points, then running three times and reporting the best is worth about two free points to anyone who does it, and nothing in a results table reveals that they did. This is the quiet reason leaderboards compress at the top: the cost of an extra run is small and the incentive is one-directional.

Two habits make you immune and both are cheap. Fix the number of runs before you see any of them, and report the mean with an interval rather than the maximum. And when you read somebody else’s number, the useful question is not “is this real?” but “how many runs, and which one is this?” — asked neutrally, because the honest answer is very often “one, and we don’t know.”

09

What you check before you call it a regression

Five checks. One of them is a gate — meaning the word “regression” does not get used until it comes back, and the other four are things you report whether or not anybody asked. All five fit inside a day and none of them requires training anything.

  1. Check 01preconditionthe instrument
    Run the gold patches through today’s container.

    They are already in the dataset. Apply each reference solution, run that task’s target tests, and count. The expected result is 100%; anything less is a count of tasks that have stopped being measurable, and each one was contributing a silent zero to your score.

    Report it as a rate, not as a boolean, and report it on every run from now on — it is the single cheapest piece of permanent instrumentation available. A gold-patch pass rate printed at the top of every eval report means the next person to hit this spends ten seconds on it instead of nine days.

    Reads as “94.2% of gold patches pass on today’s image, against 100% at dataset build. Twenty-nine tasks are currently unmeasurable and I have excluded them from both arms.”

  2. Check 02gatethe comparison
    Re-run last quarter’s model on today’s harness, and report the gap rather than the level.

    This is the gate, and it is worth being clear about why it outranks the gold-patch check even though that one is more famous. Gold patches validate the tasks. They do not validate the scaffold. A harness that changed its edit format, its retry policy, its tool set or its parallelism will leave every gold patch passing and still move a model’s score by several points. The baseline re-run is the only check that covers the tasks, the image and the scaffold in one pass, and it does so without requiring you to guess in advance what moved.

    The reading is mechanical. If both models drop by roughly the same amount, the environment decayed and the model is fine — possibly better. If the old model holds its score and the new one does not, you have a regression, and now reading failures is the right thing to do. If the old model drops further, you have an improvement that the benchmark was hiding.

    It is also the only item on this list that measures your system rather than citing somebody else’s published hygiene. Everything in sections 05 and 07 is evidence that this class of problem exists. This is the measurement that tells you whether you have it.

    Reads as “On today’s image the old checkpoint scores 37.4% against the 41.2% we reported in Q3, and the new one scores 38.9%. The environment cost us 3.8 points and the model gained 1.5. There is no regression and I’d like to correct the Q3 figure.”

  3. Check 03reportthe attribution
    Sort every failure into exactly one bucket, and put patch-apply rate next to resolve rate permanently.

    Infra error, timeout, patch did not apply, assertion failed. The information is already in the logs; what is missing is the column. Timeouts get one extra pass — re-run only those, serially, with ten times the wall clock — which converts the most ambiguous bucket into a clean one for a handful of minutes of compute.

    Patch-apply rate deserves standing next to the headline for the same reason a conversion funnel shows its first step: if it moved, nothing downstream is comparable. A model that complies with your edit format 81% of the time cannot beat one that complies 96% of the time, whatever either of them knows about code.

    Reads as “Of 100 failures: 31 infra, 12 timeout of which 4 survive a 10× budget, 19 patch-apply, 38 assertion. Apply rate fell from 94% to 81% when we changed the edit format in v4.6.”

  4. Check 04reportthe suite
    Run the negative control, then read twenty assertion failures by hand.

    The negative control is scriptable: apply an empty patch and confirm the target tests still fail. Any task where they pass has been handing out free points, in both quarters, and should be removed from both.

    The hand-read is not scriptable and there is no substitute for it. Twenty failures, one question each: wrong, or merely different? You are estimating the one quantity none of the controls can reach, and a rough answer is enormously more useful than no answer. “I read twenty and four of them were valid alternative implementations the test rejected” is a sentence that changes a decision.

    Reads as “Three tasks pass with an empty patch and are removed. Of twenty assertion failures I read, four were correct patches rejected for implementation detail, which puts a rough ceiling of 7 or 8 points of false-negative rate on this suite.”

  5. Check 05reportthe interval
    Say how many runs, fix that number in advance, and publish a confidence interval rather than a point.

    Three runs is the usual floor and five is better. Decide before you look. Cluster the standard errors by repository, because tasks from one codebase are not independent draws. And state the smallest difference your task count can actually resolve — if 500 tasks cannot distinguish 2 points, then 2 points is not a finding and should not be in a title.

    The piece people forget: this applies symmetrically. The same interval that protects you from a false regression protects everyone else from your false improvement next quarter.

    Reads as “Three runs each, means 38.6% and 37.1%, paired difference 1.5 points with a 95% interval of −2.6 to 5.6. We cannot resolve this at 500 tasks and I am not going to pretend otherwise.”

The artefact: an eval run that describes itself

Each guide in this series ends with the small document the failure would have prevented. Q7 wanted a provenance field, Q8 an evaluation contract, Q9 a decomposition manifest, Q10 a tokenizer change request, Q11 a split specification. This one is a run record — and the useful thing about it is how ordinary most of it looks, because most of it already exists somewhere in your CI. Only two fields are genuinely missing from every real eval configuration I have seen, and they are marked.

# eval-run.yaml claim: "ckpt q4-0929 is better at resolving real issues than ckpt q3-0714" # write this line FIRST. it is what every field below is in service of, # and it is the thing that stops being true when the harness moves. comparison: arm_a: {model: ckpt-q3-0714, run: 2026-07-14, image: sha256:1f0c9a, harness: v4.2} arm_b: {model: ckpt-q4-0929, run: 2026-09-29, image: sha256:9ab3e7, harness: v4.6} held_constant: [task_set, scoring, timeout_s] # THE FIRST MISSING FIELD. the only honest version of this list is # COMPUTED by diffing the two run records, never asserted by a human # from memory. a human writing this list writes what they intended. changed: [model, image, harness] # three. the claim names one. controls: gold_patch_pass_rate: at_dataset_build: 1.000 on_arm_a_image: unknown # image was overwritten on_arm_b_image: 0.942 # 29 tasks currently unmeasurable empty_patch_pass_rate: 0.006 # target: 0.000. three tasks are free points baseline_rerun: model: ckpt-q3-0714 on: arm_b_environment score: 0.374 # vs 0.412 reported in Q3 failures: total: 100 infra: 31 # excluded, reported timeout: 12 # 4 survive a 10x wall clock patch_apply: 19 # apply rate 0.81, was 0.94 under v4.2 assertion_gold_also_fails: 9 # task is broken, drop from BOTH arms assertion_on_validated_test: 29 # the evidence. n = 29, not 100 overspecification_sample: {read: 20, correct_but_different: 4} noise: runs_per_arm: 3 # fixed before looking. not 1. cluster_by: repository paired_difference: {point: 0.015, ci95: [-0.026, 0.056]} min_detectable_difference: 0.041 # at n=500. we cannot resolve 2 points. decision: gap_old_model_across_environments: -0.038 # THE SECOND MISSING FIELD. if this is empty, the review has not begun. call_regression_if: "arm_a holds its score on arm_b's environment and arm_b does not" withhold_if: "gold_patch_pass_rate on_arm_b_image is below 1.000" narrow_to: "no regression detected; environment cost 3.8pp; correct the Q3 figure"

The two marked fields are the whole difference between a run record and a comparison record. Everything else here is logging, and most teams have it scattered across a CI artefact, a notebook and somebody’s memory. Pulling it into one file that lives next to the score is a half-day of work and it makes the question in this interview answerable in ten minutes rather than nine days.

Twelve follow-ups

The first three are near-certain if you give the answer above, because each one tests whether you said it from understanding or from a list. F12 is the one worth rehearsing word for word.

F1You ran the gold patches and every one of them passed. Now what?

“Then the tasks are intact and I’ve ruled out the loudest cause. I have not ruled out the scaffold. Gold patches are applied by the harness’s patch path, not generated by the model, so they exercise almost none of what changed if we bumped the agent loop — edit format, tool set, retry policy, context budget, parallelism. A gold patch validates the task; it does not validate the scaffold.”

“So I go straight to the baseline re-run, and I’d also look at patch-apply rate, because a harness change that moves apply rate from 94% to 81% is worth several points and leaves every gold patch green.”

F2We can’t re-run last quarter’s model. The checkpoint is gone and the API version was retired.

“Then the thing I actually need is not that model. I need any constant arm — something whose true capability cannot have changed, run on both environments. If it scores differently, the environment moved.”

“Three of those are usually available. The gold patches themselves are a perfect constant arm and I already have them. A small open-weights model pinned by hash is a cheap one, and its absolute score being bad is irrelevant — I only care that it is the same model. And a scripted non-model baseline, even something trivial like ‘revert the file’, gives a floor that should be stable.”

“Going forward I’d make that permanent: a frozen canary system whose only job is to be re-run on every image build. It costs almost nothing and it converts this entire investigation into a chart.”

F3Why not just pin everything by digest and never rebuild?

“Pin by digest, yes, always — that part is free and we should do it today. Never rebuilding is not achievable and not even desirable.”

“Not achievable, because base images get withdrawn for CVEs, registries migrate, and the day somebody needs an arm64 variant you are rebuilding whether you planned to or not. Not desirable, because a benchmark environment that has been frozen for two years stops resembling the environment the model will actually work in, and you have traded a drift problem for a validity problem.”

“And a frozen image is not a frozen experiment anyway. Certificates expire inside it, anything that touches the network still touches today’s network, and tests that depend on the date keep depending on the date. So the goal isn’t immutability. It’s recorded identity plus a control that fires when the identity changes: store the digest with every score, and re-run the gold patches on every new digest.”

F4Excluding infrastructure failures sounds like massaging the number in your favour.

“It is, if it’s done after seeing the results. It isn’t, if three conditions hold: the exclusion criterion is causal and written down before the run, the excluded count is published alongside the score, and it is applied identically to both arms.”

“The test for ‘causal’ is the gold patch again — if the reference solution also kills the container on that task, the agent did not cause it. And the asymmetry matters: wrongly excluding a model failure makes the score too kind, which is a bias I can state and bound. Wrongly including a cluster failure makes the score a function of our Kubernetes scheduler, which is a bias nobody can state at all.”

F5How do you choose the timeout?

“Not as a global constant, which is what almost everyone does and which guarantees that the slowest ten per cent of repositories are measuring your hardware. Set it per task, as a multiple of how long the gold patch takes on today’s machine — something like ten times, measured during the same gold-patch run I already need for the positive control. That makes the budget a property of the task and the machine rather than a guess, and it re-derives itself automatically when the hardware changes.”

“Separately: the authoritative run should be serial, and the fast run can be parallel. If a parallel result disagrees with the serial one on timeouts, the parallel one is wrong. Sixteen test workers on four cores is a measurement of core count.”

F6Execution evals are slow and expensive. Can we use a cheap similarity metric for day-to-day iteration?

“Yes, with one condition: a proxy is allowed to steer, never to decide, and you have to measure its correlation with the real metric rather than assume it. Then re-measure that correlation every so often, because the relationship drifts as the models change.”

“And know the resolution limit of the proxy before you use it. The published finding on code metrics is that when two models are within about five BLEU points, no similarity metric reliably reproduces a human ranking — so a proxy like that can tell you ‘this run collapsed’ and cannot tell you ‘this run is one point better’.”

“Worth noticing that the modern cheap proxy is an LLM judge, and it has exactly this guide’s problem one level up: the judge gets upgraded, and your historical scores silently stop being comparable. A judge needs a version, a pin and a re-run on the old set, for the same reason the container does.”

F7Reading twenty patches doesn’t scale. How would you find over-specified tests at scale?

“You can get most of the way with disagreement signals and then the last mile is human, and I’d say that plainly rather than oversell a pipeline.”

“The cheap signals: collect patches for the same task across several models and harnesses — a task that many systems fail with patches that differ a lot from each other is a strong suspect, because genuine difficulty produces similar near-misses while over-specification produces a scatter of valid alternatives. Compare against any task set where the same issue is solved by a human in a different way. And for the opposite error — weak tests — mutation score is the standard, principled measurement and it does automate.”

“But generating extra tests, which is what UTBoost does, catches tests that are too loose. It cannot catch tests that are too tight, because deciding an alternative is also correct is exactly the judgment the test got wrong. That one needs a person, and I’d budget for it rather than pretend otherwise.”

F8Every check comes back clean. The model is stronger and the score is genuinely lower. Now what?

“Then it’s a real finding and an interesting one, and the first thing I’d say is that ‘2.3 points worse’ is almost certainly the wrong description of it. I’d look at which tasks flipped in each direction, because a model that gains sixty tasks and loses forty-eight is not slightly worse. It is differently shaped, and the net is hiding both halves.”

“Then I’d segment: by repository, by language, by patch size, by how many turns the agent took, by whether the task needed a new file. Regressions that are real almost always live in a segment — a context length, a language with less post-training data, a tool the new model uses differently. A uniform 2-point drop across every segment is much more likely to be the harness than the model, which sends me back to check 02 with a sharper question.”

F9Isn’t the real problem contamination? These models have memorised the benchmark.

“It’s a real problem and it is well evidenced — models identify the correct file path from a SWE-bench issue description alone about 76% of the time, against 53% on tasks from repositories outside the benchmark, and when OpenAI retired the Verified set they reported frontier models reproducing the human gold patch verbatim. But it is a different defect with a different remedy and I’d keep them apart.”

“Contamination makes the benchmark too easy and inflates everyone. Decay makes the benchmark wrong and moves scores in either direction. Neither control catches the other: re-running gold patches tells you nothing about memorisation, and a fresh uncontaminated task set tells you nothing about whether your container drifted.”

“And note which way contamination cuts in this scenario. If the old model had more exposure to these tasks than the new one, contamination is a reason last quarter’s number was too high — which is another path to ‘stronger model, lower score’ with nothing wrong at all.”

F10The harness team says nothing changed.

“That’s a memory claim, and I don’t doubt it — I just can’t use it. ‘Held constant’ has to be computed by diffing the two run records, not asserted: image digest, harness commit, task-list hash, timeout, concurrency, decoding parameters, and the resolved dependency tree inside the image.”

“In my experience the diff usually turns up something nobody counts as a change, because it wasn’t one: a transitive dependency of the harness itself, a default that moved in a library upgrade, a base image rebuilt under the same tag. Nobody edited anything. The build just resolved differently, which is the whole point of this question.”

“If we genuinely cannot produce those two records, then the honest statement is that we do not have a comparison, and the fix is to start recording them today rather than to argue about last quarter.”

F11Forget how we run it. What would you change about how the benchmark is built?

“Five things, roughly in order of cost. Ship images, not Dockerfiles, and address them by digest — a recipe is not a record. Carry the harness inside the benchmark, versioned with it, so a score names a complete apparatus rather than half of one.”

“Re-validate the controls on a schedule and publish the date: run the gold patches and the empty patches monthly, and put the resulting pass rates in the dataset card. That single change would have surfaced most of what has been found retrospectively about these benchmarks. Carry a mutation score per task so a consumer can weight tasks by how much their verdict is worth. And make the task set append-only with release dates, so anybody can slice to a window after a given model’s cutoff.”

“That is not a wish list — the newer efforts do several of these already, which is a reasonable argument that it is achievable rather than ideal.”

F12If you had to leave us with one habit from this, what would it be?

“That a result is a difference between two runs, and you usually only controlled one of the things that differed. Every score is a joint measurement of a model, a harness and an environment, and it gets written down under the model’s name, because the model is the part anybody cares about. The mislabelling is so natural that nobody notices it is happening, and it is where this whole failure lives.”

“The habit is not distrust of bad news. Distrust is free, and a reviewer who distrusts everything is just slow. It is one question, asked early and without any edge to it, of every comparison including the ones that flatter me: which of these two runs was I actually able to hold still?”

“And the thing that makes it a habit rather than a slogan is that the answer is almost always cheap. Re-run the thing you already have. It is the same move whether you are worried about the data, the environment or the scaffold, and it is nearly always an afternoon.”

Your numbers, not mine saved in this browser as you type

Row 2 is the one that earns the conversation, and almost nobody prepares it. Every team has an environment that somebody built once, nobody owns, and everybody describes as “pinned”. Being able to say out loud, unprompted, exactly how tightly yours is pinned — and that you know the difference between a tag that looks immutable and one that is — is the most senior thing available in this exchange.

If the honest answer to row 4 is “we have no controls at all and we report a single run”, write that. It is extremely common, it is a better sentence than an invented process, and it is precisely the gap section 09 exists to close.

Glossary

The terms that make the rest of the answer compress. The three worth knowing cold are gold patch, positive and negative control, and the difference between a tag and a digest.

execution-based evaluation
Scoring generated code by running it against tests rather than comparing it to a reference text. Accepts any implementation that passes — which is a superset of the ones that look right, and still a subset of the ones that are right.
reference-similarity metric
BLEU, CodeBLEU, ChrF and relatives: score the output by its textual or structural overlap with one known-good answer. Cheap, deterministic, and blind to correct code that looks different.
pass@k
The probability that at least one of k samples passes the tests. Reported with an unbiased estimator rather than by sampling k and checking, because the naive version is biased upward.
resolve rate
On agentic benchmarks, the share of tasks where the submitted patch applied, ran and passed the target tests. A product of four probabilities, only one of which is about correctness.
gold patch
The reference solution shipped with the task — usually the human commit that actually fixed the issue. Doubles as a positive control, and is structurally unable to detect over-specification.
positive control
A sample known to produce a pass. If it fails, the instrument is broken and no other reading from it means anything.
negative control
A sample known to produce a fail — typically an empty patch or the pre-fix code. If it passes, the test is not testing.
FAIL_TO_PASS
Tests that must fail before the fix and pass after it. The task’s definition of success, and a cached result of running both controls once, on the day the dataset was built.
PASS_TO_PASS
Tests that must pass in both states, guarding against a patch that fixes the issue by breaking something else.
patch-apply rate
The share of model outputs that git apply accepts. A property of the model’s edit-format compliance and of the harness, and not of its reasoning. Report it next to resolve rate or neither is interpretable.
unified diff
The patch format with hunk headers and line counts. Brittle to generate directly, which is why harnesses increasingly have the model edit files and export the diff themselves.
harness
Everything around the model: prompt, tool definitions, edit format, retry policy, context budget, turn limit, scoring code. Versioned separately from both the model and the image, and therefore the variable most often forgotten.
image tag
A mutable pointer to an image. python:3.11.9 looks like a version and behaves like a branch name — it is rebuilt when its base gets patched.
image digest
The content hash, sha256:…. The only identity that means the same bytes tomorrow. The field to record next to every score.
pinning
Constraining what a build resolves to. A spectrum, not a boolean: tag, exact version, digest, lockfile with hashes, and finally no network at test time.
transitive dependency
A dependency of a dependency. Pinning your direct requirements leaves these free to move, which is the most common way a “pinned” image changes.
environment drift
The environment changing without anybody intending to change it — almost always at a rebuild, and almost always triggered by routine maintenance rather than by a decision about the benchmark.
Dockerfile flakiness
The same unmodified Dockerfile producing different build outcomes at different times. Measured at 9.81% of projects over nine months, three-fifths of it dependency resolution.
flaky test
A test that passes and fails on identical code. 1.5% of runs at Google; a floor you inherit when you assemble a benchmark from third-party repositories.
over-specified test
A test that enforces implementation details the task never required, rejecting correct alternatives. The false negative. Invisible to a gold-patch control, and more visible the stronger the model gets.
weak test
A test that passes on incorrect code. The false positive, and the reason a historical baseline may never have been real.
mutation score
The share of deliberately introduced faults a test suite catches. The mature, automatable measurement of test-suite strength, and a field that benchmark cards do not carry.
failure taxonomy
Sorting every failure into infra error, timeout, patch did not apply, or assertion failed. Costs nothing, changes no score, and decides which failures you are entitled to make a claim about.
clustered standard error
A variance estimate that accounts for tasks arriving in correlated groups — several from one repository. Treating benchmark items as independent understates uncertainty.
batch invariance
The property that a kernel’s output for one row does not depend on how many other rows were in the batch. Absent by default, which is why temperature 0 is not deterministic.
best-of-N reporting
Running an eval several times and publishing the highest. Worth roughly the run-to-run spread — a couple of free points — and invisible in any results table.
benchmark saturation
Scores compressing near the top. Partly a real ceiling, and partly the accumulated false-negative rate of an ageing suite, which rises as models improve.

Sources

Twelve things, ordered by how much they would improve this answer if you read exactly one. The first is short, recent, and does more work in this conversation than anything else on the list.

  1. Why SWE-bench Verified no longer measures frontier coding capabilitiesOpenAI, February 2026. The organisation that built the carefully-audited version of the benchmark explaining why it stopped using it: 138 hard tasks audited, 59.4% with flawed tests, 35.5% specifically over-narrow, plus evidence of memorised gold patches and six months of score compression. Read this one first; it is the shortest and it contains the number you will actually quote. openai.com
  2. Introducing SWE-bench VerifiedOpenAI, August 2024. The other half of the pair, and it is the half that makes the story interesting rather than cynical: 93 professional developers, 1,699 instances annotated for underspecified issues and unfair tests, 500 kept. Read the two together and the lesson is not “benchmarks are bad” — it is that an audit has a date on it. openai.com
  3. UTBoost: Rigorous Evaluation of Coding Agents on SWE-BenchYang et al., ACL 2025. Generates additional tests and re-runs the leaderboard’s own submissions: 345 patches recorded as passing were wrong, affecting 24.4% of Verified entries and 40.9% of Lite, with 11 and 18 ranking changes. The clearest demonstration that false positives are not hypothetical. aclanthology 2025.acl-long.189
  4. SWE-Bench+: Enhanced Coding Benchmark for LLMsAleithan, Xue, Mohajer, Nnorom, Uddin & Wang, 2024. The source of the 31.08% weak-test figure and of the 12.47% to 3.97% collapse once problematic instances are removed. Also quantifies solution leakage, which is a different defect with its own guide in this series. arXiv:2410.06992
  5. Establishing Best Practices for Building Rigorous Agentic BenchmarksZhu et al., 2025. The generalisation: task-setup and reward-design flaws across many agentic benchmarks, with over- and under-estimation reaching 100% in relative terms, and a checklist you can actually apply to your own suite. The most directly useful thing here if your job is to build the eval. arXiv:2507.02825
  6. Dockerfile Flakiness: Characterization and RepairGhaleb et al., 2024. 8,132 projects, untouched Dockerfiles, rebuilt over nine months; 9.81% behaved differently, with a full taxonomy of why. This is the paper that converts “images rot” from a complaint into a rate with a breakdown, and the breakdown tells you where to look. arXiv:2408.05379
  7. Adding Error Bars to Evals: A Statistical Approach to Language Model EvaluationsEvan Miller, Anthropic, 2024. Short, practical, and the right reference for the fifth bucket: clustered standard errors because benchmark questions arrive in correlated groups, variance reduction by pairing, and power analysis so you know before you run whether your suite can resolve the difference you care about. arXiv:2411.00640
  8. Defeating Nondeterminism in LLM InferenceHorace He and colleagues, Thinking Machines, 2025. Why temperature 0 is not deterministic: 1,000 completions of one prompt producing 80 distinct outputs, diverging at token 103, traced to kernels whose accumulation order depends on batch composition — and fixed to bitwise reproducibility at a measured throughput cost. thinkingmachines.ai
  9. Evaluating Large Language Models Trained on CodeChen et al., 2021. The Codex paper, and the origin of both HumanEval and pass@k. Worth reading section 2 specifically for the argument against match-based metrics and for the unbiased pass@k estimator — the moment the field moved to execution, stated by the people who moved it. arXiv:2107.03374
  10. Out of the BLEU: How should we assess quality of the Code Generation models?Evtikhiev, Bogomolov, Sokolov & Bryksin, JSS 2023. The careful measurement of how badly similarity metrics track human judgment on code, including the result that none of them reliably reproduce a human ranking when models are within five BLEU points. Useful whenever somebody proposes a cheap proxy. arXiv:2208.03133
  11. SWE-bench: Can Language Models Resolve Real-World GitHub Issues?Jimenez, Yang, Wettig, Yao, Pei, Press & Narasimhan, ICLR 2024.
