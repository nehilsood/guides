Question 10 · Microsoft · senior AI engineer

The Half-Typed Line

A teammate retrained the code tokenizer to merge freely across whitespace. Sequences got about 40% shorter and held-out loss improved. Both numbers are real and neither is wrong. They were measured on complete files — and nobody in your product is ever holding a complete file. The metric got better on the one distribution your users never occupy.

The question, as asked

“A teammate retrained our code tokenizer to merge freely across whitespace. Sequences got ~40% shorter and held-out loss improved. Why might IDE autocomplete get worse after we ship it, and what do you check before approving?”

00

What they are actually testing

This is a review question wearing a modelling question’s clothes. Nobody is asking you to design a tokenizer. They are asking whether you can be handed two improved numbers and still find the thing that got worse — and whether, when you find it, you reach for a measurement instead of an opinion. Four things sit underneath.

P1

Do you know what a tokenizer is, layer by layer?

“The tokenizer” is four pieces stacked, and only one of them decides which boundaries are even eligible. “Merge freely across whitespace” is not a change to the merge table. It is a deletion from the pre-tokenizer’s split rule. Until you can name the layer that moved, you cannot name a fix that is narrower than a revert.

P2

Can you tell “the metric improved” from “the metric is comparable”?

Per-token loss has the token count in its denominator. Change the tokenizer and you change the denominator, so the two numbers are not two measurements of one quantity — they are measurements of two different quantities. Bits-per-byte is the first repair. It is not the last one, and the candidates who stop there are the ones who get the follow-up.

P3

Do you see that this is a sampling gap, not a domain gap?

Training and serving here see the same language, the same repositories, the same style. What differs is only where the text was cut. Held-out loss cuts at offset zero, every time. Autocomplete cuts wherever a human stopped typing. A candidate who says “train/serve skew” and moves on has named the family without naming the member.

P4

Will you price the mitigation, or just name it?

Saying “token healing fixes it” fails this question in exactly the same shape as “ship it” — you have accepted a claim without asking for its residual. Healing backs off one token, and nobody knows in general that one is enough. An answer that names healing without naming what healing leaves behind is not a safer answer, only a longer one.

Not the same question as guide 0005

0005 also has a tokenizer in it, so keep the seam sharp in case they probe it. 0005 is about the string the tokenizer is handed: does the harness build the exact prompt this model was trained to continue — the right chat template, the right tool-call markup, the right special tokens. Its failure is a serialisation bug, and it kills the call outright.

0011 assumes the string is perfect and asks the next question: given the right text, are the cuts in the right places. Its failure is a segmentation bug, and it kills nothing — it degrades quietly, in one slice of traffic, while every dashboard stays green. You can get the template exactly right and the segmentation exactly wrong, and the second one is much harder to see.

01

The answer, at three lengths

Lead with the cut, not the compression. The sentence that does the work is “those two numbers were measured on text cut at offset zero” — everything else in this guide is that sentence with evidence attached.

One sentence

Compression is measured on finished files and autocomplete lives at a cursor, so the change improved the model on the one cut point — the start of the file — that no user is ever standing at.

Thirty seconds

A tokenizer does two jobs. It compresses a document into few tokens, and it segments it, deciding which character offsets are legal places to divide the text. Merging trades the second for the first, and the trade is one-directional: every merge you add deletes a boundary, and a deleted boundary is a cursor position the model can no longer be prompted at. Held-out loss only ever reads whole files, so it cannot see the thing that got worse. Three concrete failures follow — the cursor lands mid-token and the model is asked to continue from a state the merge table made unreachable in training; the newline fuses into its neighbours so there is no longer a token that means “stop at the end of the line”; and in Python the indent fuses into the statement, so scope and content get committed in the same decision.

Two minutes

Everything above, then the honest part: this was already broken before your teammate touched it. Under the old tokenizer most cursor positions already fell inside a token — for Python, published measurements put the mis-segmentation rate around two thirds. The merge did not create the failure, it spent the margin you were living inside. That matters because it changes the decision from “approve or revert” to “how much of a margin do we have left, and what is buying it back worth.”

So what I’d check, in order. Bits-per-byte, not per-token loss — per-token loss changed its own denominator, so the improvement is partly an artefact of measuring in bigger units. Cursor-conditioned evaluation — cut held-out files at character offsets sampled the way our users actually cut them, mid-identifier, mid-line, just after an indent, and score both tokenizers on exact match and edit similarity. That is the number the decision turns on, and full-file loss is the special case of it with the cut pinned to zero. Clean-stop rate — what fraction of single-line completions end at the line boundary rather than overshooting. Round-trip fidelity — that decode(encode(x)) == x survives tabs, mixed indentation, CRLF and trailing whitespace, on raw text rather than normalised text. And the mitigation, priced — token healing recovers a good part of this, but it is a partial fix with an unknown backoff depth and it runs on every keystroke, so it comes with a residual and a p99, not just a name.

And I’d say what would make me approve: this ships if cursor-conditioned bits-per-byte improves and clean-stop rate does not regress. It does not ship if sequence length is the only number that moved.

Don’t say Shorter sequences plus lower loss means a better model. Ship it.

It treats two numbers as one verdict. Shorter sequences and lower per-token loss are not independent confirmations — the second one is partly caused by the first, because shortening the sequence shrank the denominator the loss is averaged over.

The deeper miss is that both numbers were computed by an evaluator that reads files from the beginning. The product never does. You have been shown a strong result about a question nobody asked.

Say instead Both numbers are real. They were measured on text cut at offset zero, and our users cut it somewhere else.

This concedes the win immediately, which is what makes the rest credible — you are not arguing that the teammate’s work is bad, you are arguing that it has not yet been measured against the product.

Then you name the mechanism in one line — a merge is a deleted boundary, and a deleted boundary is a cursor position — and go straight to what you would measure. Mechanism, then measurement. Never mechanism, then opinion.

02

A tokenizer is four things, and only one of them moved

Almost every confused answer to this question comes from treating “the tokenizer” as a single object that was retrained. It is a stack of four, they do different jobs, and the change under review is in exactly one of them — the only one whose job is to decide where the text is allowed to be divided. Name that layer and the whole question becomes tractable.

1 · normaliser

Decides what counts as the same text. Unicode normalisation, sometimes case folding, sometimes whitespace tidying. Runs before anything else sees the string.

the layer that quietly eats your trailing whitespace and your CRLF
2 · pre-tokenizerchanged

Splits the raw text into chunks that merges are not allowed to cross. Usually one regex. Traditionally it splits at whitespace, which is why tokens have historically been words or pieces of words.

decides which boundaries are ELIGIBLE. “merge freely across whitespace” is a deletion from this regex
3 · merge table

The learned part. Byte-pair encoding repeatedly fuses the most frequent adjacent pair in the corpus, strictly inside a pre-token. This is what people mean when they say the tokenizer was “retrained”.

decides how many of the eligible boundaries actually get spent
4 · vocab + decoder

The id↔bytes map, plus byte fallback for anything unseen. Decides whether decode(encode(x)) == x closes, and on what class of input it stops closing.

the layer your round-trip test is actually testing

Four layers, one direction of travel. The load-bearing fact is the one in row 3: BPE cannot invent a boundary the pre-tokenizer refused, and cannot preserve one the pre-tokenizer deleted. The merge table is downstream of the split rule. So a change described as “we retrained the tokenizer” may be a layer-3 change, which is a tuning decision — or a layer-2 change, which is a change to the model’s input alphabet. This one is layer 2.

The evidence that this is layer 2 and not layer 3

There is a clean empirical demonstration of it. A 2026 study measured, across production tokenizers, how often a token’s right edge fails to land on a punctuation boundary in real source files. Three of the tokenizers — Qwen3, Llama 3 and OLMo 2 — report the identical figure on Python: 68.08%. Three different vocabularies, three different training corpora, three different merge tables, one number to four significant figures.

They share a pre-tokenizer regex. Gemma, which splits runs of punctuation instead of letting them fuse, comes in at 25.11% on the same corpus — less than half of everyone else, with no change to how BPE was trained. The mis-segmentation rate is a property of layer 2, and it is therefore something you can design rather than something you inherit.

The sentence to have ready

“Your teammate did not retrain the merge table, they widened the pre-tokenizer. That is a change to which boundaries exist at all, and it is the only layer whose output our product depends on directly — because the product cuts the text, and the cut has to land somewhere the tokenizer admits.”

03

What the 40% was actually spent on

Here is the mechanism, and it is arithmetic rather than intuition. A tokenizer does two jobs at once and they are in direct opposition. It compresses — fewer, longer tokens for the same text. And it segments — it decides which character offsets are legal places to divide the stream. Every merge you add makes the first number better by making the second number worse, because a merge is literally the deletion of a boundary. There is no version of this where you get compression for free.

boundary coverage = legal cut points / character offsets = (tokens - 1) / (characters - 1) ≈ 1 / L L = mean bytes per token so the two numbers your teammate reported are the same number: "40% fewer tokens" and "40% fewer places a cursor can stand" are one measurement, written twice, with opposite signs.

Take one real line and look at it. This is return self.cache.get(key, None) — thirty-six characters including a four-space indent — segmented first by a pre-tokenizer that splits at whitespace, then by one that does not. The chips are tokens. The violet ticks between them are boundaries. The strip underneath has one cell per character offset and marks the ones a cursor can legally occupy.

The middot stands for a space, the way an editor renders whitespace when you turn it on. Nothing in the lower row is wrong. It is a better compressor by every measure a compressor is judged on. It has simply stopped being able to represent five of the eleven positions the upper row could represent, and one of the five it kept is the end of the line.

what was reported −41.7%

tokens on this line: twelve down to seven. The headline number, and it is true.

what it was spent from 31.4 → 17.1%

boundary coverage: the share of interior offsets a prompt is allowed to end on. Eleven down to six.

what the user meets 68.6 → 82.9%

share of cursor positions that now land inside a token. Five in six, up from two in three.

Now put a cursor in it

The user has typed return self.ca and paused. The caret is at column 18, which is not a boundary under either tokenizer — and that is the first thing worth saying out loud, because it means the failure is a matter of degree rather than of kind.

Under the old segmentation the prompt encodes as ···· return ·self . ca. That last fragment is a mis-segmentation, but a mild one: ca is a short, common piece, the model has seen plenty of prompts ending in a short identifier fragment, and the natural continuation che is one it can produce. You are off-distribution, but not far.

Under the new segmentation the prompt encodes as ····return ·self .ca. The merge table’s whole purpose is that .cache is one token — which means during training, in every one of the millions of places that string appeared, it was emitted as .cache and never as .ca followed by something. The state you have just constructed is not rare in the training data. It is absent from it. You are asking the model to continue from a position the tokenizer was specifically trained to make unreachable.

The analogy, if you want one

A phrasebook that lists “Where is the train station?” as a single numbered entry is excellent — it is the shortest possible encoding of a sentence you say constantly. It is worth nothing to a person who has already said “Where is the tr…” and needs the rest. The book got smaller by deleting exactly the thing that lets you join halfway.

Then the sentence that makes it an engineering point rather than a metaphor: the phrasebook is scored on how few pages it has, and the score never involved anybody interrupting.

04

How much it costs, measured by somebody else

You do not have to argue this from first principles in the room. It has a name — the partial token problem — and as of February 2026 it has a study behind it, from Washington, Nvidia and AI2, which is recent enough that quoting it is itself a signal.

# the partial token problem, stated exactly encode(prompt) + encode(continuation) ≠ encode(prompt + continuation) # the two sides are the same text. they are not the same token # sequence, because the boundary at the join was forced by where # the user stopped rather than chosen by the merge table. # # the model was trained on the right-hand side. # at serving time you hand it the left.

What that costs, on their numbers. On code, across six model families and five to six thousand instances per setting, accuracy on a partial-token continuation task falls by 87.6% to 95.61% against the aligned baseline. Across all three domains they test, the drop ranges from 60% to 95.6%, with log-probability reductions of two to four orders of magnitude. Code is the worst of the three, and for a structural reason they name: punctuation occurs in contiguous runs in source, and most pre-tokenizers let punctuation merge freely, so code accumulates exactly the long multi-character tokens that this failure needs.

The finding that changes what kind of problem this is

Bigger models do not fix it. In their scaling figure, larger variants are not more robust to partial tokens, and several of them are worse. That is the sentence to carry into the room, because it settles the question of whether this belongs in a review gate or on a backlog.

A capability gap closes on its own — you wait two quarters and the next checkpoint has eaten it. This is not a capability gap. It is a mismatch between two encodings of the same text, and a larger model trained through the same tokenizer inherits the mismatch exactly. Scale is not a mitigation here. Nothing you were already planning to do will make this go away.

The honest counterweight, which you should volunteer rather than wait for: this was already broken before your teammate touched anything. The 68.08% figure from the previous section is the current tokenizer, the one in production, the one your acceptance rate was measured on. Two Python cursor positions in three already failed to land on a boundary. The merge did not introduce the failure. It spent the margin you were living inside without knowing you had one.

That reframes the decision, and reframing it is most of what seniority looks like here. The question is not “does this break autocomplete” — autocomplete has always run slightly off-distribution and shipped anyway. The question is how much margin is left, what the remaining margin is worth, and what buying some of it back would cost. Which is a question you answer with two measurements and a price, not with a veto.

05

Autocomplete has two boundaries, not one

Nearly every discussion of this problem — including the version of it you were handed — describes a single cut: the prompt ends mid-token. That is right for a chat completion. It is incomplete for an IDE, because IDE completion has not been plain left-to-right continuation since 2022. It is fill-in-the-middle, and fill-in-the-middle cuts the file twice at the same offset.

# what the model is actually handed at Ln 12, Col 18 <|fim_prefix|>def get(self, key): return self.ca<|fim_suffix|>che.get(key, None)<|fim_middle|> ^ A ^ B A the prefix's right edge. last token is a fragment. this is the boundary everybody discusses, and the one token healing was designed for. B the suffix's LEFT edge. the suffix is encoded starting from an arbitrary character offset, so its FIRST token is a fragment too - and healing does not reach it. healing constrains the first token the model EMITS. the suffix is not emitted. it is read.

The two boundaries fail differently, and B is the one worth raising, because raising it is the difference between having read about this problem and having thought about it.

  • B is quieter

    A can produce something visibly wrong — a doubled prefix, a stray space, http: // instead of http://. You can see it in a sampled output.

    B produces a completion that is perfectly well formed and merely conditioned on slightly corrupted evidence. There is no artefact to find. The only symptom is that the suggestions are a little worse, which is indistinguishable from the model being a little worse.

  • B sits nearest the hole

    In the suffix-prefix-middle ordering that several code models prefer, the suffix is placed immediately before the generation point. Its first tokens are the closest context the model has to the thing it is being asked to write.

    So the one token that is guaranteed to be mis-segmented is also the one with the most influence over the prediction.

  • B scales worse

    The prefix ends wherever the user stopped, which is often at a natural pause — after a dot, after an open bracket, at the end of a word. Those are positions a word-bounded pre-tokenizer tends to admit.

    The suffix begins with the remainder of the line the caret is in, which is almost never a natural pause. Cross-whitespace merges reach into exactly that region, so widening the pre-tokenizer damages B strictly harder than it damages A.

  • B has no good fix

    You have two moves and both cost something. Snap the suffix forward to the next legal boundary — which silently deletes the few characters in between, and those characters are the ones the user just typed around. Or accept the fragment and take the degradation.

    Every published mitigation targets A. B is not a decoding problem, so no decoder change touches it — the only lever you have on B is the pre-tokenizer, which is to say, the change under review.

Worth saying plainly if you raise this: the second boundary is a consequence of how FIM prompts are built, and how much it costs in a given stack depends on that stack’s ordering and on whether it already snaps the suffix. It is a thing to go and measure on Monday, not a thing to assert a number for in an interview. Saying it that way is stronger than a number would be.

06

The line ending and the indent

Two more failures, and they are not variations on the first one. The cursor problem is about the start of the generation. These two are about its end and its meaning, and in a Python product the second one is the expensive one.

There is no longer a token that means “stop”

Single-line completion is defined by where it stops: you emit until the line ends. That works because \n is an atom — a token the model can choose, whose selection means “this line is finished” and nothing else. Let the pre-tokenizer fuse a newline with the indentation after it and the first word of the next statement, and that atom is gone. What the model now has is a token meaning “end this line, indent four spaces, and begin a return statement.” Ending the line is no longer something it can decide on its own.

You get one of two failures and they look unrelated in a bug tracker. Either the model emits the fused token and your single-line completion arrives carrying the first fragment of the next statement — overshoot. Or your serving layer cuts the string at the newline, which lands in the middle of a token you have already sampled and paid for — truncation, with a ragged edge.

The mismatch worth naming

Stop sequences are strings. Sampling happens in tokens. Every serving stack quietly bridges the two, and the bridge is only invisible while stop strings tend to coincide with token boundaries. Widening the pre-tokenizer widens that gap directly: the more text each token carries, the more often your stop string falls inside one, and the more of each response you are generating only to throw away.

This is also why the fix has a natural shape. You do not have to give up cross-whitespace merges. You have to give up one class of them: nothing may swallow a newline. That preserves the atom the product depends on and keeps most of the compression, and it is a four-line change to a regex.

In Python, whitespace is not whitespace

In most languages indentation is decoration and the compiler ignores it. In Python it is the block structure. So when the indent fuses with the statement that follows it, choosing what to write and choosing where it goes collapse into a single sampling decision with a single probability attached.

The model can no longer be confident about the content and uncertain about the scope, which is exactly the epistemic state it is usually in — “this is a return, though I am not sure whether it belongs in the if or after it.” That state has become unrepresentable. Turn the temperature up and you are now sampling over block structure.

The same change has a different blast radius per language

This is worth raising unprompted, because it tells the interviewer you are thinking about the product and not the paper. In a brace language, indent fusion causing a scope error produces a syntax error — the braces do not match, the compiler complains, the user sees a red squiggle and rejects the suggestion. Loud, cheap, self-correcting.

In Python, YAML, Haskell or F#, the same mistake produces valid code with different behaviour. Nothing complains. The suggestion gets accepted, and the defect is now in a commit. So the identical tokenizer change has one severity in your Go traffic and another in your Python traffic — and whether you catch it before shipping depends entirely on the language mix of the eval corpus, which nobody chose with this in mind.

07

Why the metric said yes

“Held-out loss improved” is three claims in a trench coat, and the first thing to do is separate them. There is a repair to make to the units, and then a second, larger repair to make to the sampling — and the second one is where the answer lives, because bits-per-byte alone is a well-known move and the interviewer is waiting to see whether you stop there.

Ask how the loss was computed, before you agree or object

There is arithmetic here worth doing out loud. Coarser tokens carry more information each, so per-token cross-entropy normally goes up when you compress harder — the same total surprise divided among fewer terms. On the line in section 03, twelve tokens became seven; for the model to be exactly as good as before, per-token loss would have to rise by about 71%.

So a per-token loss that fell alongside a 40% shorter sequence is an extraordinary claim, and it has three possible explanations. The model really did get much better, which would be excellent news and is worth confirming. Or the two numbers came from different vocabularies and were compared anyway, which makes them two answers to two different questions. Or — and check this first, because it is the most common — the “loss” was a sum over the sequence rather than a mean, in which case it fell by 41.7% for free and measures nothing at all.

# three metrics. each one repairs the one above it. per-token loss L = -(1/T) Σ log p(t_i) T is the token count, and T is what the change moved. the comparison changed its own denominator. bits per byte BPB = (T · L) / (ln 2 · B) B = utf-8 bytes of the SAME text, fixed by the document and not by the vocabulary. this repairs the units, and it is where most good answers stop. cursor BPB BPB_c = E_c [ BPB( x[c:] | x[:c] ) ] c = the character offset the text is cut at, drawn from the distribution of caret positions in your OWN telemetry. this repairs the sampling. and the line that makes it land: full-file BPB is BPB_c with c pinned to 0. it is one point of this distribution, and it is the only point no user has ever occupied.

Two practical notes on c, because an interviewer may push. Sampling it uniformly over character offsets is a fine first approximation and it is enormously better than zero — it is an afternoon’s work against a held-out corpus you already have. But the real distribution is not uniform: people pause after a dot, after an opening bracket, at the end of a word, and just after pressing return. Drawing c from your own telemetry instead costs a day and turns the eval into a simulation of the product rather than an approximation of it. And the second note: report it bucketed, not just averaged. The interesting result is almost never the mean — it is that the on-boundary bucket did not move and the mid-identifier bucket fell off a cliff.

The through-line, if you have read the other guides

This is the same defect as guide 0009’s acceptance rate and guide 0008’s self-written skills, wearing a third costume. In each case a metric was computed correctly and reported honestly, and the thing that was wrong was the distribution it sampled from — a denominator the agent controlled, a test set derived from the thing under test, an evaluator that cuts every document at offset zero.

Which suggests the habit rather than the fact: when someone shows you a number, ask what it was sampled over before you ask whether it went up.

08

Token healing is a mitigation, not a fix

This is the part of the question most candidates get half right. Naming token healing is good — it shows you know the literature. Treating it as the answer is a repeat of the mistake the question opened with: accepting a claim without asking what it leaves behind.

What it does

Back the generation up by one token before the end of the prompt, then constrain the first token generated so that it starts with the characters you removed. The model gets to re-choose the boundary instead of being stuck with the one the user’s keystrokes imposed.

It is needed because of a property of vocabularies that is easy to underrate: in a typical model, about 70% of the ten thousand most common tokens are prefixes of longer tokens. Which means the last token of almost any prompt is a token that, in training, would usually have grown into something bigger. The canonical demonstration is a URL — a prompt ending in http: produces http: //www.google.com, with a space, while the same prompt ending in http produces http:// correctly. The model is not confused about URLs. It is correctly continuing from a token that, in its experience, only ever appeared in weird places.

What it leaves

Healing backs off one token. Nobody knows in general that one is enough — the partial-token study says so directly, that it is not known in advance how far to back off — and after a cross-whitespace merge, one token can be ten characters of a fused run, so one step may not reach a genuine boundary at all. Their measurements bear it out: healing takes code accuracy to 94.09% but German to only 83.16%, and on German it comes out 6.29 points below the properly-aligned baseline. Same technique, same model, wildly different residual by domain.

The exact alternative in the same table is byte-level resampling: build the tree of all valid token sequences that cover the prompt’s bytes, sample a path through it, and continue from there rather than assuming a boundary at the end. It reaches 100.00% on all three domains, at a cost of 0.12 to 1.17 additional forward passes. That is a real, quotable price, and quoting it is what turns “we could add healing” into an engineering decision.

  • The residual is silent

    This is the property that makes “we turned on healing” an insufficient answer. When healing works you get a good completion. When it fails you get a completion that looks exactly as good and is conditioned on a broken boundary.

    So the mitigation and its own failure are indistinguishable from the outside. Until somebody has measured the residual, turning healing on has not reduced your risk — it has reduced your ability to see it.

  • It costs on every keystroke

    Autocomplete latency is a p99 budget in the tens of milliseconds, because the interaction dies if the suggestion arrives after the user has typed the next character. Healing adds constrained-decoding work to every request, and byte-level resampling adds up to roughly one extra forward pass.

    Both are affordable. Neither is free. Price them in the same document as the compression win, or you are comparing a benefit to nothing.

  • It does not touch the other two failures

    Healing is about the boundary the generation starts at. The missing stop atom and the fused indentation are about what the model can emit and what a single emission commits to. No decoding-time repair reaches either.

    And it does not reach the suffix boundary from section 05 at all, because the suffix is input. Healing covers one of the four problems on this page.

The strongest argument on your teammate’s side, stated fairly

Cross-whitespace merging is not a bad idea, and you should say so before anyone accuses you of being the person who blocks things. The published work on superword tokenization takes exactly this approach — standard byte-pair encoding first, then a second phase with the pre-tokenizer boundary lifted so merges may bridge whitespace — and reports up to 33% fewer tokens, +4.0% absolute across 30 downstream tasks including +8.2% on MMLU, and 27% less inference compute. Your teammate is reproducing a real result, competently.

The disagreement is not about the model. It is about which distribution the benefit was demonstrated on. Those gains are measured on complete prompts, scored on complete answers. Nothing in that evaluation ever ends a prompt inside a token, because nothing in that evaluation has a caret in it. So the right sentence is not “this is wrong” — it is “this is a strong result about a workload we do not have, and I want the same result on the workload we do.”

09

What you check before approving

The second half of the question is the half they are actually scoring, and it wants a list with an order and a veto in it. Four of these are permits — evidence you want on the page. One of them is a gate: without it the review has not started, and saying which one is a gate is the answer.

  1. 1 · unitspermit Bits-per-byte, and say how the old number was computed

    Per-token loss is not comparable across vocabularies, so normalise by UTF-8 bytes of the same text. But do not stop at “use BPB” — go back and ask how the original figure was produced, because a sum-over-sequence loss falls automatically when the sequence gets shorter and a mean-per-token loss should have risen by around 70% for a model of unchanged quality.

    what it rules out a comparison whose denominator moved. Nothing more. Passing this tells you the numbers are numbers; it tells you nothing about the product.
  2. 2 · samplinggate Cursor-position evaluation, bucketed

    Cut held-out files at character offsets drawn the way your users cut them — mid-identifier, mid-line, immediately after an indent, after a dot, after an opening bracket — and score both tokenizers on exact match and edit similarity against the real continuation. Report it split by bucket, never only as a mean.

    This is the gate because it is the only measurement on the list that samples the product’s own distribution. Everything else on this page is a proxy for it. If it is not in the document, the reviewer has been shown a model result and asked to make a product decision.

    what it rules out approving a change that improves the model everywhere except where it is used. It is also the only check that can approve: if the mid-identifier bucket holds, the rest of this is caution rather than objection.
  3. 3 · behaviourpermit Clean-stop rate

    What percentage of single-line completions end at the line boundary and nowhere else — not one token early, not carrying the first fragment of the next statement. This is the direct measurement of whether the newline survived as an atom, and it is cheap: you already have the completions.

    what it rules out the overshoot-and-truncate failure, which is the one users describe as “it keeps suggesting too much” and which nobody ever traces back to a tokenizer.
  4. 4 · correctnesspermit Round trip, on raw text rather than normalised text

    That decode(encode(x)) == x holds for tabs, mixed indentation inside one file, trailing whitespace, CRLF, a missing final newline, a byte-order mark, and non-ASCII identifiers. There is a trap in how this is usually tested: the standard lossless-tokenization guarantee is Decode(Encode(Normalize(x))) = Normalize(x) — it holds over normalised text. Feed your test corpus through the same preprocessing the tokenizer uses and you have written a tautology that passes on every input.

    what it rules out silent mutation of the user’s file. In an editor this is not a quality regression, it is data loss, and it is the one item here that can justify a revert on its own.
  5. 5 · costpermit The mitigation, with a residual and a p99

    If the plan is token healing, then healing needs its own row: how far it backs off and why one token is enough for this vocabulary, what fraction of the cursor-eval gap it closes, and what it adds to p99 latency per keystroke. If the plan is byte-level resampling, the published cost is up to roughly one extra forward pass and the accuracy is exact — which may well be the better trade, and you will not know unless both are on the page.

    what it rules out a mitigation that is named rather than measured. “We’ll turn on healing” is a plan; it is not evidence.

10

Twelve follow-ups, with answers

The first four are the ones you will actually get. F6 and F8 are the practical ones — if you can only rehearse two, rehearse those, because they are the ones where a candidate stops sounding well-read and starts sounding like someone who has shipped a model.

F1“Fine — then just turn on token healing and ship it. What’s your objection?”

None, in principle. My objection is to shipping it unmeasured, and the reason is specific rather than procedural: healing backs off exactly one token, and after a cross-whitespace merge one token can be ten characters of a fused run, so one step may not reach a real boundary. The published numbers show the residual is uneven — 94% on code, 83% on German, and on German it lands below the aligned baseline.

And the property that makes this more than pedantry: healing’s failures look exactly like its successes. It always returns a fluent completion. So turning it on without measuring the residual does not reduce the risk, it removes the symptom you would have used to detect the risk. One number fixes this — the cursor-eval gap remaining after healing — and it takes an afternoon.

F2“We’re training a much bigger model next quarter. Won’t that absorb this?”

No, and this is the most useful single fact in the area. The partial-token study scales it explicitly and finds larger variants are not more robust — several are worse.

The reason is structural. This is not a gap in what the model knows; it is a mismatch between two encodings of identical text. A larger model trained through the same tokenizer sees the same merged form in training and meets the same fragmented form at serving time, and it has learned the merged form harder. Scale sharpens the prior that makes the fragment unfamiliar. Which is exactly why this needs a gate now rather than a backlog item later: nothing you are already planning to do will touch it.

F3“We use fill-in-the-middle, so the model sees the code after the cursor. Doesn’t the suffix rescue the prefix?”

It helps with the semantics and makes the second boundary worse. FIM cuts the file twice at the same offset: the prefix ends mid-token, and the suffix begins mid-token. Token healing reaches the first and cannot reach the second, because healing is a constraint on the first token the model emits and the suffix is never emitted — it is read.

The suffix boundary is also the quieter one. A broken prefix boundary can produce visibly malformed output; a broken suffix boundary produces a well-formed completion conditioned on mildly corrupted evidence, which is indistinguishable from the model simply being a bit worse. And in suffix-prefix-middle ordering the suffix sits adjacent to the generation point, so the guaranteed-mis-segmented token is the one with the most influence on the prediction. I’d want that measured on our stack before I formed a view on the size of it.

F4“Why is code worse than prose here?”

Three reasons that compound. Punctuation occurs in contiguous runs in source — ).get(, }});, => { — and most pre-tokenizers let punctuation merge without limit, so code accumulates precisely the long multi-character tokens this failure needs. Published misalignment rates for Python sit near 68% for the common pre-tokenizer family.

Second, identifiers are long, compound and open-class, so the caret spends most of its life inside one. Third, the cut distribution is adversarial in a way prose’s is not: a person writing prose pauses at word ends, while a person writing code pauses inside self.cach and waits for the editor to finish it. Autocomplete is a product built specifically around the offsets that are worst for the tokenizer.

F5“Could we fix this in training instead — randomly split tokens so the model sees fragments?”

Yes, and it is the only option on the table that repairs the training distribution rather than patching at decode time. It exists in production: at least one frontier model randomly splits a proportion of multi-punctuation tokens during training. Note how narrow that is — multi-punctuation specifically, not everything — which is the design telling you the trade is real.

Because the cost is direct: stochastic tokenization spends the encoding efficiency you just bought, and the study that reports it declines to recommend it broadly for exactly that reason. The practical constraint is scheduling. It requires a pretraining run, so it is not available for this decision. It is the right thing to put in the next tokenizer’s design document, alongside the note that we will want cursor-conditioned evaluation before that run rather than after it.

F6“You have one afternoon and a held-out corpus. What do you actually run?”

Two things, and the first needs no GPU at all. Boundary coverage: take a few thousand held-out files, encode each with both tokenizers, and count what fraction of character offsets are token boundaries. That is twenty lines, it runs in seconds, and it gives you the size of the margin being spent before you have asked anyone for inference budget. I would run it first because it cannot fail to produce a result and it decides whether the rest is worth doing.

Then cursor evaluation. Same corpus, ten cut offsets per file sampled to match our caret telemetry, generate to end-of-line from each under both tokenizers, and score exact match and edit similarity against the real continuation. Bucket by context — on boundary, mid-identifier, just after an indent, after a dot. I would expect the on-boundary bucket to be flat or better and the mid-identifier bucket to carry the whole regression, and if that is the shape, the fix is narrow rather than a revert.

F7“Suppose bits-per-byte also improves. Do you approve then?”

Not on that alone, and this is the follow-up that separates the two halves of the argument. Bits-per-byte repairs the units — it stops us comparing a per-token average across two different token counts. It does not repair the sampling. It is still computed on complete files read from the beginning, so an improvement in it is an improvement at cut point zero.

The cleanest way to say it: full-file BPB is cursor-conditioned BPB with the cut pinned to zero. It is one point of the distribution we care about, and the only point no user has ever occupied. So yes, I want BPB, and it is necessary; it is a repair to how we compare, not a measurement of what we ship.

F8“It went out a month ago. What’s the fastest way to find out whether it hurt?”

Re-cut the metric you already have, retroactively, with no new logging. Acceptance rate is already recorded per suggestion, and the prompt prefix is in the request logs — which means the caret offset is recoverable, and so is whether it landed on a token boundary, under either tokenizer, computed after the fact. Segment historical acceptance by that and compare the before and after windows within each bucket.

The shape to look for is the giveaway: an aggregate that moved a couple of points while the on-boundary bucket held flat and the mid-identifier bucket fell hard. That is this bug’s signature, and it also explains why nobody caught it — the aggregate mixes a bucket that did not move with a bucket that collapsed, and the average lands somewhere unremarkable. Cost is a query. If the buckets move together instead, my hypothesis was wrong and something else regressed.

F9“Why does stop behaviour break? Can’t we just stop on the newline string?”

You can, and you should, and it is not sufficient. Stop sequences are strings; sampling is tokens. If the newline has fused into a token that also carries the next line’s indent and its first word, then stopping on the string means truncating inside a token you already sampled — so part of what you generated is discarded, and the remainder is a string the model never chose to emit as a unit.

The deeper cost is to the distribution. When a single token means “end this line and begin a return statement”, the probability of stopping is entangled with the probability of what comes next, and you can no longer read “should I stop here” off the model’s output at all. That is a vocabulary problem, and no serving-layer change reaches it. It is also why the narrow fix is the right one: forbid merges that swallow a newline, keep everything else.

F10“Set autocomplete aside. Isn’t more compression strictly better at fixed compute?”

No, and there is 2026 work on exactly this. A study training nearly a thousand models from 50M to 7B parameters over a wide range of compression rates finds that parameter count scales with data measured in bytes, not in tokens — which is already a correction to how most people reason about this — and that the compute-optimal compression rate is not the one byte-pair encoding lands on, and decreases as compute grows.

So the optimum is real, it is not at infinity, and it moves in the opposite direction from the intuition. Which makes “shorter is better” an unbounded claim about a quantity that has an interior maximum. Even without a single cursor anywhere in the argument, the right question is not how much shorter, it is whether we are on the correct side of the peak.

F11“Superword tokenization does exactly this and reports +8.2% on MMLU. Is your teammate wrong?”

Not about the model. That line of work — standard byte-pair encoding first, then a second phase with the whitespace constraint lifted — reports up to 33% fewer tokens, +4.0% absolute across 30 downstream tasks including the +8.2%, and 27% less inference compute. It is a good result and I would expect our teammate to reproduce a version of it.

Two things about it, though. Those benchmarks score complete prompts and complete answers; nothing in them ever ends a prompt inside a token, because nothing in them has a caret. And the method itself is a curriculum — it learns subwords first and only then lifts the constraint — which is the design conceding that boundary structure is worth building before it is worth spending. “Merge freely” is not what that paper did. The honest position is that this is a strong result on a workload we do not have, and I want the same result on the workload we do.

F12“If there’s one thing you’d want a junior engineer to take from this, what is it?”

That every metric has a sampling distribution hidden inside it, and the name of the metric almost never mentions it. “Held-out loss” sounds like it means how surprised is the model by text it has not seen. What it actually means is how surprised is the model by a file it has not seen, read from the beginning, all the way through. The clause that does the damage is the one nobody says out loud.

So the habit is not scepticism about numbers — scepticism is cheap and it makes you useless in a review. It is one question, asked early and without any edge to it: where did this metric cut the data, and is that where we cut it? Here the answer is that the metric always cuts at offset zero and the product never does, and once that sentence is on the table the entire disagreement resolves into an afternoon of work.

Glossary

Only the terms this question actually needs. The four that get misused most often are pre-tokenizer, held-out loss, token healing and bits-per-byte — and using those four precisely is most of what makes an answer here sound like it came from someone who has read the code.

bits per byte
Cross-entropy normalised by the UTF-8 byte length of the text rather than by the token count. The standard repair when comparing models with different vocabularies, because bytes are a property of the document and tokens are a property of the tokenizer.
boundary coverage
The share of character offsets in a text that are token boundaries — the fraction of cursor positions a prompt may legally end on. Approximately the reciprocal of mean bytes per token. Named and derived in this guide; it is not standard terminology, so introduce it as a definition rather than a citation.
byte fallback
Encoding any character the vocabulary lacks as its individual UTF-8 bytes, so that nothing is unrepresentable. It is what makes a tokenizer total; it is also where round-trip tests usually break, when the decoder does not convert those byte tokens back.
byte-pair encoding (BPE)
The learned layer. Repeatedly fuse the most frequent adjacent pair in the corpus until the vocabulary is full. Operates strictly inside a pre-token, which is why the pre-tokenizer is upstream of everything it can do.
clean-stop rate
The share of single-line completions that end exactly at the line boundary — not early, not carrying part of the next statement. The direct measurement of whether the newline survived as an atom.
compression rate
Bytes per token, or its inverse, tokens per byte (sometimes “fertility”). The number a tokenizer change is usually justified by, and the number this guide argues is only half of the accounting.
constrained decoding
Restricting which tokens may be sampled at a step so the output satisfies some property — a grammar, a schema, or in token healing’s case a required prefix. Costs work at every step it is active.
cursor-conditioned evaluation
Scoring a model on continuations from cut points sampled the way users cut text, rather than from the start of a document. The measurement this whole question exists to elicit.
edit similarity
A graded match between generated and reference text, usually from edit distance. Paired with exact match because completion quality is rarely binary — a suggestion one character off is not the same failure as a suggestion of the wrong statement.
fill-in-the-middle (FIM)
Training and prompting a left-to-right model to write a span given both what precedes and what follows it, using sentinel tokens to mark the three regions. How IDE completion has worked since 2022, and the reason the cursor creates two boundaries rather than one.
held-out loss
Cross-entropy on text the model was not trained on. Note the clause that is never said aloud: on complete documents, read from the beginning. That unstated clause is the whole question.
merge table
The ordered list of pair-fusions BPE learned. Applying it deterministically to a pre-token is what produces a canonical segmentation — and what makes the fragment at a cursor a state the model rarely saw.
normaliser
The first layer: Unicode normalisation and any tidying applied before splitting. Convenient for prose, lossy for code, and the reason a round-trip test has to run on raw text.
partial token problem
The formal name for the failure in this question: encode(prompt) + encode(continuation) does not equal encode(prompt + continuation), because the join was forced by where the user stopped rather than chosen by the merge table.
pre-token
One chunk produced by the pre-tokenizer — the unit inside which merges are permitted. Historically a word or a run of punctuation; under the change in this question, potentially several words and the spaces between them.
pre-tokenizer
The regex-shaped layer that splits raw text before BPE sees it. It decides which boundaries are eligible, it is the layer this question’s change edits, and it is the layer whose properties survive into serving.
prompt boundary problem
The older, informal name for the same failure, from the prompt-engineering literature. Useful because it names the location rather than the artefact, and the location is what you fix.
PSM / SPM
Prefix-suffix-middle and suffix-prefix-middle: the two orderings for laying out a FIM prompt. In SPM the suffix sits next to the generation point, which is why its first — mis-segmented — token has the most influence.
round-trip fidelity
Whether decode(encode(x)) == x. The usual guarantee is weaker than it sounds: it holds over normalised text, so it says nothing about tabs, CRLF or trailing whitespace unless you test the raw bytes.
sentinel token
A reserved vocabulary entry with a structural rather than linguistic meaning — the FIM markers, end-of-text, chat-role tags. They exist precisely because some boundaries are too important to leave to the merge table.
stochastic tokenization
Deliberately splitting some tokens at random during training so the model sees non-canonical segmentations. The only mitigation on this page that repairs the training distribution rather than patching at decode time, and it spends encoding efficiency to do it.
stop sequence
A string whose appearance ends generation. Sampling produces tokens. The gap between the two is invisible until stop strings stop coinciding with token boundaries.
superword
A token spanning more than one whitespace-separated word. What “merging freely across whitespace” produces, and a live research direction with real reported gains — on workloads that never cut a prompt mid-token.
token healing
Back the generation up one token, then constrain the first generated token to start with the characters removed. A mitigation for the prefix boundary only, with an unknown backoff depth and a residual that has to be measured because its failures look like its successes.
vocabulary
The id-to-bytes map. Its size gets all the attention in tokenizer discussions; its shape — which strings are atoms and therefore which boundaries survive — is what this question is about.
