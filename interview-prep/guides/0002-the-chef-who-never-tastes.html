The Chef Who Never Tastes

An interview question about AI systems, explained with a restaurant.

They asked

"Your AI checks its own work and gets better each time it tries. But your slowest customers now wait three times longer. Your teammate says just limit it to three tries. Why is that a plaster, and what's really wrong?"

The whole answer

Limiting the tries doesn't fix the slowness. It hides it — by quietly giving up on the hardest jobs, where nobody is looking.

The real problem is that nothing in the system ever tastes the food. It has no way to know when the work is good enough, so it keeps going until something forces it to stop.

The story

Read this once and you can explain the whole thing without a single technical word.

1

You run a kitchen

Orders come in. The chef cooks a dish, plates it, sends it out. Most customers wait about ten minutes. Everyone is happy.

2

You give the chef a new rule

"Before it goes out, look at the plate. If it isn't right, cook it again."

This is the self-correction loop. And on average, the food genuinely does get better.

3

Then the complaints start

The typical customer still waits about ten minutes. But the unluckiest customers — roughly one in a hundred — now wait half an hour.

That number, the unluckiest one-in-a-hundred, is what engineers call p99. It's the number that tripled. And it's the number customers actually complain about.

4

Your teammate's fix

"Tell the chef: three attempts, maximum."

The waiting time drops. The dashboard goes green. Everyone relaxes.

5

But look at what you just did

The dishes taking six attempts were the hard ones. Now they go out on attempt three — unfinished.

You didn't make the kitchen faster. You made it give up. And unlike the waiting time, "the food got worse" isn't on any screen in the building.

6

Here's the thing nobody noticed

The chef is judging the food by looking at it. Nobody ever tastes it. Nobody uses a thermometer.

That's why the chef can't stop: there's no way to know when a dish is actually done. So they keep going until the clock or the rule stops them — on every dish, including the easy ones that were perfect the first time.

That's the real bug. Not the number of attempts. The missing taste test.

7

And it's worse than one slow dish

There is one kitchen. When the chef re-cooks one dish four times, everyone else's food is sitting there waiting for a burner.

So the loop didn't just slow down the fussy orders. It slowed down everyone — including customers who ordered something simple that came out perfect first time.

Five ideas, three ways each

The plain version to understand it. The kitchen version to explain it. The real words to say in the room.

Idea 1 · The number that matters

An average hides the people who are suffering. You have to look at the worst cases separately.

In the kitchen

"Average wait: 10 minutes" sounds fine. It is still true on the night one table waited an hour and walked out. Averages cannot see that table.

Say it

"Average latency and p99 are different kinds of number. You can't fix one by arguing about the other."

Real words

p50 = the middle customer. p99 = the unluckiest 1 in 100. Tail latency. SLO = the promise you made about the worst case.

Idea 2 · Why only the worst cases got worse

No single attempt got slower. What changed is that nobody knows how many attempts a dish will take.

In the kitchen

Most dishes still take one go — that's why the typical wait barely moved. But a few now take eight goes, and those are the ones stacking up at the pass.

Say it

"The cost per pass didn't change — the number of passes became unpredictable. That uncertainty lands almost entirely in the tail. It's exactly why p50 held and p99 tripled."

Real words

Compound variance. The iteration count N is now a random variable, adding a Var(N)·E[X]² term that scales with the square of per-pass cost.

Idea 3 · The missing taste test

The system has no honest way to tell "this is finished" from "this needs more work", so it can never stop early.

In the kitchen

A thermometer costs two seconds and tells you the truth. Re-cooking costs ten minutes and is a guess. You've been buying the expensive guess because nobody bought the cheap truth.

Say it

"I want an external check the loop can stop on — run the tests, run the compiler, validate the schema, check the citation exists, score it with a small model. Stop on evidence, not on the model's opinion of itself."

Real words

Verifier. Reward model. Extrinsic vs intrinsic self-correction. Early-exit criterion. Stopping rule.

Idea 4 · One shared kitchen

Extra attempts aren't free and they aren't private. They eat the capacity everyone else is queuing for.

In the kitchen

And it feeds itself: a busy night makes things slow, slow makes more dishes get re-cooked, re-cooking makes it busier. The kitchen can stay jammed long after the rush has gone home.

Say it

"This loop is really an unbounded retry loop inside a shared engine. Under load it amplifies its own work. It needs a budget, a circuit breaker, and its own queue so retries can't crowd out first attempts."

Real words

Utilisation ρ. Queue wait scales like 1/(1−ρ). Retry storm. Work amplification. Metastable failure. Admission control.

Idea 5 · The wrong question

Nobody wrote down how long a customer is willing to wait. So the kitchen optimised for perfect food, forever.

In the kitchen

A perfect steak at minute forty is a worse steak than a good one at minute twelve, because by minute forty the table has left. Past a point, slow is bad.

Say it

"The goal was never maximum quality. It's the best quality we can deliver inside a p99 budget. If we'd written the SLO down first, the loop would have had a deadline from day one."

Real words

Goodput. Error budget. Deadline propagation. Anytime algorithm. Graceful degradation.

What I'd actually do

Cheapest and most reversible first. Notice that your teammate's idea survives — it just stops being the plan and becomes the safety net.

  1. Take the cap today. Customers are waiting right now. A crude limit beats an argument. Add "and no dish takes longer than twelve minutes" alongside it, because time is what people actually care about — attempts are just a stand-in for time.
  2. Find out where the time is going. Count the attempts per dish. Is it a few dishes going eight rounds, or every dish going four? Is the chef slow, or is the kitchen jammed? Those are three different problems with three different fixes, and one afternoon of measurement tells you which one you have.
  3. Check the boring stuff first. Is the chef starting each dish from scratch every time, instead of reusing what's already on the stove? That alone can explain the whole slowdown, and it's a settings change, not a redesign.
  4. Buy a thermometer. This is the actual fix. Give the loop something outside itself to check against — tests, a compiler, a schema, a real lookup, a small scoring model. Then it stops because it's done, not because it ran out of tries.
  5. Stop giving every dish the same budget. A salad doesn't need three attempts. A soufflé might need five. One rule for every order is wrong for nearly every order.
  6. Cook three at once instead of three in a row. Where you can, make several versions in parallel and pick the best. Same effort, one-third of the waiting — and mistakes don't get passed down a chain.
  7. Write down the promise. "Ninety-nine out of a hundred customers get their food in twelve minutes." Once that exists, everything above has a target instead of an opinion, and the cap becomes a fuse that almost never blows.
The line to close on

"I want the cap. I just don't want the cap to be the design. Once the loop can stop because it has evidence it's finished, gets a budget that matches how hard the job is, and can't spend everyone else's time buying its own quality — then the cap almost never fires. And that's how you know it's a safety net rather than a policy."

Explaining it to three different people

Same idea, three altitudes. Pick the one that matches who's in front of you.

To a friend with no tech background

"We built something that keeps redoing its work until it's happy. Problem is, it has no way to tell when it's happy — so it never stops on its own. My colleague wants to just cut it off after three tries. That makes things fast again, but only because it gives up on the hard stuff. What it actually needs is a way to check its own work against something real."

To a product manager

"Capping the retries trades a latency problem we can see for a quality problem we can't. The requests hitting the cap are our hardest ones, and we have no measurement that would tell us they got worse. I'd ship the cap today to stop the bleeding, then spend a week on a real stop signal — and I'd want us to agree on a latency promise so this doesn't happen again."

To the interviewer

"The cap truncates the tail rather than removing it, and it truncates on exactly the requests that needed the compute — so it converts a visible latency regression into an invisible quality one. What's broken is that there's no per-request stopping rule, the quality claim is an average rather than paired per-request, and the loop is an uncontrolled retry loop on a shared engine with no latency budget."

Say it out loud · about 75 seconds

The same answer as a script you can say from memory — four beats, no jargon at all. This is the version for the person in front of you who does not work on the system. Read it aloud, then say it again with the page covered.

  1. Situation

    We built something that keeps redoing its own work until it’s happy.

    It got slow — not for most people, but for the unluckiest one in a hundred.

  2. Mechanism

    It’s a chef who never tastes the food. He can cook it again and again, but he has no way to know when it’s right, so he only stops when someone takes the plate away.

    The slowness isn’t that any one attempt is slow. It’s that nobody knows how many attempts there will be.

  3. What I did

    My colleague wants a rule: three attempts, then serve. I’d take that today, because the kitchen is on fire.

    Then I’d spend the week giving him a way to taste — something real to check the dish against — and a timer sized to how hard the dish is.

  4. What it cost

    Three attempts makes us fast by giving up on the hard dishes. They go out worse, and nobody sends them back, because nobody knows what they were supposed to taste like.

Six traps

Each of these sounds reasonable and each one loses you the room.

Don't say
  • "We'll set the max to three and ship it." That's the exact answer the question is designed to catch.
  • "Just add more servers." Sometimes right, but it pays for the bug instead of fixing it — and the bill scales with the bug.
  • "p99 is only 1% of requests, who cares." If a user makes twenty calls, they hit that 1% about a fifth of the time. In a long AI agent run, the worst case is the normal experience.
  • "The model says its output improved, so it improved." A model grading its own work is the thing that's missing here, not the evidence that it's fine.
  • Telling a story with no measurement in it. "What's broken" needs evidence. Always name what you'd measure first.
  • Making your teammate look stupid. They're right that you need a limit. The question put a colleague in the room on purpose — it's testing whether you can disagree and still ship their change.

If you remember only three things

The whole sheet, compressed
  • A limit doesn't remove the slowness — it gives up early, on the hardest work, silently.
  • Nothing tastes the food. Without an outside check, the loop can never stop on its own, so it runs to the limit on every order — including the ones that were fine immediately.
  • There's one kitchen. The loop spends everyone's time, not just its own — which is why the slowest customers got three times slower while the typical one barely noticed.
