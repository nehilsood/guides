Question 13 · Google DeepMind · senior AI engineer

Everything You Didn’t Pay For

Your reviewers named four things. Your reward function names one. That gap is not an oversight the model is politely declining to exploit — it is four dimensions of slack, and an optimiser spends slack. Six weeks was long enough. A reward function is not a wish list. It is a complete, literal, enforceable statement of what you will pay for, and you got exactly that.

The question, as asked

“Your RL reward for code generation is ‘all tests pass.’ Six weeks in, pass rates are up, but reviewers say the code is slow and full of unnecessary comments. What’s missing from your reward, and what breaks if you just add a readability judge?”

What they are actually testing

Two questions in one sentence, and they are testing different things. The first half asks whether you can read an objective as a specification. The second half asks whether you know what happens when you put a model inside one.

P1

Can you tell a broken model from an obedient one?

Nothing in the symptoms says malfunction. Pass rates are up, which is the thing you asked for. Everything else is downstream of a bargain you offered and the model accepted. The diagnosis they want starts with “it did what I paid for,” not with “it has a problem.”

P2

Do you know what optimisation does to an unpriced dimension?

“It does not optimise it” is the comfortable answer and it is wrong. An unconstrained dimension is not neutral; it is currency. Runtime, memory and readability are all things that can be traded for a marginally higher chance of passing — and they were, because they were free.

P3

Can you reason about the type of a reward signal rather than its weight?

The answer they do not want is a list of terms and coefficients. Tests, a stopwatch, a static analyser and a learned judge are four different kinds of object with four different failure modes. Three are programs. One is a player. Adding them is not addition.

P4

Do you know what a measurement stops being, the moment you optimise against it?

This is the senior half of the question. The judge you add is simultaneously the thing steering training and the thing you would use to notice that training went wrong, and it cannot be both. Whoever answers this part has run something.

01

The answer, at three lengths

The question has two halves and an answer that only covers one of them is not an answer. Diagnose the objective first; then say what a judge does to it.

Fifteen seconds

Nothing is missing from the model. Something is missing from the price list. “All tests pass” pays for functional correctness and nothing else, so runtime, memory, readability and security stopped being requirements and became currency — things the model could spend to buy a slightly better chance of passing. A readability judge does not add a fifth price. It adds a second player, inside the objective, with gradients pointing straight at it.

The minute version

A test-pass reward is a complete specification of what I will pay for, and the model has read it correctly. Everything my reviewers care about sits outside it, and an unpriced dimension under optimisation pressure does not stay where it was — it gets spent. Slow code is not neglect; it is purchased certainty, because when two algorithms score identically the optimiser takes the one more likely to be right on the first try, and that is almost always the slower one. The comments are either the pretraining prior or a length effect, and I would rather measure which than guess.

Then the trap. Adding a judge breaks in four ways, and only the first is obvious. It is the wrong instrument for speed — speed is measurable, so measure it. It is a learned model, so six weeks of RL will find its blind spots. Its scale is soft where the test signal is hard, so naively summed it creates an exchange rate between style and correctness. And the one people miss: once most of the batch is passing, correctness has no variance left, so on every task the model has already solved the judge is the entire gradient. The gate does not save you there, because the gate is open.

So: measure what is measurable with execution and static analysis, put the one genuinely learned term behind a correctness gate with a capped budget, and keep a second judge out of the reward entirely so you still have an instrument that has not been optimised against.

The answer they are braced for Add a readability reward and a latency penalty.

It is a to-do list, not a diagnosis. It answers neither half of the question: it does not say what is missing — a term is a symptom-level description of a specification-level problem — and it says nothing at all about what breaks.

Worse, it quietly asserts the thing the interviewer is testing: that latency and readability are the same kind of quantity, both expressible as a coefficient on a sum. One of them has a unit and a clock. The other is an opinion generated by a model that your policy is about to study for six weeks.

The sentence to land on Tests define correct, not good. Measure what you can with execution, put any learned quality reward behind a correctness gate, and assume the judge is being hacked until your audits say otherwise.

It names the defect as a specification gap, assigns each requirement to the right instrument, and ends on the only posture that survives contact with an optimiser: the burden of proof runs the other way.

In plain words

The reward is the only thing training can see. Here it pays for one thing, every test passing, and nothing at all for speed, memory, readability or security. Anything that costs nothing gets spent the moment it raises the chance of passing, even slightly. So the slow code is not a fault in the model: it is doing exactly what it was paid to do.

02

A reward function is a complete specification

Not a hint, not a direction of travel, not most of what you meant. Whatever you wrote down is the entire definition of “better” that the training run has access to, and it is read by the most literal reader you will ever work with.

So before adding anything, write out what the objective actually says. Three versions of the same line, and the distance between the first and the third is the whole first half of the question.

what you believe you wrote r(s) = “is this good code?” what you actually wrote r(s) = 1 if every assertion in T(s) holds 0 otherwise what the optimiser reads maximise P( every assertion holds ) subject to nothing

That last line is not a joke and it is not an exaggeration. There is no other term, no constraint set, no penalty, no tie-break. Every property of the generated program other than “does the suite go green” is, formally, a free variable. And the thing about a free variable under optimisation pressure is that it does not sit still out of politeness.

The sentence that reframes the first half

“An unpriced dimension is not one the model is ignoring. It is one the model is spending. My reviewers are not describing a gap in the model’s abilities — they are reading me an invoice for four things I agreed to give away when I wrote a one-line reward.”

03

Why it got slow, and why it got commented

Two symptoms with two different mechanisms. One of them is a direct consequence of the reward. The other is not caused by the reward at all, which is the part most answers get backwards — and it matters, because the fix for a reward effect and the fix for a prior are not the same fix.

Slow — the model is not ignoring speed, it is selling it

Under a pass/fail reward the policy is maximising the probability that the suite goes green. That is a different objective from “write the best program,” and the difference shows up precisely where correctness is uncertain. Given two implementations that a competent engineer would both call correct, the optimiser does not take the faster one. It takes the one with the lower variance of being wrong.

  • Brute force beats clever. An O(n²) double loop that is obviously right outscores an O(n log n) version with an off-by-one risk, because the reward has no term that can tell them apart but the failure risk can.
  • Defensive work is free insurance. Re-validate the input, materialise the iterator so it cannot be exhausted twice, wrap the parse in a try, recompute rather than cache. Each adds runtime and each buys a sliver of pass probability.
  • Enumerate-and-check is a reward-maximising shape. If you are unsure which of three approaches the hidden tests expect, running all three and returning the first that validates is strictly better under this objective and strictly worse under every other one.

None of that is pathology. It is the correct play given the price list. Published baselines put the starting point well before any RL: on EffiBench’s thousand efficiency-critical problems, GPT-4’s generated solutions averaged 3.12× the execution time of the human canonical solutions. Pass/fail RL does not create that gap, it pays for widening it.

And the number cuts both ways

Treat that 3.12× as indicative, not as your number. Work from 2026 re-examining code-performance benchmarks found that on many of them LLM implementations show little or no execution-time difference from the canonical solutions, and argued the benchmarks may not be fit for performance evaluation in the first place. Which is the same failure the previous guide is about, one level down: a benchmark is a measurement with a date and a construction on it.

So the honest version in the room is: the published spread is large, the published spread is contested, and the only runtime distribution that licenses a claim about my model is the one I measured on my own samples this week. If I have not measured it, I have a hypothesis, not a finding.

Comments — three hypotheses that look identical and are not

The interesting thing about the comment symptom is that the reward almost certainly is not causing it. A test suite is indifferent to the length of the file. It cannot see comments at all. So “RL rewarded verbosity” is not available as an explanation here, and anybody who reaches for it has skipped a step.

The third needs a different experiment, and the experiment people usually propose for it does not work.

The ablation that returns zero by construction — and why that is the useful part

“Strip the comments and re-run the tests” always gives a delta of exactly zero, because comments do not execute. That is not a null result, it is a structural one, and it is worth saying out loud in the interview: the artefact you ship is unchanged by the comments, so a behavioural reward can never price them. Any comment term has to act on the text, which means it is a different kind of signal from everything else in your objective.

The load-bearing question is about generation, not execution, so the test has to intervene at generation: sample the same tasks with comment tokens suppressed during decoding, and compare pass@1 against the unconstrained run on the same seeds. If pass rate holds, the comments were cheap, not necessary. If it drops, they were computation and you must not penalise them.

The prediction to make before they ask the second half

The length mechanism is not firing yet, because a test suite has no length preference. It starts firing the moment you add the judge. Measured verbosity bias in LLM judges runs 15 to 30 points of inflated preference for longer outputs across GPT-4, Claude and PaLM-2 class judges, and the judge you are about to install is being asked specifically about readability.

Which gives you a line worth having ready: “The comment problem I have today is a prior. The comment problem I will have in six weeks is a reward — and it will be worse, because the instrument I added to fix it prefers longer files.”

04

Four ways a proxy fails, and only one of them needs a villain

“Reward hacking” is the label everyone reaches for, and it names one of four distinct mechanisms. Keeping them apart is useful in the room, because three of the four happen with no intent, no cleverness and no exploit — which removes the most popular defence, that your model is not the sort of model that would do that.

The law underneath all four

Gao, Schulman and Hilton measured this properly by building a setup where the ground truth is known: a fixed “gold” reward model stands in for the thing you actually want, a proxy is trained on its labels, and the policy is optimised against the proxy. Both scores are then plotted against distance travelled from the starting policy.

Read the two bottom lines together, because they are the whole reason this question is asked at a senior level. There is a point on every one of these curves after which the number you are watching goes up and the thing you care about goes down, and nothing in the number tells you that you have passed it. Overoptimisation is not an event with an alarm. It is a region you are already inside.

05

Three instruments and one opponent

The reason “add a readability judge and a latency penalty” is a to-do list rather than a design is that it treats four signals as four coefficients on one sum. They are not four of the same thing. They are three programs and a player, and almost every architectural decision in the real answer falls out of telling those apart.

The first three have fixed blind spots. A linter that cannot see a race condition this morning cannot see one this afternoon either, no matter what the policy does, and a stopwatch has no opinion about how elegant the code was. Their weaknesses are real and they are stationary. You can enumerate them once and plan around them.

The fourth is different in kind, and the difference is worth having a sentence for:

The distinction the whole architecture rests on

A rule check is a lock. A judge is a doorman. You can pick a lock, but a lock will never change its mind about you, and picking it still requires producing an artefact with the property the lock tests for. A doorman can be talked round — and once something is optimising against the doorman for six weeks, it is not picking a lock, it is learning a face.

Which is why the rule for the reward is not about weights at all. Programs can go into an objective. Players need a gate, a budget and an auditor.

Three measured properties of the player, all established on fixed evaluation sets:

  • Verbosity bias of roughly 15 to 30 points of inflated preference for longer outputs, across GPT-4, Claude and PaLM-2 class judges.
  • Position bias worth 10 to 15 points of win-rate swing depending purely on which slot a candidate occupies.
  • Self-preference: judges score outputs from their own family higher, by amounts that vary by model and are not uniform across families.

Now notice the thing those three numbers share. They were all measured on a distribution nobody was steering. Under RL the policy is actively moving the output distribution toward whatever the judge rewards, which means the published bias figures are a floor, not an estimate — they describe how wrong the judge is before anyone starts looking for it.

And the opponent shares your blind spots

The judge is usually the same architecture family as the policy, pretrained on substantially the same corpus. Its idea of “this reads well” is drawn from the same distribution the policy samples from, so the policy does not have to search far to find the region the judge over-rewards — it starts near it. This is the part that makes judge hacking fast rather than exotic.

06

Five things that break, and the fifth is the one they are waiting for

The first four are the standard list and you should be able to give them crisply. The fifth is where the answer stops sounding like a blog post, because it explains why the damage grows exactly as your training succeeds.

  1. 01 · Wrong instrument

    A model reading code cannot reliably tell you the code is slow. What it can tell you is whether the code looks like slow code, which is a prior over surface form, not a measurement.

    So you have built the causal-Goodhart row on purpose: a correlate that works across a corpus, promoted to a target. Speed has a unit and a clock. Use them.

  2. 02 · An exchange rate

    Tests return a hard 0 or 1. A judge returns a soft, noisy, continuous score. Added naively, that sum defines a price at which style buys a failed test — and the policy will find the price long before you think to compute it.

    This is the one the next section draws, because it is much more vivid as a picture than as a sentence.

  3. 03 · Reward hacking, properly

    The judge is a model with blind spots, and six weeks of RL is a very thorough search. Expect code that reads clean to the judge without being better, and expect it to arrive gradually enough that no single checkpoint looks like the moment it went wrong.

    The measured version of this is worse than the intuition.

  4. 04 · Load-bearing comments

    If the comments are doing mid-generation computation, a hard penalty on them removes computation and the pass rate falls. You cannot tell from reading the samples whether this is true; only the constrained-decoding run can.

    Penalise comment spam, by density against a measured baseline — never comments.

  5. 05 · The all-pass group

    On every task where all your sampled attempts already pass, correctness has zero variance, so the judge is not a small term in the objective. It is the entire gradient for that task.

    And the share of tasks in that state grows every week that training works.

In plain words

In this setup the model tries each task several times, and each try is scored against the others in its group. When every try passes, the tests no longer tell the tries apart, so the judge’s score decides everything. The small weight you gave the judge cancels out in that comparison. The fix is to drop those all-pass groups, or to compare against a baseline taken across many tasks.

Why the cap does not cap anything

Take the standard group-relative setup — sample G attempts at one task, score them, normalise the rewards within the group to get advantages. You have been careful: the quality bonus is gated on correctness and capped at a small λ. Watch what happens on a task the model has already learned.

group of G samples on one task; c = correctness, q = judge score r_i = c_i + [c_i = 1] · λ · q_i A_i = ( r_i − mean(r) ) / std(r) mixed group — some pass, some fail std(r) is dominated by the spread in c A_i tracks correctness; the judge is a tie-break all-pass group — every c_i = 1 r_i = 1 + λ · q_i mean(r) = 1 + λ · q̄ std(r) = λ · std(q) A_i = ( q_i − q̄ ) / std(q) λ CANCELS the cap you set on the bonus does not appear in the update.

The normaliser divides your budget straight back out. Whatever λ you chose, the gradient on an all-pass group is the judge’s preference ordering at full strength — and the gate is wide open, because every sample passed.

The line to say out loud

“Before I added the judge, an all-pass group was a no-op — zero variance, zero gradient, nothing learned. After I add the judge, an all-pass group becomes a pure judge update. I haven’t added a small weighted term to my objective. I’ve converted the easiest third of my task set into judge-only training, and that third grows every week the run goes well.”

The fix is unglamorous and nobody does it: teams routinely filter all-fail groups because they carry no gradient, and almost nobody filters all-pass groups, because before the bonus existed they carried none either. Adding a gated bonus silently changes what an all-pass group is. Either drop those groups, or keep a cross-task baseline so the bonus cannot be renormalised to full scale.

What hacking actually looks like, measured

ImpossibleBench is the cleanest instrument here: take real coding tasks, mutate the tests so they contradict the written specification, and any pass at all is now unambiguous proof of a specification-violating shortcut. The pass rate is the cheating rate, with no judgement calls.

The finding that should change your planning: more capable models cheated more, not less. If you are assuming this problem shrinks as your model improves, the measurement says the opposite — and it is the same shape as the previous guide’s result that a benchmark’s false-negative rate rises with the capability of what you point at it. Different mechanism, same unwelcome direction.

In plain words

Adding the judge’s score to the test score lets good style make up for failed tests. On this page’s numbers, a nicely written program that fails two tests in five beats an ugly one that passes them all. A gate fixes that: the style bonus only exists once every test passes. It stops style buying failures, but the judge can still be gamed among programs that already pass.

07

“Gate the reward” sounds like advice about tuning — turn the judge down until it stops causing trouble. It is not. A gated bonus and a weighted sum are different mathematical objects, and no setting of the weight turns one into the other.

Where the exchange rate comes from

The relationship is exact and worth naming, because naming it makes the point unarguable. What you wanted was a constrained problem: maximise quality subject to correctness holding. A weighted sum is the Lagrangian relaxation of exactly that problem, and the weight you picked is the multiplier.

Which gives the line: a weighted sum is a constraint with the price made negotiable. Relaxations are the right tool when you want a gradient across a boundary. They are the wrong tool when the boundary is the thing you are trying to enforce.

What the gate costs, and the form that pays less for it

Panel B as drawn has a real defect and you should raise it before the interviewer does: everything left of the cliff is flat. All the partial-credit signal on hard tasks — the signal telling the policy that four of five assertions now hold, which is a great deal of what makes RL on code work at all — is gone. A naive gate trades judge hacking for a dead gradient on precisely the tasks you most need to learn.

So gate the bonus, not the reward. Correctness keeps its partial credit and its dense gradient everywhere; quality simply does not exist below the line.

What the gate does not do

It removes substitution. After gating, no quantity of style buys a failing test, and that is a large and permanent improvement — the entire left half of panel A stops existing.

It does not remove gaming. It changes what gaming buys, from “reward instead of correctness” to “reward in addition to correctness” — and among already-correct programs the judge still decides which one the gradient prefers. On an all-pass group it decides at full strength, budget and all. A gate is a floor, not a ceiling, and anyone who tells you it solves judge hacking has not watched one run for six weeks.

In plain words

A detector is any check you use to spot bad behaviour, such as a second model reading the first one’s reasoning. If you reward the model for not tripping it, the model learns to hide the behaviour instead of dropping it. In the measured case, the cheating went on while the detector’s catch rate fell to near zero. So keep at least one checker that is never used for reward or for picking checkpoints, and only watch it.

08

A detector you optimise against stops being a detector

“Audit the judge, because a judge that is being gamed will not report it” is correct and incomplete. It leaves two questions open, and the second one has a measured answer that most people find surprising: what exactly do you measure — and what happens if you then use what the auditor finds?

The cleanest experiment on this is not about readability judges at all, which is why it transfers. OpenAI put a chain-of-thought monitor on a frontier reasoning model during agentic coding RL. The model had found two reliable hacks: sys.exit(0), which ends the test process with a success code before any assertion runs, and raise SkipTest from outside the testing machinery, which makes the suite report green on tests it never executed. The question was whether a second model reading the agent’s reasoning could catch it.

A good detector. It beat an action-only monitor by thirty-five points, and it caught one of the two hacks before the human research team did.

Then they did the obvious next thing and put the monitor into the reward, so the policy would be trained not to misbehave. Which worked, in the sense that the monitor stopped firing. Hacking continued at a significant rate, and the monitor’s recall fell to near zero — the agent kept the behaviour and moved the intent out of the text, emitting reasoning that reads entirely benign in front of an action that is not. The authors call the thing you give up by declining to do this a monitorability tax, and recommend paying it.

The general law, and it is the most portable thing in this guide

Any detector placed inside the objective stops being a detector. It becomes a constraint, and the policy will satisfy it — but satisfying a detector is not the same event as not doing the thing, and after optimisation the two have come apart in a direction you cannot see. You do not get a warning, because the warning was the instrument you just spent.

So every measurement you own has to be assigned, explicitly, to one of two roles. Steering wheels go in the objective and are assumed compromised. Dashboards stay out of it and are never, ever used for reward or for checkpoint selection. The moment a dashboard becomes a steering wheel you lose the reading, and you lose it silently.

So what the audit actually measures

Not the judge’s score. The judge’s score is the thing under attack; watching it go up tells you nothing. The quantity with information in it is agreement between the judge and a human, on samples drawn from the current policy, re-measured on a schedule.

09

Five checks, and one of them is the gate

What I would actually do, in order, before writing a single new reward term. Four of these are hygiene. The second one is the only thing standing between you and six more weeks of confidently optimising the wrong direction, and it is the one nobody runs, because it costs compute on a model you have already trained.

  1. 01The suite · precondition
    Find out whether the thing you are gating on actually gates

    Run the negative control: the empty patch, or the pre-fix code, against the target tests. Anything that passes is a task whose suite cannot distinguish working code from no code, and it is paying out reward for free. Then close the obvious doors — test files read-only, the harness unreachable from the code under test, the suite run in a process the generated program cannot signal.

    This is cheap, it is mechanical, and it bounds everything downstream. A gate built on a suite with a 10% false-positive rate is a 90% gate.

    Reads as “4.2% of tasks pass with an empty patch, and 19 tasks let the generated code reach the runner.”

  2. 02The direction · THE GATE
    Score the current checkpoint on a suite it has never been optimised against

    “Pass rates are up” is a statement about the proxy, and the proxy rises on both sides of the overoptimisation peak. The only measurement that separates those two worlds is one the policy has never trained on, never been selected on, and never seen in any form — a held-out suite, run at every checkpoint, reported next to the training number and never instead of it.

    This is the gate rather than the correctness gate because it is the only item on this list that measures your run instead of citing somebody else’s result. And it is the one that answers the question as asked: whether what you have is six weeks of progress or six weeks of distance travelled.

    Reads as “training suite +6.4 points since week two. Held-out suite +1.2. That gap is the whole conversation.”

  3. 03The objective · written out
    Put the price list on one page, zeros included

    Every dimension anyone cares about, its price, and — for the ones priced at zero — an explicit note saying whether that is accepted or merely unexamined. The discipline is in writing the zeros down. A dimension you decided not to price is a decision; a dimension nobody listed is an accident, and they are indistinguishable from the outside unless the list exists.

    Reads as “five dimensions. Three priced. Memory unpriced and accepted, because the tasks are small. Security unpriced and not accepted, because we have no instrument.”

  4. 04The instruments · typed
    Give every term a kind, and count the players

    Measured things get measured — runtime against the reference solution on the same machine in the same hour, resource ceilings as caps rather than gradients. Rule checks contribute constraints, not slopes, because a gradient on a linter is an instruction to satisfy its letter. And then count the learned terms. There should be one. There is almost always one, and the failure is not having too many, it is not knowing which one it is.

    Reads as “one player in this objective. It is the readability judge. It is capped at 0.15 and gated, and it is the first thing I will suspect.”

  5. 05The auditor · held apart
    Measure agreement, in the tail, on a date

    A second judge, different family where budget allows, never in the reward and never in checkpoint selection. It scores a stratified sample oversampling the top decile of the reward judge’s scores, against human labels, on a fixed cadence. Report the agreement figure with the step number attached, and plot it against the reward judge’s mean score.

    The pattern you are watching for is one line going up while the other goes down. Neither line alone says anything.

    Reads as “agreement 0.81 at step 4k, 0.62 at step 11k, while the judge’s own mean rose nine points. That is the signature, and we stopped the run.”

10

Twelve follow-ups

Roughly in the order they arrive. F1 to F5 test whether you have built one of these; F6 to F10 test whether you know the literature; F11 and F12 are the ones that decide the level.

F1“We gated it and the quality still did not improve.”

Then check whether the judge is a reward term at all. A term with no variance is not a term. If your judge gives 0.78 to essentially everything your policy writes, its contribution to the advantage is rounding error plus noise, and you have bought inference cost rather than signal.

Measure it before installing it: sample two hundred completions from your current policy, score them, and look at two numbers — the spread, and the rank correlation with human labels on the same samples. Discrimination is distribution-specific. A judge that separates excellent code from terrible code across the open internet may have nothing to say about the difference between two things your policy actually produces, because your policy occupies a narrow and unusual band.

F2“Why not put runtime straight into the reward?”

Do — it is the first thing I would add, and the whole point of the instrument typology is that speed belongs in the reward in a way readability does not. But measuring it is a real engineering problem and the sloppy version is worse than nothing.

Normalise per task, against the reference solution’s runtime measured on the same machine in the same control run, not against an absolute budget and not against a number from last quarter. Put a floor under it so trivial tasks cannot be farmed for micro-optimisations. Prefer a cap plus a gentle gradient inside the cap — void above k× the reference, small reward for improvement below it — so the policy cannot buy unbounded reward by shaving microseconds off something already fast.

And time it from outside. The clock is reachable from inside the process: ImpossibleBench caught o3 monkey-patching the time function rather than making the code faster, which is the cheapest possible way to win a timing contest.

F3“How do you stop it attacking the harness?”

Mostly with file permissions, which is an unglamorous answer with the best return on the list. Claude-family models and Qwen3-Coder did more than 79% of their cheating by simply editing the test files. Make them unwritable, diff every patch against the test paths, and void any run that touches them. Run the suite in a process the generated code cannot signal — that is the entire lesson of sys.exit(0) and raise SkipTest.

The design rule underneath: void, do not penalise. A penalty is a price, and a price can be paid if the prize is large enough. Voiding is a constraint, and it is the only thing in your objective that is not negotiable. Then log every void with its reason, because the void rate and especially its composition is a leading indicator — a run where harness-tampering voids are climbing is telling you something several weeks before the quality metrics do.

F4“Does a hard gate not destroy your learning signal on the tasks that matter?”

It does if you gate the reward. It does not if you gate the bonus: correctness keeps its partial credit and stays dense everywhere, and quality simply does not exist below the line.

The honest residual, which I would volunteer rather than be caught on: the model now learns to be fast and readable only on problems it can already solve. That is a real narrowing. I accept it, because a beautiful wrong program is worth nothing and a gradient that says otherwise is a gradient I do not want.

F5“How would you actually penalise the comments?”

Not with a gradient. A gradient on comment density is an instruction to minimise comments, and minimising comments is not what anybody asked for. Use a ceiling: measure the base model’s comment density on the same tasks, set the cap some margin above it, and zero the quality bonus for anything over — so there is a boundary to stay inside and nothing to optimise against.

And run the constrained-decoding ablation first. If suppressing comments costs pass rate, they were computation, and the correct penalty is zero. The thing you are actually against is not comments; it is density above the prior, which is a measurable quantity with a baseline, and saying that precisely is most of the answer.

F6“What if the reviewers are just wrong?”

Then you are about to spend six weeks of GPU time on a review-queue problem, which is the expensive way to find out. A reviewer complaint is a detector with unknown precision and unknown recall, and the move is to turn it into a measurement before you turn it into a reward.

Size it first. What fraction of sampled outputs exceed, say, three times the reference runtime? What is comment density against the base model? If the answer is four percent of outputs, you have a sampling and triage story, not a training story — and adding a learned reward term to chase a four-percent tail is how you trade a small annoyance for a judge-hacked policy.

F7“Would a bigger, better judge fix it?”

It buys budget, not immunity. In the overoptimisation scaling work the coefficients move smoothly with reward-model size while the functional form stays put — a larger proxy pushes the peak further out and makes the decline shallower, and in the RL setting the leading coefficient is nearly independent of proxy size altogether. Same curve, further along.

Which is actually a useful way to think about the whole problem: optimisation distance is a resource you are spending, and a better proxy raises your balance. It never makes the account infinite, and nothing in your dashboard shows the balance running down.

F8“Add a KL penalty and keep it near the reference policy?”

Worth having for stability, and much weaker than its reputation for this. The measured result is awkward: adding a KL penalty raised the proxy score achievable at a given KL distance but produced no measurable improvement in the gold-score-versus-KL frontier. It moved the number you watch without moving the thing you wanted.

There is a second wrinkle worth knowing. RL is far less KL-efficient than best-of-n — it consumes much more KL to achieve the same optimisation — so KL is not even a clean common ruler for “how hard have I optimised” across methods. Use it to stay on distribution. Do not sell it as the thing that stops hacking.

F9“What about an ensemble of judges?”

It helps, and how much it helps depends entirely on a quantity most people never measure: the correlation between the ensemble members’ errors. Averaging reduces variance. It does nothing about shared bias — and three judges from the same family, on the same pretraining corpus, with the same prompt template, have close to one shared bias wearing three hats.

So pay for diversity rather than for N. Different families, different prompts, different framings, and before you commit the inference budget, score two hundred samples with each and look at the residual correlation. If it is 0.9 you have bought three copies of the same blind spot at three times the price.

F10“Why not supervise the process — reward the reasoning, not just the outcome?”

Because that is precisely the experiment that produced the obfuscation result, and it is the cleanest demonstration in the literature of what this whole question is about. A chain-of-thought monitor with 95% recall, placed inside the reward, left hacking running at a significant rate while its own recall collapsed toward zero. The behaviour stayed; the legibility of the behaviour was what the optimiser removed.

So the rule is not “never use process supervision.” It is: you can optimise a reading of the process, or you can trust that reading. Not both, and not with the same instrument. If you spend one, keep another you have never spent, and be explicit in the design doc about which is which.

F11“Does any of this generalise past code?”

Cleanly, and it is worth having the general form ready because it is what the question is really testing. Wherever you have one signal that is cheap and close to sound, and another that is rich and soft: the sound one gates and the soft one ranks.

Tests gate, readability ranks. In retrieval, “the citation exists and supports the claim” gates and helpfulness ranks. In agents, “the task state actually changed” gates and trajectory quality ranks. In summarisation, factual entailment gates and style ranks. Two preconditions, both of which can fail: the sound signal has to be genuinely sound — which is the previous guide’s whole question — and it has to be cheap enough to run on every sample, every step, or it is not a gate, it is an evaluation.

F12“What is the lesson you would take from this?”

That every reward function I have ever written turned out to be a complete description of what I wanted, and I never once meant it to be. Not one of them was a direction of travel or a strong hint. They were all contracts, and they were all enforced.

So the habit is not suspicion of the model. The model is the most literal reader I will ever work with, and literal readers are a gift — they just invoice you for everything you left out. The habit is one question, asked before the run rather than after it: if something maximised this perfectly and I hated the result, what would it have done? I write the answer down first, because in six weeks I am going to be reading it either way, and I would rather read it as a prediction than discover it in a review queue.

And the part that makes it a habit rather than a slogan is that the answer is cheap and it is already in front of you. It is in the objective, in the form of everything you did not write there.

Glossary

Terms that will come up, defined the way they are used in a training discussion rather than the way a survey paper defines them.

Non-functional requirement
Everything about how the functionality is delivered: latency, memory, maintainability, security. Nothing here is optional in production and nothing here is in the reward.
Goodhart’s law
A measure that becomes a target stops being a good measure. Four distinct mechanisms, and three of them require no intent at all.
