The filter that only learned from wins

Original: AI Agent Engineering Interview #19 · The Rejection Sampling Trap

1The question

“Your lead wants to drop reinforcement learning altogether. Instead: sample many answers from the model, keep the ones whose tests pass, fine-tune on those, and go round again. When would that be enough to ship, and at what point does it stall in a way that policy-gradient RL doesn't?” Senior ML Engineer interview, Google DeepMind

RL, reward, policy and rollout are from guide 27; fine-tuning is from guide 25.

2The trap

Trap A: “Keeping the passes is RL in all but name, so it's just as good.”

Trap B: “Without real RL the model can't improve; insist on it.”

Both skip the detail that matters: how each method weighs an attempt, and what it does with failures.

3Your 30-second answer

“That loop is rejection-sampling fine-tuning, and it's often enough. ReST-EM used it to beat fine-tuning on human data for maths and code. I'd ship it when the tests are a reliable reward and the model already passes a fair share of tasks, so training mostly sharpens what it can do.

The stall: it learns only from wins, each counted the same. Tasks that pass eight times in eight flood the data, a rare pass on a hard task counts once, and failures teach nothing. GRPO, a policy-gradient method, scores each attempt against its group's average, so the rare pass counts most, failures are pushed down, and solved tasks drop out. DeepSeekMath found that beat the filter. But neither learns where the model never passes: Yue et al. found RL mostly sharpens what the base model could already produce.”

4The idea in plain words

Two stand-up comics record every show. The first keeps only the jokes that got a laugh and rehearses those. His notebook fills with the safe joke that always works; the new joke that killed once, on a tough night, is one line among many.

The second also notes how each joke did against that night's crowd. A laugh from a crowd that rarely laughs counts for a lot. The safe joke teaches him nothing new. A joke that died gets marked down, so he stops reaching for it.

Neither comic learns anything from a joke he has never landed.

The first comic is rejection sampling: draw many attempts, reject the failures, fine-tune on the rest. The second is policy gradient, the RL family that raises or lowers each attempt's probability by its advantage: how much better or worse it scored than expected. The expected score is the baseline.

Rejection sampling: pass counts 1, fail counts 0
Sample 8 attempts→Keep the passes→Fine-tune on them↺
Policy gradient: each attempt weighed against the group
Sample 8 attempts→Score minus group average→Push up above, down below↺
Think about it. One task passes 8 times out of 8, another 1 time out of 8. Under rejection sampling, which task supplies more training examples, and which one did the model most need to learn from?

5How it works: when the filter is enough, and where it stalls

  1. The filter works. STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) fine-tuned on reasoning that reached correct answers, round after round. ReST-EM (Singh et al., TMLR 2024) ran the same loop with right-or-wrong checks on the MATH and APPS benchmarks (maths and code) and significantly outperformed fine-tuning on human data alone.
  2. Both are one formula with different weights. DeepSeekMath (Shao et al., 2024) writes both methods as one training update. Rejection-sampling fine-tuning gives each answer weight 1 if correct, 0 if not. Its GRPO, a policy-gradient method, uses the reward minus the group's mean, divided by the spread. The filter “does not penalize incorrect responses and uniformly reinforces all responses with correct answers”.
  3. Fresh samples and graded weights each helped. On a small model with 1.3 billion weights, sampling from the current model each step “significantly outperforms” sampling once from the starting model, and GRPO beat that fresh-sample filter: “GRPO surpasses online RFT”. The test isn't perfectly clean: GRPO there scored with a trained reward model, online RFT with a rule. My reading: your lead's loop sits between the two, since it resamples once per round.
  4. Easy tasks flood the filter. ReST-EM capped kept solutions at 10 per problem because otherwise “we will have a lot more correct solutions for the easier problems.” Its gains also faded after a couple of rounds.
  5. The shared ceiling. My reasoning from the formula: if all eight attempts fail, every advantage is zero, so policy gradient learns nothing there either. Yue et al. (NeurIPS 2025) found RL-trained models beat their base model at one attempt, but base models score higher when allowed many attempts; the abilities are “bounded by the base model”. DeepSeekMath saw something similar: RL didn't raise the chance that at least one of many tries is right. So the question's premise, that policy gradient has no such ceiling, outruns the evidence: its edge is weighting, not reach.

6Follow-ups they'll ask

Isn't filtering by reward just RL anyway?

Half true. DeepSeekMath writes both as the same gradient: the filter weights each answer 1 if correct and 0 if wrong, while GRPO subtracts the group's average score (a baseline), so failures get pushed down and solved tasks stop counting. So the filter is policy gradient with the crudest weights and no baseline.

How would you get signal on tasks the model never passes?

Neither method can alone: a task that always fails gives zero weight. STaR showed the model the correct answer and had it write the reasoning (“rationalization”). Other options: easier stepping-stone tasks, more attempts on hard ones, or distillation (training on a stronger model's answers), which Yue et al. say “can introduce new reasoning patterns”.

How many rounds of the loop would you run?

Until a held-out set stops improving, which may be soon. ReST-EM found that more than a couple of rounds gave diminishing gains, and on the APPS coding benchmark the second round got worse, which the authors suspect was overfitting to the small set of training problems.

7Check yourself

In DeepSeekMath's unified view, how do rejection-sampling fine-tuning and GRPO weight each sampled answer?

Rejection-sampling fine-tuning gives weight 1 to correct answers and 0 to wrong ones. GRPO uses the answer's reward minus the group's mean, divided by the group's spread, so wrong answers get pushed down and rare successes count more.

Why does plain rejection sampling over-weight easy problems, and how did ReST-EM guard against it?

An easy problem yields many passing samples, a hard one few, so easy problems fill the training set. ReST-EM capped the kept solutions at 10 per problem.

What did Yue et al. (NeurIPS 2025) find when comparing RL-trained models with their base models?

RL-trained models did better with one attempt, but base models scored higher when allowed many attempts, so the authors conclude the reasoning abilities are “bounded by the base model”.

8Words to know

Policy gradient
RL that raises or lowers each attempt's probability by its advantage.
GRPO
A policy-gradient method that uses the group's average score as the baseline.
