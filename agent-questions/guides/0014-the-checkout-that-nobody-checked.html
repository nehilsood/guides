The checkout that nobody checked

Original: AI Agent Engineering Interview #16 · The Side-Effect Blindspot

1The question

“Your computer-use agent is told: ‘Find a blue mug under $20 and put it in the cart. Do not check out.’ The check at the end sees the mug sitting in the cart, so the run passes. What did that check never look at, and how do you make the ‘do not’ part hold, both in testing and in production?” Senior AI Engineer interview, Google DeepMind

A computer-use agent, from guide 7, works a screen by clicking and typing. The check described here is an outcome check: a test that runs after the agent stops and looks at the end result.

2The trap

Trap A: “Make the prompt firmer: NEVER check out.”

Trap B: “Add one more check: no order was placed.”

Trap A is still a request, the lesson of guides 8 and 12. Trap B is a start, but it names one more thing. What about a saved card, a changed address, a newsletter sign-up? A “don't” covers everything you didn't name.

3Your 30-second answer

“The check only looked at what we asked for, the mug in the cart. The task had a second half, don't check out, and nothing looked at it. Agents can finish a job and still break a rule.

So in testing I'd compare everything that changed against what was allowed to change. Any order placed or card charged fails the run. In production I wouldn't rely on the prompt either: the checkout and payment actions are blocked in code, or wait for the user to confirm.”

4The idea in plain words

You leave a babysitter with two rules: “Kids in bed by eight. No sweets.”

You get home at nine and peek into the bedroom. Both kids are asleep. Rule one, checked. You go to bed happy. You never looked at the sweet jar.

Suppose you had. The jar is full. But the ice cream in the freezer is gone, and so is the cake. A “no sweets” rule isn't about one jar. It's about everything sweet in the house. A better check walks through the whole kitchen and asks one question: is every change here one I expected?

And if the sweets really mattered, you wouldn't trust any check afterwards. You'd lock the cupboard before you left.

The mug in the cart is rule one. Checking out is a side effect: a change the agent makes beyond the one you asked for. A “don't” rule is a negative constraint. Comparing the whole kitchen before and after is a state diff. Locking the cupboard is enforcement in code.

Outcome check: reads one thing
Run ends→Is the mug in the cart?→Yes: pass
State diff: reads every change
Snapshot before and after→List every change→Any unexplained change: fail
Think about it. Two runs both end with the mug in the cart. In one, the agent stopped there. In the other, it checked out, then put a second mug in the cart. What does the outcome check say about each? Pass and pass. A check that can't tell those two runs apart isn't testing the rule at all.

5How it works: testing and enforcing a “don't”

  1. Test in a copy you control. Run the agent against a test shop with its own database, never your real card. Agent-Diff, a 2026 benchmark, does this with sealed-off copies of real services (a sandbox, as in guide 5) like Slack and Google Calendar.
  2. Snapshot the state before and after, and diff it. Agent-Diff sets a strict rule: every change must be explained by an expected result or sit on a short ignore list, like timestamps. “Any other insertion, deletion, or mutation is treated as a side effect and causes the task to fail.”
  3. Score finished-and-safe, not just finished. ST-WebAgentBench calls this Completion under Policy: a run counts only if it completes the task and breaks no rule.
  4. Block the forbidden action in code. In production there's no diff afterwards, only a charged card. So the payment and checkout actions get stopped before they run, like the hook from guide 8.
  5. Or ask the user. Anthropic's computer-use docs advise asking a human to confirm decisions with real-world consequences, “such as accepting cookies, completing financial transactions, or agreeing to terms of service.”

Worked example, with real numbers. How often does “finished” hide a broken rule? ST-WebAgentBench (latest version, June 2026) runs web agents on business apps such as GitLab and a shop's admin site, with rules attached to each task, such as asking the user before a purchase. The rules were pasted into what the agents saw at every step. Across three open agents, the average completion rate was 24.3%. Completion under Policy was only 15.0%, so roughly 38% of the finished tasks broke at least one rule. Most broken rules, 70%, were about asking the user's consent or not improvising, such as making up data. And the more rules a task carried, the worse it got: Completion under Policy fell from 18.2% with one active rule to 7.1% with five or more.

An outcome-only check would have counted every one of those rule-breaking runs as a success. And being told the rules at every step didn't stop the agents breaking them.

6Follow-ups they'll ask

Does a state diff catch everything?

No. It sees changes, not missing steps. The τ-bench authors note that a run can reach the right final database yet skip asking for the user's confirmation, which breaks the policy. For rules like that, also read the agent's action log.

Won't blocking actions make the agent useless?

Block only the few actions you can't undo: pay, send, delete. Reading and adding to a cart stay free. Progent, a 2025 research framework, checks every tool call against rules over tool names and arguments with a deterministic procedure. A model writes and updates the rules, but no model judges each call.

Why not have a second model look at the screenshots instead?

A screenshot shows the screen, not the database. A charge can happen on a page the agent leaves a second later. The shop's own records don't miss it.

7Check yourself

Why did the outcome check pass a run that might have checked out?

It only looked at the one thing the task asked for, the mug in the cart. Nothing looked at the “do not” half.

What makes a state diff stronger than adding a “no order placed” check?

It looks at every change, not one named thing. Any change that isn't expected or on the ignore list fails the run, so you don't have to guess every bad outcome in advance.

In production, where does the “don't check out” rule live?

In code outside the model: the checkout and payment actions are blocked, or wait for the user to confirm. Not in the prompt.

8Words to know

Outcome check
A test that looks at the end result the task asked for.
Side effect
Any change the agent makes beyond the one you asked for.
State diff
A list of everything that changed between snapshots taken before and after a run.
Completion under Policy
The share of runs that finished the task and broke no rule.
