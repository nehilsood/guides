The org chart that merged too often

Original: AI Agent Engineering Interview #30 · The Multi-Agent Org Chart

1The question

“Our multi-agent system copies a company org chart: workers write drafts, middle managers merge them, and one manager presents the result. A study suggests flipping it: one agent sets the direction, branches spread out from it, and a single extra agent combines all the end results. Why would the upside-down tree do better?” Senior AI Engineer interview, Google DeepMind

The topology (who passes work to whom) was met in guide 21; how many agents to use, in guide 22. Here only the direction of the arrows changes.

2The trap

Trap A: “The flipped version wins on headcount.” Both charts can use the very same agents.

Trap B: “Org charts work for companies, so they'll work for agents.” Agents pass on text, and each merge can drop some.

The real question is where the work gets squeezed, and how often.

3Your 30-second answer

“Each merge squeezes several drafts into one, and whatever it drops is gone for everyone above. In the org chart, merges stack: the manager only sees summaries of summaries. In the upside-down tree, branches spread out freely and there's one merge at the end, by a synthesiser that reads every leaf.

The evidence is real but thin. The MacNet study reversed the arrows on two shapes and the spreading versions scored higher, about 63 against 59 and 60 against 58 out of 100, with GPT-3.5. Mixture-of-Agents shows layered merging can work when each merger sees every output below it. So I'd merge as few times as I can, give the merge to my best model, and keep the raw outputs where the synthesiser can read them.”

4The idea in plain words

Twelve friends come back from a trip with 50 photos each, and you want a 20-photo album.

The org chart way: friends in groups of four each pick their group's best ten. Then a coordinator picks 20 from those 30. The best photo of the trip was a blurry-looking shot that only its taker knew was special. Her group picker skipped it, and the coordinator never saw it.

The upside-down way: one friend plans the shots everyone should try for, each friend shoots freely, and one editor goes through all 600 at once. The odd shot has one chance to be noticed, and the person deciding sees it next to everything else.

Each picking-down step is a merge. A node that merges several inputs into one is convergent; a node that hands its work to several others is divergent.

Org chart: merges stack up
8 workers draft→2 managers merge 4 each→Top merges 2
Upside-down: spread out, merge once
Root sets the direction→Branches develop it→One synthesiser reads every leaf
Think about it. In the org chart, the presenting manager is asked why an option was rejected. Can she answer? Who could, and is their reasoning still anywhere she can read?

5How it works: what the evidence says, and doesn't

  1. The study behind the question. In MacNet (Qian et al., ICLR 2025), only each agent's finished work moves on; its reasoning stays behind. The authors reversed the edges of two shapes and found that divergent shapes, “having more child nodes than parent nodes, substantially outperform their convergent counterparts”. Where several branches end, it adds a final node to merge them: the extra combiner in the question.
  2. Their reason. “Aggregating multiple artifacts at a convergent node is more challenging”, so they recommend shapes “that maximize divergence while minimizing convergence”. They also found wide shapes beat deep ones of the same density, because deep shapes can lose track of distant agents.
  3. Our reasoning on why stacking hurts. A merger can only pass on what it kept. Every layer above sees less of the original work, so a later merger can't recover what an earlier one dropped. It's the handoff loss from guide 20, repeated at every level. Anthropic's research system writes subagent output to a filesystem “to minimize the ‘game of telephone’”.
  4. What the evidence doesn't show. MacNet's comparison is one model (GPT-3.5) and two shapes. Its divergent scores match its main table, which used networks of about four nodes, so by my reading these are small networks. The paper doesn't test whether this gap could be chance. And MacNet itself found that on open-ended tasks convergent shapes revise more and write longer answers, that no shape suits every task, and that its random and fully connected shapes beat the star in its main table (0.6522 and 0.6316 against 0.6267).
  5. The counterweight. Mixture-of-Agents (Wang et al., 2024) merges in layers, and its open-model version reached a 65.1% win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0, against GPT-4o's 57.5%. (AlpacaEval 2.0 has a judge model compare each answer with a reference model's, adjusting for length; the score is how often the answer wins.) But each of its agents “takes all the outputs from agents in the previous layer”: every merger sees everything below it, not one slice. By my reading, the loss comes from narrow, stacked merges, not from merging itself.

Worked example, with real numbers. MacNet's quality scores (averaged over four benchmarks, 0 to 1) for each shape and its reverse:

And an illustration with made-up numbers. One worker's draft holds the key idea, and each merge keeps a minority idea 70% of the time. In the org chart, it must survive two merges: 0.7 × 0.7 = 49%. In the upside-down tree, one merge: 70%. It's the same compounding as the step-by-step success rates in guide 1.

6Follow-ups they'll ask

If merging loses things, why not skip it and have a judge pick the best leaf?

Because combining can add value. In Mixture-of-Agents (Wang et al., 2024), the aggregator beat an LLM ranker that picked one proposed answer, which suggests it was combining, not just selecting. So merge once, with every leaf in view.

What if there are too many leaves for one synthesiser to read?

Then some layering is unavoidable. Keep it wide rather than deep, as MacNet found wide shapes beat deep ones of the same density. Store each leaf's full output in files the synthesiser can open, as Anthropic's research system does to avoid a game of telephone. My suggestion: ask middle layers to list disagreements instead of rewriting.

Who should do the one merge?

Your strongest synthesiser, and test for it: by my reading, proposing and combining are different skills. In Mixture-of-Agents, WizardLM 8x22B reached a 63.8% AlpacaEval 2.0 win rate as a proposer but 52.9% as the aggregator.

7Check yourself

In the MacNet study, what makes a topology divergent or convergent, and which did better?

Divergent nodes have more children than parents, so work spreads out; convergent nodes merge several inputs. With GPT-3.5, divergent versions of a star and a tree beat their reversed, convergent versions: 0.6267 against 0.5902, and 0.6015 against 0.5821.

Why doesn't Mixture-of-Agents' success with layers of merging contradict the upside-down tree?

Each Mixture-of-Agents agent reads every output from the previous layer, so no merger works from a slice. The org chart's problem is narrow merges stacked on each other, where the top sees only summaries.

Each merge keeps a minority idea 80% of the time. How likely is it to survive three stacked merges, against one?

About 51% (0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 = 0.512) against 80%, assuming each merge drops it independently.

8Words to know

Merge (aggregation)
One agent combining several inputs into one output.
Convergent node
A node with more inputs than outputs: it merges.
Divergent node
A node with more outputs than inputs: it hands work to several others.
Synthesiser
The agent that does the final merge.
